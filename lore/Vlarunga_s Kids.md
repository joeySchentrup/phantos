I have never understood why everyone thinks creation ought to be gentle.

Rokesh takes his time. Of course he does. He will stare at a mountain for three hundred years before deciding it needs another ridge. Then he will spend another hundred discussing whether the ridge ought to face east or west. And once he has finally shaped the thing, he sits beside it as though he expects the mountain to thank him.

Yvander is scarcely better. She lays every current with care, measures every depth, whispers to every little creature she sets swimming beneath the waves.

Ouro’ras? Do not get me started on Ouro’ras. He makes something and immediately begins worrying about what it will become.

I make something and wait to find out. That is much more interesting.

When Kallisto gave us Phantos, the world was still young enough that you could feel it shifting beneath your claws. The mountains had not quite decided where they wanted to stand. The oceans were filling their basins. Stone still remembered being molten. It was beautiful, especially underground.

Oh, the others loved the surface: green valleys, shining seas, crystal skies, you know, pretty things. But beneath all that? That was where the world was alive. You could hear it: stone cracking, metal groaning, magma moving through the earth like blood through veins, pressure, heat, violence, change, I loved it.

So when the others began making their children, I knew exactly where mine belonged, not in some pleasant meadow, not beneath some kindly sun, not somewhere safe. Safe things do not grow strong. I went down, far down, past the roots of mountains, past the buried rivers, past caverns where even Golestandt's darkness had never bothered to wander, down where the rock glowed, down where the air itself could kill, down into the burning heart of the world, and there I began.

I drew fire from the veins of Phantos and rolled it between my claws.The first spirits were easy, for fire likes being alive, Have you ever noticed that? Give flame enough room and it dances; give it wood and it eats; give it wind and it runs; Fire wants.

So I gave that wanting thought: I breathed into the flames, and they laughed. That was the first sound my children ever made, laughter, good children. They danced across the magma, tumbling over one another, changing shape every few moments because none of them had yet learned that bodies were supposed to stay put.

I watched them for a little while. One jumped into a lavafall. Another tried to eat a piece of sulfur. A third discovered that it could make itself explode and immediately did so seven times. I was very proud, then I left them alone.

When I came back a few centuries later, they had learned. Some had become great spirits of flame, racing through the veins beneath the earth; others had learned to hide inside furnaces and volcanoes. A few had even learned how to creep upward and whisper to mortals through campfires, clever little things.

The others were horrified: “You abandoned them,” Ouro’ras told me.

“I checked on them.”

“After three hundred years!”

“Yes?”

He stared at me.

I stared at him. I never understood what the problem was. They were alive, weren't they? More importantly, they were interesting, so I made more.

The infernals came next. Those took some thought. Fire alone burns quickly. I wanted something that could endure, so I dug deeper. I found iron, copper, sulfur, and stone. Things that had spent ages crushed beneath the weight of the world. I took them and cast them into the deepest furnaces I could find, then I gave them souls. That part was important, for bodies can be forged, but souls must be given room.

When the first of them awoke, the heat was so great that the stone beneath his feet melted. He looked at me, then at the fire, then at his hands, then he punched the cavern wall. The cavern wall broke and he laughed. I laughed, Excellent.

“You are mine,” I told him.

He looked at me for a moment, “Why?”

Good question, “Because I made you.”

He thought about that, then pointed upward, “What is there?”

“The world.”

“What is it like?”

I grinned, “Go find out.”

So he did. That was the beginning: Some of my children remained beneath the earth, ome climbed upward, some built homes beside volcanoes, some dug kingdoms beneath mountains, some found the mortal peoples and traded with them, some fought them, some loved them, some hated them. Good. That was their choice.

People have always misunderstood that about me. They think because I do not command my children, I do not care about them. Nonsense. I care about them enough not to live their lives for them. What sort of mother builds a child and then spends eternity telling it where to walk? I made them strong enough to choose poorly. That is important.

Eventually, I began experimenting, that is how the Tieflings happened. No, I did not plan every horn. Who plans horns? Rokesh, probably; he would spend fifty years choosing the curvature. Mine simply grew them, some curled backward, some rose straight upward. One child had three. I thought that was delightful. I made them deeper than the spirits and softer than the infernals. Not soft in the weak sense, but soft in the way heated metal is soft: capable of being shaped, capable of being scarred, capable of becoming something their maker never imagined.

I placed them in the chambers beneath the earth where fire met stone. It was not comfortable, that was intentional. They learned quickly that heat must be respected. They learned to find water. They learned which stones cracked before an eruption. They learned to work metal because metal was everywhere. They learned cooperation because the caverns did not care whether they lived. They learned courage because sometimes the only path forward went through fire. Most importantly, they learned that no one was coming to save them, well, usually. I was watching, sometimes. Do not give me that look. There is a difference between allowing your child to struggle and allowing your child to die because a mountain decided to fall on them. I have limits.

Once, a whole settlement became trapped when a magma chamber shifted beneath them. They tried everything. They dug. They reinforced the tunnels. They prayed. For three days, I watched. On the fourth, the ceiling began to collapse, so I stepped in, just a little: one breath, one tiny breath. I redirected the magma twenty miles east and accidentally created a volcano. Rokesh complained about that volcano for nearly a thousand years. Apparently I had “ruined a perfectly stable geological formation.” It was a boring geological formation. The volcano improved it. My children survived, that was what mattered. And afterward? I left again. They rebuilt better this time. That is what I wanted. Not children who needed me. Children who could endure even when I was gone.

Eventually they found the surface. I remember the first Tiefling who climbed out. She stood on the lip of a volcanic crater just before dawn. She had never seen the sky, imagine that. Her entire life had been stone ceilings, glowing rivers, smoke, darkness. Then she climbed over the ridge and saw the horizon. I was there, not as myself obviously. I had horns at the time, small ones, very fashionable. I was pretending to be a traveler. She looked at me, then at the sunrise.

“Is it always that large?”

“The sky?”

She nodded.

“Usually.”

She stared for nearly an hour. Then she laughed, same laugh as the first spirits: different voice, same fire. I nearly told her who I was, nearly. But why ruin it? Instead, I walked beside her. We traveled together for several weeks. She wanted to see everything: forests, snow, the sea. She hated the sea, good instincts. Sorry, Yvander. She eventually found a town, then another, then she met people unlike herself. Some feared her. Some welcomed her. Some tried to cheat her. One tried to stab her; she threw him through a table, so I bought her a drink afterward, lovely girl.

Eventually she asked me where I came from. “Oh, everywhere,” I told her. She thought I was avoiding the question. I was. She went east. I went west. Never saw her again. That is life. Rokesh says I should keep records. Ouro’ras says I should establish laws. Yvander thinks I should visit my children more often. Golestandt mostly understands me, though he pretends not to. Lotus thinks the whole thing is fascinating.

The Greater Dragons are worse. They call me irresponsible. Nekthar thinks I should command armies. Pyroxis thinks I should oversee every forge. Ignivar once tried explaining that my children needed “structured divine instruction.” I threw him into a lake. He deserved it, probably.

The truth is, they are all mistaken about what my responsibility is. I created my children. I gave them fire. I gave them strong bodies. I gave them minds. I gave them a world difficult enough to teach them what I could not.

What remains? Should I tell every smith where to strike? Every traveler which road to take? Every warrior when to fight? Every lover whom to choose? Every ruler how to govern? No. That would make them extensions of me. I did not create extensions of myself. I created people, and people must be allowed to surprise you.

So now I travel. I keep a dozen faces. Sometimes I am a Tiefling mercenary. Sometimes a human adventurer. Once I spent twelve years as a dwarven blacksmith.Nobody questioned why I was seven feet tall; dwarves are wonderfully polite about that sort of thing. I have fought monsters,robbed tombs, won fortunes, lost fortunes.I started three tavern brawls, only one was my fault, maybe two. I once traveled with a band of adventurers for forty years and none of them ever discovered who I was. Well, one suspected, but he was eaten by something in a swamp before he could prove it. Unfortunate, I liked him.

Sometimes, in my travels, I meet my children. They never recognize me. That is my favorite part. I sit beside them. Drink with them. Fight beside them. Listen to them complain about their lives. Sometimes they even complain about me. Those are especially entertaining.

“Vlarunga abandoned us,” one priest said once. I was sitting three chairs away.

“Terrible mother,” another agreed. I bought them both another round.

Maybe they were right, but then the first priest told me about his daughter, a smith, brilliant girl. She invented a furnace that burned twice as hot using half the fuel. His eyes lit up when he spoke about her: pride, love, fire. I thought, *There. That is why I left, because she did that. Not me. Her.*

My children have kingdoms now, forges, families, wars, songs, failures, victories. Some curse my name. Some worship me. Some insist I never existed, I like those ones; they have spirit. And the others—the other Dragons—they still say I am shirking my responsibilities. Maybe I am, but whenever they lecture me, I look down at the world, at every furnace glowing in the night, at every Tiefling child learning to stand, at every fire spirit racing through the mountains, at every smith raising a hammer, at every fool who walks into danger because somewhere inside them burns the certainty that they can survive it, and I smile, because they are still burning. They do not need me to tend the flame. I lit it. My children learned how to keep it alive. My work is done.

Now, I heard there is a tavern somewhere in the east where no one has managed to drink the owner under the table in eighty years. That sounds like a challenge, and I have never been very good at ignoring those.
