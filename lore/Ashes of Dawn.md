# **Ashes of Dawn:**

### **A Recollection of Vlarunga, Primal Dragon of Fire**

I have always liked mortals. The others never really understood that. Ouro’ras loves mortals, certainly, but he loves them in the way a shepherd loves sheep. Everything must be ordered. Everything must be guided. Every choice must point toward some greater purpose that only he and Kalistos properly understand. Rokesh loves his Kobolds with such intensity that I sometimes wonder whether he remembers the rest of creation exists. Yvander watches. Golestandt broods. Lotus pokes things to see what happens.

And me? I like mortals. I like sitting beside them in taverns. I like losing money to them at cards. I like listening to them complain about taxes and mud and their mother-in-law's cooking as though these were the greatest disasters creation had ever endured. I like watching them build things that ought not stand and then cheer when they do. I like fighting beside them. I like their little lives. Perhaps that sounds cruel, calling them little. I do not mean it that way. When you have seen mountains born, continents crack, seas boil, and forests grow from seedlings into kingdoms of green, a mortal life is little. Brief. A spark tossed upward from the forge, but a spark can be beautiful, and sometimes, if you are careless, you can fall in love with one. I learned that during the reign of Ha-Des. Though that was not his name then, not yet.

I had been traveling through western Hurley for several months when I met Verrik Dawn. Naturally, I was not traveling as myself. That would ruin everything. There is very little adventure in walking into a village as a forty-foot dragon wreathed in primordial flame. People either run away, prostrate themselves, or start asking you to solve problems.

"My crops won't grow, Great Vlarunga."

"My husband drinks too much, Great Vlarunga."

"The eastern bridge needs repairing, Great Vlarunga." I am the Primal Dragon of Fire, not a municipal works department, so I usually wore a mortal shape.

In those days I favored a red-haired woman named Vara. Original, I know. I had horns, small ones. I told people I was a Tiefling. Nobody questioned it much. Tieflings were rare enough in Hurley, so many attributed any strangeness to my race.. I carried an old sword that I almost never needed. I wore leather armor because plate was too uncomfortable, and I deliberately kept my magic unimpressive. A little flame here. A little heat there. Enough to convince people I was an adventurer. Not enough to convince them I could turn a castle into molten slag if irritated.

Verrik met me while I was attempting to steal my own horse. That requires explanation. I had bought the horse three days earlier. The innkeeper's son had then stolen it from me. I found it tied behind another tavern two villages away. Therefore, I was reclaiming my property. Verrik disagreed, "Step away from the horse."

I looked over my shoulder. He was leaning against the alley wall with his arms crossed: Beastkin, wolf, mostly, tall, brown-grey fur. One ear had a notch missing from it, and there was a pale scar running from beneath his left eye toward the corner of his muzzle. He carried a longsword across his back and wore one of those ridiculous traveling cloaks adventurers always wear because they think cloaks make them mysterious. They do not. They make you wet when it rains. "It is my horse," I said.

"So all horse thieves say."

"I have papers."

"Of course you do."

"I bought him."

"Naturally."

I stared at him. He stared at me. Then the horse bit me. Verrik laughed. I nearly burned the entire alley. Instead, I pointed at him and said, "I dislike you."

"Most criminals do."

"I am not a criminal."

"The horse remains unconvinced."

That was how we met. It took perhaps half an hour to find the merchant who had originally witnessed the sale. Another half hour to locate the innkeeper's idiot son. By evening, I had my horse back, the boy had lost three teeth after Verrik discovered he had also robbed several travelers, and Verrik had somehow convinced me to buy him dinner. I still do not understand how that happened.

We traveled together the next morning. There was no grand decision. No sacred oath. No moment where destiny descended from heaven and announced that our lives had become intertwined. We were simply going in the same direction, then we were going in the same direction the day after that, then the week after that. Then there was a merchant caravan being attacked by goblins, and we helped.

Then there was a ruined shrine where something with too many mouths had begun eating pilgrims. Then there was the village with the well that screamed. That one was Lotus's fault. She denies it. She is lying. Somewhere along the way, people began calling us companions. I suppose we were. Verrik was a very good swordsman. Not the best I had ever seen. Mortals love stories about warriors who can defeat twenty men at once. Most cannot. Verrik certainly could not, but he knew that. That was what made him good.

He never fought like someone expecting a bard to describe it later. He fought to survive. He watched feet. He used walls. He threw sand. He kicked knees. Once, when a bandit challenged him to honorable single combat, Verrik shot him with a crossbow. I liked him immensely.

"You could at least pretend to respect the traditions of heroic combat," I told him afterward.

"He had six men hiding in the trees."

"Yes."

"Three archers."

"Yes."

"And poison on his sword."

"Yes."

"And you wanted me to duel him?"

"I wanted to see whether you would."

He looked at me for a long time, "You are a deeply strange woman, Vara."

"You have no idea."

That became something of a joke between us. He would say it whenever I did something unusual. When I picked up a hot cooking pot without thinking, when I walked barefoot across embers, when I knew the name of some ruin supposedly forgotten for three thousand years, when an Infernal bowed to me on the road and then fled before Verrik could ask questions.

"You are a deeply strange woman, Vara."

And I would smile, "You have no idea."

Hurley was rotting in those days. People like to imagine empires collapse dramatically. They imagine armies, burning cities, kings dragged from their thrones, but empires rarely begin dying with a scream. They begin dying with a shrug: a road goes unrepaired, a magistrate stops holding court, a governor realizes that the emperor has not answered a petition in six months. Soldiers are not paid. Bandits discover nobody is coming to stop them. A tax collector still arrives, of course. Tax collectors survive everything.

Ha-Des still ruled Drynell, technically, Alexander. That was his name. Alexander. Remember that. Someone should. He had not always been Ha-Des. That is important too. He had been a prince once. A troubled one, certainly, quiet, withdrawn, frightened of responsibility. Burdened by an empire already decaying beneath generations of excess, suspicion, and greed.

Then he found the Rod, or perhaps the Rod found him. I never learned which. At first it seemed to solve his problems. Officials obeyed. Rebellious nobles became compliant. Governors who once ignored imperial orders suddenly knelt. Alexander discovered that ruling was much easier when everyone simply did what he wanted. That is the seduction of power, not cruelty, convenience.

Eventually, he stopped speaking to men when he could command their minds; stopped visiting provinces when he could project his will; stopped appearing publicly when he could act through servants. Then he stopped acting much at all. He withdrew further and further from the world. His ministers ruled in his name. His magistrates collected taxes in his name. His soldiers killed in his name, and somewhere inside the palace, Alexander became less a man and more a shadow tied to a rod.

People began calling him Ha-Des, the Dark Ruler, the Emperor Who Was Not There. I did not care, not then. Empires rise. Empires fall. Mortals create governments. Mortals destroy governments. I had decided very early in creation that I would not spend eternity fixing every foolish decision my children made. That was Ouro’ras's hobby; I had adventures to pursue and Verrik was very good company.

The day I realized I loved him, we were surrounded by thirty-seven men. I remember the number, thirty-seven: mercenaries, Imperial irregulars, technically, though by that point the distinction between soldier and bandit had become academic. They had been extorting villages along the old northern road and so se had interfered. They objected. There were too many, that rarely concerned me, but the problem was Verrik. If I transformed, he would learn exactly who his traveling companion had been. I had traveled with mortals before, many times; eventually a lot of them discover the truth. Some kneel; some become frightened; some become obsessed; a few begin treating every casual remark as divine revelation.

Once I told a companion that his stew needed salt. Three generations later there was apparently a religious ceremony involving salted soup, so I delayed. Verrik was bleeding, badly. I could hear his breathing growing ragged. My sword was broken and seven men were moving around behind us with another ten ahead and archers on the ridge. I remember thinking: *Enough.* I could feel my true form pressing against the mortal one, fire beneath skin, wings behind shoulders. The delightful pressure before transformation, then Verrik grabbed my arm.

"Run."

I looked at him, "What?"

"Western slope."

He was barely standing, "There are twelve men there."

"Nine now."

"Verrik"

"Vara."

He looked at me. There was blood on his teeth, "Run."

Then the idiot charged, not away, forward, straight into them. He screamed something, I never learned what, and drove his sword into the first man's shield hard enough to knock him backward into another. He ducked, cut low, spun. Took a spear across the shoulder, kept moving. One man fell, then another, then another. It was not graceful. It was not heroic. It was desperate, ugly, beautiful.

He carved an opening through their line, "For once in your miserable life," he shouted, "listen to me!"

Then someone struck him from behind and Verrik fell. He did not rise. Something inside me became very quiet. I stepped through the opening he had made. The mercenaries cheered; they thought I was fleeing, then I turned around and I let Vara burn away. There are moments when mortals understand how very small they are. This was one. My wings unfolded across the valley. My horns tore through the glamour. The earth blackened beneath my claws. Flame rolled between my teeth. One of the mercenaries dropped his sword. Another whispered my name, "Vlarunga."

I smiled, "Hello." The fight lasted perhaps twelve seconds. I was being careful.

Verrik woke three days later. He was lying beneath a tree. His wounds had been cleaned. His ribs were wrapped. I was sitting beside a small fire, cooking rabbit. He opened one eye and saw the destruction all around him, "You burned them."

"Some."

"The hillside is gone."

"I became frustrated."

"The stones are glass."

"Yes."

He closed his eye again. There was a long silence. Then: "You are a deeply strange woman, Vara." I laughed so hard I nearly dropped dinner. He opened his eyes again. "Was I supposed to bow?"

"Please don't."

"Pray?"

"Absolutely not."

"Offer sacrifice?"

"I will throw you into the river."

He smiled, "Good."

That was all. He had questions, of course he did. Who was I? How old was I? Had I really created his people's ancient furnaces? Could I command volcanoes? Had I met the other dragons? Was the story about Ouro’ras and the naked king true? Yes. Very much yes, but when I did not answer, Verrik did not press. That mattered more than I understood at the time. He did not suddenly treat me differently. He still argued with me, still complained when I cheated at cards, still told me my cooking was terrible, still called me Vara when we were alone.

Once, after watching an entire village kneel as I passed, he whispered, "Enjoying yourself?"

"Immensely."

"Liar."

"Completely."

"Liar."

Then he took my hand. That was the first time. Love did not descend upon us like lightning. That would have been Caltheris's style. Ours crept: a hand held too long, a shoulder leaned against another beside a campfire. The realization that I had begun measuring journeys by whether Verrik would enjoy them. He made me laugh. I made him furious. He worried constantly; I pretended not to notice.

One evening, while camped beside a river, he asked: "Are you going to live forever?"

"Probably."

He stared into the fire, "And me?"

"No."

That was the first time mortality had felt cruel to me. I had known thousands who died: friends, champions, companions. Mortals die, that is what mortals do. Usually I accepted it, but with Verrik, suddenly I hated it.

He nodded slowly, "Then we'd better not waste time." He kissed me. So naturally I set his cloak on fire, by accident, mostly.

We stopped adventuring eventually. Not intentionally. We found a village named Dawncross. Verrik insisted this was proof that providence wanted us to stay there because his surname was Dawn. I told him this was idiotic. We stayed anyway. The village had once sat along an imperial trade route, by then the road was broken. The guard post was abandoned. The magistrate lived two valleys away and appeared only when taxes were due. Dawncross had perhaps two hundred people: farmers, millers, a blacksmith with one arm, a healer who was nearly blind, good people, stubborn people. They had learned to survive without the empire.

At first, Verrik and I stayed for winter, then spring, then another winter. We bought a small house. I repaired the chimney, badly. Verrik repaired my repairs. We planted vegetables. I discovered that controlling primordial flame does not make one good at growing onions.

Then Lyla came, our daughter, our beautiful little impossible Lyla. She was Dragonborn, there was no hiding that. She had crimson scales along her cheeks, little black horns, golden eyes, and a tail that knocked cups from tables with astonishing efficiency. When she sneezed, smoke came from her nose. When she became angry, candles lit themselves. The first time she cried hard enough, our curtains caught fire.

Verrik looked at the burning window, then at me, then at Lyla, "You."

"Possibly."

"Definitely you."

She laughed whenever I transformed. Most children scream the first time they see a dragon. Lyla clapped, "Again!" So I would change back, Then transform again, again, again, again, twenty times. Verrik would sit on the porch watching the Primal Dragon of Fire transform repeatedly because a three-year-old found it amusing.

I had fought ancient horrors. I had shaped mountains. I had watched the first forge ignite. Nothing in my existence had prepared me for father and daughter conspiring to make me perform tricks. Those were the happiest years of my life. I wish I had understood that while I was living them.

Dawncross began depending upon us. How could they not? Bandits stopped coming. Monsters disappeared. The road became safe. Verrik organized watches. I taught their smith how to build a proper furnace. People from neighboring settlements began coming for help: disputes, protection, food during bad harvests. Soon they stopped traveling to the magistrate, that was our mistake.

His name was Magistrate Corvan, a small man, not physically, but spiritually. He had inherited his position. His father had collected taxes for Drynell. His grandfather had collected taxes for Drynell. Therefore Corvan believed the people existed primarily so his family could collect taxes from them. When he visited Dawncross and discovered the villagers asking Verrik to mediate disputes, he became furious, "You undermine imperial authority."

Verrik laughed. That did not help, "What authority?"

"The Emperor's."

"Which emperor?"

Corvan's face went red. "Emperor Alexander rules all Hurley."

"Then perhaps Emperor Alexander should repair his road."

I should have stopped him. Instead, I laughed. Corvan looked at me. He knew. Everyone knew by then, not officially, but rumors travel. He knew exactly who stood before him. His anger turned instantly to terror. I leaned forward, "Go home, Magistrate." He did.

I thought that was the end of it because I was arrogant. I believed fear would keep my family safe. I believed that because Corvan would never dare challenge me directly, he could not hurt me. I forgot the oldest truth mortals ever taught themselves: if someone cannot hurt you, they will hurt what you love.

Lotus summoned us several weeks later, all six Primals. That almost never happened. Something about disturbances in the northern magical currents. I considered refusing, but Verrik insisted I go.

"You complain constantly that the others never tell you anything."

"They usually tell me boring things."

"Go."

"Lotus will talk for hours."

"Go."

"Rokesh will somehow make the conversation about Kobolds."

"Vara."

I hated when he used that tone. Lyla hugged my leg, "Bring me something."

"What?"

"Dragon treasure."

"I am dragon treasure."

She considered this, "Different dragon treasure."

Verrik laughed. I kissed him, then her. I remember exactly what she smelled like: soap, woodsmoke, and berries. I remember Verrik's hand resting against my cheek. I remember him saying: "Come home soon."

I said: "I always do." Those were the last words I spoke to him.

The meeting lasted three days, three days. That is all. When I returned, Dawncross was burning. At first I thought it had been raided, then I saw imperial banners, soldiers, not many. Perhaps fifty. Corvan had waited until I left. He accused Verrik of sedition and insurrection, usurpation of imperial authority. The villagers resisted his arrest. So Corvan declared Dawncross rebellious. The soldiers came before sunrise. Verrik fought, of course he fought. They killed him in front of our house. Lyla ran to him. Someone, I never learned who, perhaps that is mercy, someone drove a spear through her back. She was six. Six. My daughter was six years old.

Corvan ordered the bodies burned. Treason, apparently, did not deserve burial. When I arrived, the pyre was still warm, there are pains too large for screams. I found Verrik first. I recognized the clasp from his cloak. The ridiculous cloak I had mocked for years, then Lyla. One horn had survived the fire. I held it in my hand, for a very long time. Someone approached behind me, a villager, old Marra.

She was crying, "Vlarunga…"

I turned. She stopped. Everyone stopped. The fires throughout Dawncross bent toward me. Every candle. Every hearth. Every burning roof. Every ember. All of them turned. Like flowers toward the sun, "Where?"

Marra trembled, "Magistrate Corvan returned to Valecross." I stood. The sky turned red.

I killed Corvan first. I wish I could say I gave him some magnificent speech. Something worthy of history. I did not. He begged. I burned him. Then I found the soldiers who had gone to Dawncross. Then their captain. Then the governor who approved the order. Then the official who appointed the governor. I followed records, names, seals, chains of command.

At every step I found another person who said: "I was following orders."

"I had no choice."

"It was lawful."

"The Emperor's authority must be maintained."

The Emperor. Always the Emperor: Ha-Des, so I continued. Courthouses burned. Tax offices burned. Magistrates fled their homes when they heard I was coming. Some resigned. I killed several anyway. That frightens people when I tell the story. Good. It should. I was not dispensing justice. I was angry. There is a difference.

Ouro’ras found me outside Tarsis. I had destroyed the governor's palace. He landed before me in a blaze of white, "Enough."

I walked past him, "Move."

"This is not justice."

"I did not say it was."

"Vlarunga."

"Move."

"They did not all kill them."

"They served the one who did."

"That does not make every servant guilty."

"I do not care." He stepped in front of me again. I struck him. Not hard. Hard enough. Light exploded across the valley. He did not retaliate. That made me angrier. "Do you know what they did to her? SHE WAS SIX!"

The mountains echoed. Ouro’ras lowered his head, "I know."

"No. You don't."

And perhaps that was unfair. Perhaps he did, but grief is not reasonable, "I am going to kill Ha-Des."

Ouro’ras looked toward the east, "Then you may already be too late."

Hurley was burning before I ever reached the imperial heartland. Rebellion had erupted everywhere. Cities rejected imperial governors. Soldiers deserted. Nobles declared independence. Peasants burned tax records. Priests denounced the Rod. The empire was collapsing. Good. I wanted it dead. I crossed battlefields without choosing sides. Armies fled when they saw me. I entered the imperial capital through gates that had been abandoned. The palace was nearly empty. I tore open the throne room. No emperor. I descended beneath it. No emperor. I shattered sealed chambers. No emperor. Finally, deep beneath the palace, I found the place where Alexander had performed his last transformation.

The Rod's chamber, his body was gone. Not dead, gone. He had withdrawn so far into the artifact, into shadow, into whatever pathetic imitation of immortality he had constructed, that there was nothing left for me to kill. I screamed his name. His real name. "Alexander!" Nothing. "COME OUT!" Nothing. I breathed fire through the chamber until stone ran like water. Nothing. I tore apart the throne. Nothing. I shattered statues. Nothing. I clawed through the walls with my bare hands. Nothing. He had escaped me. Verrik was dead. Lyla was dead. Dawncross was ashes, and the man whose empire had killed them had slipped beyond my claws. So I sat in the ruins of the throne room, and for the first time since I was created, I wept until I had no fire left.

Then I decided Alexander would die after all, nNot his spiri, not his body, but his name, his memory, his legacy. If I could not kill the man, I would kill everything he hoped history might remember. Alexander the Troubled Prince? No. Alexander the frightened boy crushed beneath an empire already falling apart? No. Alexander the ruler seduced by an artifact he could no longer control? No. Those stories invite sympathy, understanding, pity. I wanted none of it. I went to the archives. I burned his childhood records. I destroyed his portraits. I shattered monuments bearing his birth name. I found imperial chroniclers and told them: "You will call him Ha-Des."

Some objected. I persuaded them. I found bards, priests, rebels, soldiers, survivors. "Tell them what he became." The Dark Ruler. The Emissary of Death. The Emperor of Shadow. The man who bent minds. The ruler who abandoned his people. The one whose negligence turned law into cruelty and empire into ash. Were the stories exaggerated? Eventually of course. That is what stories do. Did I correct them? No.

When someone claimed Ha-Des drank the blood of children, I said nothing. When someone claimed flowers died wherever his shadow passed, I said nothing. When someone claimed he had never been mortal at all, I said nothing. Alexander disappeared. Ha-Des remained. Within generations, scholars argued whether Alexander had ever existed. Within centuries, almost nobody remembered the name. I did. I remember every syllable. Alexander. Sometimes I wonder whether what I did was crueler than killing him. Good.

I returned to Dawncross once. Years later. The village had been rebuilt. Not in the same place. A little farther north. The survivors had planted a tree where our house once stood. Someone had carved three names into a stone beneath it. Verrik Dawn, Lyla Dawn, and Vara. Not Vlarunga, Vara. I sat beneath that tree until sunset. An old woman approached me, Marra's daughter, I think. She did not recognize me. I had changed my face. She sat beside me anyway.

"You visiting someone?"

"Yes."

"Family?"

I looked at the stone, "Yes."

She nodded, "Good people?"

I laughed, then cried, "Very."

We sat silently. After a while she told me the story the villagers remembered. There had once been an adventurer named Verrik Dawn. He protected the village when the empire could not. His wife was a strange Tiefling woman named Vara. Their daughter was Lyla. When the magistrate came, Verrik defended his neighbors. He died doing so. Then, according to the story, the Dragon of Fire herself descended from the heavens in rage and avenged them. I almost corrected her, almost.

Instead I asked: "What happened to Vara?"

The woman shrugged, "No one knows."

I smiled, "Probably went adventuring."

"Maybe." She stood. Before leaving, she touched Verrik's name, "We remember them."

After she was gone, I placed my hand against Lyla's, "I remember too."

That was a very long time ago. Long enough that Dawncross is gone. Long enough that languages have changed. Long enough that kingdoms have risen over the graves of kingdoms that rose over the graves of Drynell. Mortals sometimes ask why I wander. Why I hide my name. Why I still take mortal shape and walk into taverns looking for adventurers. Perhaps I should have stopped after Verrik. Perhaps love should have taught me caution. Instead it taught me the opposite. Verrik lived only a little while. Lyla lived hardly at all, yet those years remain among the greatest treasures of my existence.

That is the terrible thing about mortals. You know how the story ends. You know from the beginning. They will age. They will weaken. They will die,and if you are something like me, you will remain, but knowing the flame will go out does not make the flame less warm. Sometimes it makes you draw closer. I still have Lyla's horn. I still have Verrik's cloak clasp, and somewhere, if Ha-Des still exists, wandering as a shadow bound to that accursed Rod, I hope he remembers me.

I hope he remembers the sound of his palace breaking. I hope he remembers the name I took from him because Alexander the Troubled Prince is dead. I killed him. History knows only Ha-Des now, the Dark Ruler, the Emissary of Death. And if someday that shadow crawls back into this world, if he finds flesh again, if he dares take form where my fire can reach him, then, after all these thousands of years, Verrik and Lyla will finally have their vengeance. And I will finally have mine.
