In the beginning, there was Light, not mine, never mine.

Before Phanatos turned beneath the heavens, before stone knew weight or water knew depth, before there was an eye capable of beholding brightness, there was He who is Light Himself: Kalistos.

I have heard mortals call me the Dragon of Light. They carve the title beneath my likeness. They raise golden towers toward the dawn and say that the sun bears something of my glory; they are mistaken. I possess no glory that was not first given. He who is Light itself gave me the privilege of being the light to this world. That is all. I am not the flame from which all things burn. I am but one ray cast forth from it. He is the Light. I am but a ray of that Light, being shone upon Phanatos, and what a privilege it has been.

When first I looked upon this world, I saw what the others saw: emptiness waiting to be filled. Rokesh saw mountains waiting for feet. Yvander saw oceans waiting for motion. Vlaurunga saw the deep fires and wondered what might endure them. Lotus saw mysteries hidden even from us. Golestandt looked into darkness and heard life whispering within it, but I saw something else. I saw order, or rather, I saw where order might be.

The world was beautiful, certainly, but beauty seeks one who can behold it. The mountains required someone to climb them. The seas required someone to cross them. The stars required someone to count them, and above all, creation required creatures capable of looking beyond creation. Creatures who could see the ray and ask after the Light, so I gathered my power.

I touched earth, and shaped bodies capable of standing upright beneath the heavens. I breathed light into minds so that they might reason. I touched their tongues so that they might speak to one another. I formed hands that could build, write, cultivate, paint, fight, heal, and, most importantly,raise themselves in prayer.

Thus came my children: Humans, Elves, Dwarves, Gnomes, Halflings, and the many peoples who would spring from them and beside them. I loved them immediately. Perhaps that was my first weakness, or perhaps it was simply what Kalistos intended. I could not merely create them and depart. How could I? A father does not place a child upon a road and say, “Walk wherever you wish. I have done enough.” A craftsman does not construct a tower and refuse to correct it when its foundations crack. We had been entrusted with Phanatos. Entrusted. That word matters.

The others sometimes forgot it. Vlaurunga was among the worst. I would find her wandering some distant land wearing the face of a mortal, laughing in a tavern or joining some band of adventurers while her children struggled through wars and calamities.

“You have responsibilities,” I told her.

She laughed, “They're alive, aren't they?”

“That is not sufficient.”

“It is for them.”

“No. It is sufficient for you.”

She called me unbearable. I called her irresponsible. Neither of us has changed our mind.

Yvander was hardly better, though at least she watched. Always watching from that frozen cavern of hers, knowing every current and every movement beneath the sea.

“You see their suffering,” I once told her.

“Yes.”

“And you do nothing?”

“Usually.”

I could never understand her.

Golestandt… Golestandt was different. There was tenderness in him, certainly, but far too much hesitation. He loved his children so deeply that he sometimes confused love with permitting them anything they desired. Love without order becomes indulgence. He did not appreciate hearing this.

Rokesh, at least, understood duty. Not as I did, perhaps. He could be stubborn beyond measure. He cared too much for stone and tradition and the slow working of things. But he remained near his children. He taught them. Protected them. Corrected them. I respected him for that. Still do.

Lotus? Lotus does as Lotus wills. I long ago learned that asking the Dragon of the Arcane to explain herself merely produces another mystery.

And so, when the others would not act, I often did. Not merely for my own children, but for theirs as well. A Kobold village threatened by beasts? If Rokesh could not reach them, I would send aid. A Lanyarui vessel lost beneath a storm? If Yvander remained silent, I would guide them by a light upon the horizon. A wandering Tiefling pursued by those who feared her? I cared little whether Vlaurunga believed hardship would “shape character.” I would place someone upon the road who might protect her.

They complained, often.

“You meddle,” said Yvander.

“You interfere,” said Vlaurunga.

“You do not trust them,” said Golestandt.

Perhaps, but when I looked upon suffering, I could not simply ask whether it belonged to me. All Phanatos belonged to Kalistos. Therefore, in some lesser sense, all Phanatos was my concern. That does not mean I believed myself wise enough to know everything, quite the opposite.

When I did not know what was right, I prayed. When events exceeded my understanding, I lifted them upward. “Kalistos,” I would say, “this is beyond me.” And then I would wait. Sometimes the answer came as clarity. Sometimes as circumstance. Sometimes as silence. Silence was the most difficult, but even silence must be obeyed.

I desired nothing except to do what I perceived He desired. That became the rule by which I ordered myself, and the rule by which I taught my children. Build. Cultivate. Defend. Teach. Make laws that protect the weak. Raise cities in which beauty points beyond itself. Let rulers govern by justice rather than appetite. Let scholars seek truth because all truth ultimately terminates in Him. Let families become schools of virtue. Let your whole civilization become an arrow pointed toward Heaven. That was why I made Heraklion, my city of light.

I chose a place upon Hurly and laid its foundations myself. I set its streets according to harmony and proportion. I raised walls strong enough that generations might live without fear. I built plazas where philosophers could argue, markets where merchants could trade, schools where children could learn, and temples where prayers could rise to Kalistos. Heraklion was never meant to glorify me. It was meant to demonstrate what mortals might accomplish when their lives were rightly ordered. A city upon the world like a lamp upon a stand, and for a time, it was.

I would walk its streets unseen. I would hear craftsmen singing while they worked. Children reciting lessons. Judges pronouncing verdicts. Priests offering prayers, and I was pleased. Then I began hearing my own name. At first I thought little of it.

“Ouro’ras, guide us.”

“Ouro’ras, protect our children.”

“Ouro’ras, bless this harvest.”

Such prayers could be understood properly. A mortal might ask another mortal for aid without mistaking him for the source of all aid. I would simply carry such prayers upward.

“Kalistos,” I would say, almost amused, “they mean You, even if they have confused the messenger.”

But the prayers multiplied. Shrines became temples. Temples became sanctuaries. People knelt before statues of me. They called me Lord of Light. They thanked me for victories I had not granted. They credited me with creation itself.

And one morning in Heraklion, I watched a mother lift her newborn child toward an image of me. “Ouro’ras,” she whispered, “this child is yours.”

No. I wanted to scream the word across the city. No. Your child belongs to Kalistos. You belong to Kalistos. I belong to Kalistos. Everything belongs to Kalistos. Do you not understand? The light which comforts you is not mine! I am the window! Why have you begun worshiping the glass?

I appeared before priests. I corrected them. “Do not worship me.”

They bowed. “Of course, Great Ouro’ras.”

“You misunderstand. Worship Kalistos.”

“As you command, Lord of Light.”

“No!”

Even my correction became another reason for reverence. My humility became a doctrine about my greatness. My obedience became evidence that I was worthy of worship. Every attempt to turn their eyes upward seemed only to turn them back toward me, and I began to understand something terrible. Perhaps my constant presence had taught them the wrong lesson.

I had desired to show them Kalistos through my works. Instead, some had stopped at me. They saw the ray and never sought the sun, so I intervened less. Not suddenly. I could not bear that. A city in danger would still sometimes find unexpected deliverance. A child lost in the wilderness might still see a golden light leading her home. A righteous king might still awaken with clarity concerning some impossible judgment, but these things became rarer. I stopped appearing openly. I stopped answering every cry. I allowed causes to remain hidden. And when men said, “Ouro’ras has saved us,” I remained silent. Perhaps, I thought, silence would teach what speech had failed to teach.

The other dragons noticed. Vlaurunga found me once upon a high mountain overlooking the lights of Heraklion. “Well,” she said, sitting beside me, “look who finally learned to leave his children alone.” I nearly struck her. She laughed. I did not.

“They worship me,” I said. Her laughter stopped. Below us, thousands of lights burned against the night. “They pray to me as though I were Him.” Vlaurunga looked down at the city. For once, she had no clever answer. “I wanted them to look through me,” I said. “Instead they looked at me.” She put a hand against my shoulder. It was unexpectedly gentle. Perhaps I have judged her too harshly. Only perhaps.

I still watch my children. I could never cease. And sometimes I intervene despite myself. A prayer rises from some forgotten village. A father begs that his daughter survive the night. A soldier asks for courage. A sinner asks whether he can begin again. And everything within me longs to descend. To mend. To correct. To illuminate. Sometimes I do. But now, before I move, I look beyond Phanatos. Beyond the heavens. Toward the Light from whom every light comes, and I ask: “Is this what You desire?”

For I have learned that even love can become disordered when it attempts to possess the work that belongs to God alone. I created my children so they might know the Light. I guided them so they might follow the Light. And now, perhaps, I must sometimes hide… so that they might finally see beyond the ray. He is the Light. I am but a ray of that Light, being shone upon Phanatos, and if the ray must fade for the eyes of my children to behold its Source—then let me fade.
