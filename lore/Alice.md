## Prologue: The Order from Korland

The stone citadel of Korland, heart of the Kobold Empire, stood as an unshakable monument to the will of Rokesh. Its towers gleamed with crystal-veined basalt, and the streets echoed with the chants of geomancers and the stomp of iron-booted patrols. Beneath it all, in the sealed chambers of the Imperial Kobold Secret Service (IKSS), a name was spoken in hushed reverence: Alice.

She was barely more than a whelp—young for a Kobold, still smoothing the ridges of her gem-scaled muzzle—but she was a Metabold, chosen by the Kedgelony and baptized in Snawk venom. The venom had not killed her. It had made her... precise. Now she was to be tested.

A rebel group had formed on Doran, an island state just past the reach of direct imperial control. It was once a Dwarven fort, now a nest of idealists: dissident gnomes, renegade elves, dwarves embittered by the Treaty of Emberquartz, and Kobolds who had turned their backs on the Kledgelony. Their dreams were noble. Their mistake was thinking the Empire would tolerate them.

Alice’s mission: Infiltrate. Befriend. Ascend. Then annihilate. She would be a sister to them. A sword among them. And finally—their grave.

## Chapter I: The Smile That Lied

Alice arrived in Doran as a stowaway refugee: “Ash,” a broken-smiled Kobold with a trembling voice and a fabricated past of imperial oppression. She wore no badge, only soot and scars—painted with care.

The rebels bought it. They welcomed her into the cliffs of Fort Starfall, now reoccupied by the so-called Free Union of Peoples. They gave her warm food, a room carved into volcanic stone, and worst of all—friendship.

She met Commander Elwick, a gnome war veteran who’d lost a hand to imperial shrapnel and gained a heart of gold; Liora, an elven seer who sang to the stars and said she saw something “fractured but radiant” in Alice’s aura; Torren Stonebeard, a dwarf smith who brewed his own mead and argued politics with passion; and Kael, a fellow Kobold deserter who joked too easily and trusted too quickly.

She should have kept her distance. She didn’t.

## Chapter II: The Long Pretend

Alice had assumed becoming one of them would be easy. Learn their names, mirror their speech patterns, echo their values just enough to blend in. She had done it before in lesser missions. But Doran was different.

Fort Starfall, once a dwarven artillery post carved into volcanic cliffs, now held warmth. Not just torchlight—real warmth. The kind that crept into the heart despite all training.

### 1. The Forge and the Fire

Torren Stonebeard, the dwarf smith, insisted on teaching her the forge’s rhythm, “Strike on the beat, girl. Not like you’re afraid of waking the mountain.”

Alice nodded, mimicking his heavy strikes, though her limbs weren’t built for dwarven smithing. Her strength came from precision, not brute force.

“You learn fast,” he grunted. “Most just make noise.”

She glanced up. “I prefer impact to volume.”

He laughed, loud and gravelly. “Spoken like a proper kobold. You ever consider defecting for good?”

She smiled. Just enough.

That night, he gave her a bracelet hammered from black Doranite ore. “For luck,” he said. “Or vengeance. Whichever you’re short on.” She would wear it every day.

### 2. A Shared Watch with Kael

Kael was trouble from the start—too perceptive, too familiar. A fellow kobold who’d left the Empire under… unclear circumstances. They watched the sea together one moonless night.

“You don’t sleep much,” he said, leaning on the stone parapet beside her.

“Old habit,” she replied. “Some of us were trained not to.”

He tilted his head. “IKSS?”

Her heart skipped, but her face didn’t flinch.

He laughed. “Relax. I was, too. Briefly. Before I failed their ‘moral flexibility exam.’ Too sentimental, they said. Can you believe that?” Alice said nothing. Her grip on the railing tightened. “I know the signs,” he continued. “The pauses in speech. The way you don’t ask too many questions. You’re not like the others.”

She turned to him slowly. “And what am I like?”

He smiled. “Alone. Like me.”

He never asked again.

### 3. Liora’s Stars

Liora, the elven seer, was the first to see through her lies—but only poetically, “You hold something cracked inside you,” she said, one twilight after teaching Alice the constellations of the Western Sky. “But light spills through the cracks. That’s why the stars like you.”

Alice scoffed. “They must have low standards.”

Liora smiled sadly. “No. Just soft hearts.”

Later, Liora would teach her to dance in the glade near the sulfur springs. Not a war dance—something old and elven. The kind passed from mother to daughter.

“You move like a soldier trying to be a ghost,” she teased. “But that’s alright. We need both.”

She began sharing meals. Telling jokes. Giving advice. She even sang once, off-key, at Brennan the quartermaster’s birthday. It was hideous and perfect. Everyone laughed, even Alice.

Her reports to Korland became increasingly clinical. Less... detailed. Names were replaced with numbers. Dates with estimates. She skipped the passage where Kael helped her when she got wounded in a training exercise. She didn’t mention that Torren cried when a shipment from his dead son’s old clan arrived, or that she held his hand.

She was eventually promoted to First Warden.

Elwick, the aging gnome commander, had grown too tired for field work. He passed the title with tears in his eyes and said, “You’ve got the spine of a mountain, Ash. Don’t let us fall.”

She accepted the pin. Her hands did not shake, but that night, she couldn’t sleep. She sat alone by the Pierre d’Amour in her satchel, watching it pulse like a sleeping animal. It no longer glowed from duty, it glowed from attachment.

Location: Fort Starfall, Doran Island*

The forge smoke smells like cedar and soot.

The plan is proceeding on schedule. I have access to blueprints of the substructure. I’ve begun mapping the optimal collapse vectors. Tectonic fault lines run close—Starfall’s bones are already brittle. One push. One rite. That’s all it will take.

But—

It’s not weakness. That’s not what this is. I was trained for identity layering—what the instructors called "sympathetic insertion." The purpose of the mask is to see what they see. To feel what they feel. It makes the knife cleaner.

I am still the knife. I know that.

But Kael—Kael is not the mission. He shouldn’t matter. Neither should Liora. Or Torren. Or the way they wait for me at supper. Or the bracelet I haven’t taken off. Maybe this is what it feels like to wear the mask too long. The edges blur. The lines shift, but this is not betrayal. It is proof of the Empire’s wisdom. How else could I know how deep the rot runs if I do not taste it?

They trust me. They love me. That is why it must be me. They will never see it coming, and the Empire will remember my name.

End Entry.

### 5. The Night Before

The wind howled through the narrow lava vents of Fort Starfall, whistling like ghosts in mourning. Most of the rebels were asleep. Only the faintest glow of embers lit the forge corridor, where Alice sat cross-legged on the warm stone floor.

She didn’t flinch when Kael appeared behind her, “I thought you’d be in the warden’s hall,” he said gently, sitting beside her. “You’re allowed to enjoy your promotion, you know.”

“I’m not used to celebrations,” she replied, staring at the emberlight. “They feel… borrowed.”

Kael chuckled. “You always talk like you’re passing through. Like none of this is yours.” Alice said nothing. Kael leaned back, folding his arms behind his head and looking up at the cracked basalt ceiling. “You know, I used to think betrayal was the worst thing that could happen to a person.” She tensed. “But I’ve come to realize,” he continued, “it’s not. It’s hope. False hope. That’s the sharpest knife.”

Alice turned toward him slowly. Her voice was quiet. “What made you say that?”

He didn’t answer right away. Then, in a voice more raw than she had ever heard from him, he said: “When I left the Empire… I didn’t do it because of morals or because I hated orders. I left because I watched a squadmate we trained with—Nari—follow an IKSS command to purge a dwarf village for harboring a scholar. I stood there while she lit the fuel lines. I saw the look on her face afterward.” He paused and exhaled slowly. “She didn’t regret it. She looked relieved. Like it made her cleaner somehow. That’s when I knew… the Empire didn’t just kill people. It rearranged their hearts.”

Alice swallowed. Hard.

Kael finally looked at her. “I see you, Ash. And I’ve wondered for a long time… if that look is in you, too.” Alice’s eyes burned—but not from the heat. Kael offered a weak smile. “But then I think—maybe you’re the proof I needed. That someone can crawl out of it. That something better’s possible.” He stood. “Don’t make me wrong,” he whispered.

Then he left her there, alone in the dark, with the stone beneath her trembling—not from tectonic fault lines, but from everything she was beginning to understand she could never bury.

## Chapter III: The Order Comes

The parchment was plain, unmarked by seal or sigil; it bore no colors of the Kledgelony or the IKSS. Only one thing made it unmistakable: the faint shimmer of encoded geomantic ink, visible only to those with metabold eyes.

Alice had found it slipped beneath her sleeping mat, folded with surgical precision. No knock, no courier. That’s how it always was when an agent was called to finish what they began.

She unfolded it slowly.

*To Agent Alice (Alias: Ash of Doran)*

*Final phase authorized. Structure: Starfall Fortress. Designation: CR-121 “Compromised Rebellion.” Imperial Clearance: Jade Fang. Protocol Ecliptica is now in effect.*

*Collapse primary structure at peak cohesion. Estimated body count: 4782. Survivors to be terminated by hand. Ensure non-transmission of any declarations. Artifacts and documents are to be unrecoverable.*

*Recognition upon successful termination: Full promotion to Black-Grade Operative. Title: Reaper.*

*The stone remembers.*

*Signed,*

*High Marshal Grovikh*

*Imperial Kobold Secret Service*

*Korland, Year 297*

Alice’s hands didn’t tremble. Her breath didn’t catch, but something, something, tightened in her chest like a vice. The bracelet Torren had forged for her clinked lightly as she closed her fist. She read the letter again.

*Ensure non-transmission of any declarations*.

It would happen during the speech. The Union had drafted a declaration of independence—an idealistic call for cross-species unity, rebellion, and freedom. Liora would recite it beneath the old dwarven banners, flanked by Kael and Elwick. Torren had even carved a stone dais for the occasion.

They would all be there. All of them. Alice folded the letter once more, neatly, reverently. Then she rose from her cot, stepped into the black of the corridor, and made her way to the inner sanctum of the fortress. Her eyes glowed faintly with snawk-born light as she placed her hand on the basalt wall, feeling for the pressure points—the old, quiet bones of the mountain.

Her power stirred. The stones whispered to her.

*Now, child of Rokesh. Now you bury them. Let them become foundation.*

## Chapter IV: The Collapse

The sky above Doran was the color of bruised copper—sunset bleeding into stormclouds. The central chamber of Fort Starfall hummed with excitement. Lanterns had been strung from ancient stone arches, and garlands of stitched cloth—crafted by Liora and the children—fluttered between rusted beams.

Torren stood near the forge, armor polished, holding a cask of his best mead. Liora hummed softly as she scrawled the final lines of the Declaration onto an oiled parchment, her script a dance of silver ink and hope. Kael adjusted the straps on his tunic, nervously checking the clasp of his dagger.

Alice stood at the back, by the base of the old dais, hands clasped before her like a silent guardian.

“Are we ready?” Elwick asked from atop the steps.

Kael grinned. “As we’ll ever be.”

Elwick nodded, raised the scroll with both hands. “Then let the world know—we are free.”

A cheer rose. Alice didn’t join. She closed her eyes. Felt the runes she had carved—hidden beneath the floor, behind the banners, inside the very bones of the fortress. She had carved them over weeks, during late watches, beneath excuses of maintenance and inspection. Now they thrummed beneath her feet like a waiting heart.

She whispered the activation phrase. Just one word. “Collapse.” It was barely audible, but the stones heard.

There was no flash. No thunder. Just a groaning—deep, ancient, as if the mountain itself had been betrayed. The floor beneath the dais buckled. A fissure split the chamber clean down the center. One by one, support pillars snapped like twigs. Screams rose like birds in a forest fire.

Liora’s eyes locked with Alice’s just before the stones took her. Her mouth opened—whether in accusation, or shock, or sorrow, Alice would never know.

Kael ran toward her.

“Ash—!”

He never finished. The ceiling came down.

Alice didn’t move. Didn’t weep. As the dust rolled over her like ocean spray, she stood unscathed, untouched, shielded by the pattern of collapse she had etched herself into. She waited.

When the rumbling stopped, the chamber was gone, only silence remained, broken only by the twitching of a single hand emerging from a pile of stone: Kael. Half-buried. Broken. Still alive. Alice walked toward him. He looked up at her, blood running down his snout, and said—quietly, “I knew.”

Her blade found his heart without ceremony. She held it there a moment longer than necessary. Then she stood, alone in the grave of trust and friendship she had built with her own hands. The rebels of Doran were no more.

## Chapter V: What Remains

The dust clung to her like a second skin. All around her, the fortress was rubble. No laughter, no breath, no songs in the corridor. Only broken stone and shattered memories. Alice stood in the center of it, unmoving, like a statue carved from guilt.

Her armor was unscathed. Her heart—less so.

She didn’t report in. Not yet. She knew the proper protocols. The IKSS would expect a signal rune fired into the sky. Instead, she sat. There, atop the dais stone that had not cracked—one small circle of smoothness amid the ruin.

The pierre d’amour slid from her satchel, landing in her lap. The little gem-creature curled once… then went still. It had pulsed with life once. Warmth. Now it was cool to the touch. Not dead—just… dormant. Silent.

“Mission complete,” she whispered. “The rebellion is ended.” But the words tasted bitter. Like volcanic ash on the tongue.

She stared into the dark where Liora had stood. She could still hear the last note of her hum. The soft whistle in the silence where Elwick’s laugh should be. The echo of Kael’s voice, not in pain—but in trust, “Don’t make me wrong.”

She hadn’t cried since her venom trial. The day her bones cracked, and her magic awoke. Back then, the Kedgelony told her pain was the chisel that shaped stone into greatness.

But now, sitting in the grave she made, she realized something: she had shaped herself into a tomb, a perfect one. She remembered the smiles. The awkward dance steps. The way Torren’s mead always tasted like burnt cherries. The stories Kael had told her about his sister. The way Liora said the stars liked her—not because she was good, but because she was fractured.

What did they see in her? What had she let them see?

She pressed a hand to her chest, not in anguish, but curiosity. As if unsure if there was still anything warm beneath.

And still the stone was silent.

*They will call me Reaper. But I don’t know whose name they’ll be burying me under.*

## Chapter VI: The Stone Crown

Korland loomed ahead—its obsidian towers crowned with thunderclouds, ever-smoking, ever-breathing like the mountain it was carved from. The capital of the Empire was not built on stone, but into it. It pulsed with the heartbeat of Rokesh himself: iron resolve, unbending will, and the cold sanctity of order.

Alice approached its gates alone. Her armor bore the dust of Doran. Her blade, still uncleaned. The pierre d’amour was tucked away in a sealed compartment—unseen, unspoken, unwelcome in these halls.

The Imperial Watch let her pass without a word. They knew her.

At the core of the city, beneath the spire of Judgment, the Chamber of Obsidian Oaths awaited.

Inside stood three figures: High Marshal Grovikh, head of the IKSS, whose face was chiseled from the same stone as his judgments; Lady Emmera of the Kedgelony, voice of Rokesh’s will, robed in prayer-carved shale; and Empress Joan, enthroned in silence, her gaze unreadable, a circlet of polished crystal upon her brow. Alice knelt before them: no pageantry, no applause, only stillness.

Grovikh’s voice echoed through the chamber like gravel tumbling down a cliff, “Alice, codenamed Ash. Metabold of the Black Vein. You were given a sacred charge—to root out rot where it hid. Did you complete your mission?”

Alice’s reply was quiet, steady, “Yes. The Free Union has ended. There were no survivors.” A pause.

Emmera stepped forward. She did not smile, “Did you hesitate?”

Alice looked up. “No.”

Empress Joan raised one hand, “Then rise, Reaper.”

She did and an aide stepped forward, carrying a ceremonial cloak—black marble velvet, etched with silver veins like cracked fault lines. The Cloak of Quiet Ends. Only one of its kind had ever been worn at a time.

Grovikh himself draped it over her shoulders, “From this day forward,” he said, “you are no longer Ash. You are no longer Alice. You are the Empire’s silence before the quake. You are its stillness after the scream. You are, Reaper!”

Empress Joan spoke, and her voice was like bedrock splitting: “You have become what they feared. What they never saw coming. And what no one will escape when the Empire calls upon you again.”

Alice bowed her head, but inside, something shifted, not a crack, not yet, but a tremor. And in the folds of her cloak, the pierre d’amour gave a single faint pulse, like a memory refusing to die

Stone and Shadow

## Chapter One: The Fort in the Fog

They called it Outpost Z-73, but to Louis, it was just "the fort." Perched on a crag in the highlands of Monteforte, it looked like someone had carved a scar into the mountain and filled it with stone and soldiers. The fog rolled in most mornings, thick as soup, and lingered through nightfall like it had nowhere else to be. It suited the place. This wasn’t a post you got sent to for honor or ambition. This was where you went when you were needed to be forgotten.

Louis didn’t mind. He hadn’t signed up for glory anyway. He’d joined for the pay, the pension, the land grant at the end of ten years. Lava forging had been honest work back home on Morcreau, but the forge had closed when the Empire restructured mineral requisitions. His father told him to fight smart, not hard. His mother told him to come home whole. He’d taken both to heart.

It was a quiet life at Z-73, all things considered. He kept his head down, boiled tea for the other guards, carved names into the memorial wall when the courier brought back dog tags and shattered medallions. And when she returned, he was the only one who spoke.

The Reaper.

He’d seen her first during a rotation near the gate. Everyone else had snapped to attention, silent and still like the statues in the Temple of Rokesh. But Louis had smiled and said, "Welcome back."

She didn’t respond. Just walked past like the fog itself—swift, cold, and unreadable.

But he made a habit of it.

Every time she returned from one of her missions—blades dulled, cloak torn, boots crusted in ash or worse—he greeted her.

"Good to see you back, Reaper."

"Hope the ground was kind today."

"You didn’t miss much—just another storm and two arguments about which soup ration is worse."

The others gave him looks. Some asked if he had a death wish. One junior officer muttered, "You know what she does out there, right?"

Louis did. Everyone did. She was a Metabold, a weapon wrapped in skin. Rumors called her the Empire’s shadow. Her real name, whispered even among high command, was Alice. But they all called her the Reaper.

To Louis, though, she was still a kobold.

He wasn’t stupid. He didn’t expect anything back. But someone had to treat her like a person. Even a statue needed to be dusted now and then.

One evening, during a howling wind, he left a cup of stoneflower tea by her door. She didn’t answer. But the next morning, the cup was empty.

That was enough.

## Chapter Two: A Name Beneath the Cloak

Time passed like dripping stalactites—slow and steady in Z-73. Louis didn’t measure weeks anymore; he measured her returns. Every mission, every successful return, marked another quiet victory for him. Not because she ever smiled, or thanked him outright. But because he had started to notice changes.

A nod, once.

Then, another time, she paused half a second longer after he spoke.

Another week, and she gave the barest reply: "You always say that."

He chuckled. "Habit."

There was a cadence to it. Not warmth, exactly, but a rhythm, like the predictable crackle of lava before it cooled into something solid. That was enough for him, too.

Louis had once told his brother that kindness was like chiseling stone—you didn’t expect it to crack right away, but every strike mattered. He just hadn’t expected to be chiseling someone like her.

Then came the day of the promotion. Word spread down the line: The Reaper had completed an unsanctioned assault behind Twili lines, cut through three Huskmasters, and brought back a relic. High Command had responded with a new name.

Reaper of Shadows.

The barracks buzzed. Even the veterans grew quiet when she returned. But Louis was already waiting by the edge of the eastern wall, like always.

He watched her approach—cloak heavier now, the air colder around her than the fog—and swallowed his nerves.

"Well done, Reaper of Shadows," he said, tipping an imaginary hat.

She froze.

Then, to his surprise, turned.

There was a heat in her eyes that had nothing to do with fury, and everything to do with desperation. When she spoke, it was like stone cracking open to reveal crystal.

"They can call me the Reaper of Shadows. But you... please. Just call me Alice."

He blinked, ears twitching. "Alice?"

She nodded, then turned away before he could say more. But he had seen the truth. The name was her armor—and she had just lowered it.

He whispered to the empty air behind her, "Alright then, Alice. I will."

The other guards teased him later, of course. "Getting cozy with the shadow?" they said. "Careful you don’t wake up as rubble."

Louis didn’t answer, he just smiled. He was starting to believe the rumors were wrong, maybe the Reaper still had a heart, and maybe, just maybe, he was the one she'd trusted to see it.

## Chapter Three: The Fog Turns Red

The first sign was the silence.

Z-73 was never loud, but it always had noise—the wind in the chimneys, the clang of boots on stone, the idle muttering of guards bored out of their minds. That morning, though, Louis woke to a quiet so complete it felt like a held breath.

Then came the horns. Not the fort’s—those were shattered in the first barrage.

It was the Twili.

Louis scrambled from his bunk and pulled on his armor with hands that shook only a little. He grabbed his pike, tucked his family crest into his belt, and joined the flood of soldiers rushing toward the walls.

Husks. Dozens of them, climbing over the rocks like spiders. And behind them, the Huskmasters—tall, crowned in antlered shadows, eyes glowing with Erosia’s cold fury.

Alice was gone. She’d left the night before for another raid. She hadn’t said goodbye.

The first wave hit hard: screams, stone shattered. One of the side towers fell within the first hour. Louis fought until his arms ached, until the fog was no longer grey but red.

He saw Relle get pulled over the parapet. He saw the med tent go up in fire. He saw a Husk rush him with a blade of glass.

Then, he didn’t see.

A weight collapsed onto him. A noise like thunder. Pain.

He was pinned beneath rubble, the air gone from his lungs, his vision tunneling. The Husk raised its weapon again.

And then—

A scream.

Not of pain, but of power.

A shard of stone erupted from the ground, piercing the Husk clean through. It twitched once, then fell.

She landed beside him in a blur.

"Don’t worry," she said, voice fierce and breaking all at once. "I’ll protect you."

He tried to speak. Couldn’t.

She turned her back to him and fought. And fought. And didn’t fall.

He closed his eyes, not out of fear—but because, for once, he knew someone would keep watch.

And her name wasn’t Reaper. It was Alice.

## Chapter Four: The Stone That Stirs

Louis awoke to the sound of dripping water.

He blinked, vision blurry. The ceiling above him was made of woven quartz slats—softly glowing with the pulse of metabold enchantment. The air smelled of moss paste, clean linen, and the distant, ever-present scent of scorched stone.

The infirmary.

His body ached. Bandages wrapped his chest and leg, thick with herbal balm. He tried to sit up and failed, collapsing back with a grunt.

A voice—familiar, fierce, terrified—cut through the haze.

"Louis!"

He turned his head, slowly. There she was.

Alice.

Not armored. Not cloaked. She wore a simple tunic, sleeveless and smudged with ash. Her eyes were wide, glowing faintly with geomantic stress. She looked as if she hadn’t slept.

He smiled, even as pain throbbed behind his ribs. "You... you stayed."

She moved to his side, gripping his hand. "Of course I stayed."

He exhaled a laugh. "Thought you were a shadow. Didn’t think shadows worried about wounded guards."

"I’m not a shadow," she said, and her voice cracked. "Not with you."

His eyes narrowed, playful even through the grogginess. "So what am I, then? Some tea-pouring fort rat who wormed his way into your stony heart?"

"You..." She hesitated. Her grip tightened. "You reminded me I had a heart at all."

That silenced him. Not because he didn’t believe it—but because he did. He saw her as no one else did, and now she was here, real and open and afraid.

He brought her hand to his cheek. "You saved me."

She nodded. "And I would again. A thousand times."

They stayed like that for a long while. Not speaking. Just breathing, tethered by the fragile thread of survival, and something deeper—something unspoken but certain.

Then Louis said, voice soft and teasing, "Next time, maybe let me do the saving. Just once."

Alice smiled.

It was the first time he’d ever seen her truly smile.

## Chapter Five: Shackled in Shadow

The night was quiet. Too quiet.

Louis stirred restlessly in his infirmary bed. Alice had left only moments before, coaxed out by a medic who reminded her she needed food, or rest, or both. She had looked reluctant, like she feared the moment her back was turned, the world would undo itself again.

And maybe it would.

The last thing Louis saw was a flicker of movement in the corridor—something darker than the torchlight should allow. Then came the cold.

He woke to stone.

Smooth, pitch-black walls surrounded him—walls that hummed with something unnatural. He reached for his side, for his badge, his medallion, even his belt. Nothing. His arms trembled. Not from fear. From the absence. No magic answered his call.

Shadowstone.

He'd heard of it in hushed rumors—stone that nullified metabold power, forged in the depths of Twili sanctums. It was a prison, not just for body, but for soul.

Days passed. Or maybe hours. He couldn't tell. Food came twice—water and coarse bread. No words. Just a slotted stone that clicked open and shut.

He tried to remember his parents' voices. His brother’s laugh. Alice’s smile.

Mostly, he remembered the way she'd said his name.

Then, on the fourth day, the humming changed.

A tremor.

Another.

Then came the sound of battle. Shouts. Crashes. The deep grind of geomantic force. Someone was coming.

No, not someone.

Her.

The far wall exploded inwards, scattering shards of shadowstone like brittle glass. And there she was, silhouetted in the debris, cloak torn, armor scorched, eyes burning like twin fault lines.

Alice.

She rushed to him, falling to her knees, breaking the shackles with a single strike of her stone-wrapped fist.

"Louis," she breathed, voice trembling. "Are you alright?"

He coughed, laughed, then reached out to steady her. "Took you long enough."

"I had to carve through a mountain."

He pulled her into a weak hug, pressing his forehead to hers. "Worth it."

Then she drew back, breath shallow. "I almost lost you."

He blinked. "You didn’t."

She hesitated. Then said it—soft and raw. "Marry me."

Louis blinked. Thought maybe the prison had broken his ears.

"What?"

"Marry me," she repeated. "Not after the war. Not someday. Now. Or as close to now as we can make it."

He stared, then chuckled, then nodded. "You’re insane. But yes."

She smiled. And in that ruined prison, amid rubble and silence, Louis felt more free than he ever had before.

They fled through the breach hand-in-hand, shadows collapsing behind them. The world was still at war.

But they weren’t alone anymore.

## Chapter Six: A Home of Stone and Shadow

The return to Z-73 was quiet. Not the grim quiet of aftermath, but the kind that follows healing rain—the silence of soil before something grows.

The fort had changed. Its walls bore fresh scars, its towers rebuilt with haste and heart. Soldiers nodded as Alice and Louis passed, no longer with fear, but with something resembling reverence—and curiosity. The legend had returned, but she walked slower now. And she held someone's hand.

Louis limped slightly but smiled through it. He refused a stretcher. "If I’m going to walk into my new life," he’d said, "I’m doing it on my feet."

They held a small ceremony two days later.

No fanfare. No royal seal. Just a half-broken courtyard, a priest from the engineers’ division, and half the garrison crowding around in borrowed uniforms and mismatched sashes. Someone strung up a banner made from salvaged tent canvas. It read, in shaky charcoal script: "STONE LOVES SHADOW."

Alice wore no cloak. She stood with her stone-scaled arms bare, eyes bright but calm. Louis wore a repolished officer’s jacket someone had found in storage. It didn’t fit.

When asked if they had vows, Louis took her hands.

"I was forged for peace, not war. You were shaped to destroy, not feel. But here we are. In between the cracks, something bloomed. You are my strength, Alice. And I promise to remind you, every day, that you are more than what they called you."

Alice blinked quickly. Then:

"I was told love makes one weak. But you taught me it makes us more whole. You saw me when I didn’t want to be seen. You spoke when everyone else stayed silent. You carved my name back into me. I am yours, Louis. Stone, shadow, and soul."

The garrison cheered. Someone fired a celebratory volley too early. Alice didn't even flinch. Louis laughed so hard he had to sit down.

Later that night, they stood at the ramparts together. The stars were out. The fog, for once, stayed away.

Louis leaned on the stone and said, "You think they’ll let us keep this place?"

Alice smiled faintly. "If not, we’ll build something new. Somewhere. Anywhere. As long as it’s ours."

He reached for her hand again.

She squeezed it.

And together, they watched the horizon.

Not for threats.

But for sun.

Reaper’s Return

## Prelude: The Reaper Comes Home

The sea between Hurley and Korland was black as polished obsidian.

Alice stood alone at the prow of the imperial vessel, her cloak snapping behind her in the night wind. The ship, The Stonewake, cut through the waves with the confidence of Kobold craft: basalt-ribbed, rune-driven, and steady even beneath a moonless sky. Deep below deck, geomantic engines pulsed with the slow heartbeat of earth-magic, calling her home.

Home.

The word felt heavier than her armor.

Behind her, Hurley had finally gone quiet. Not peaceful—not truly. Peace was too clean a word for what remained after the Great Hurlen War. But stable. Roads secured. Governors installed. Rebellions broken or folded into treaties. The continent no longer bled openly, and so the Empire had released its sharpest blade.

The Reaper was returning to Korland.

But Alice was returning to Louis, and to the child.

Her hand drifted to her abdomen, though there was nothing there to hold. Kobold children were not carried in the body, but in the earth. Months ago, before Louis returned home, they had formed the core together: a small living stone, shaped from their joined essence, warm with their love and trembling with the first mystery of life. Louis had taken it home and buried it in consecrated soil beneath the family shrine, where the mana of Korland could gather around it, feed it, and slowly shape the child within. By now, the child is almost fully formed. Almost ready to rise from the earth.

Alice closed her eyes. She had missed the first stirrings. The first signs. The gradual shaping beneath the soil. Louis had called when he could: the glow had strengthened; the stone-bed had warmed; the midwives of the Kedgelony had heard movement beneath the ground. Movement. Her child had moved, and she had been across the sea pacifying a wounded continent.

A voice behind her broke through the wind, “Alice?” She turned. The young officer froze, as though surprised she had allowed her name to be spoken, “We will sight Korland by dawn,” he said.

Alice nodded once, “Tell the captain to make full speed.”

“Yes, Ki—” He stopped himself. “Yes, Alice.”

When he left, she looked back toward the horizon. Somewhere beyond the dark, Louis was waiting. Somewhere beneath Korland’s sacred stone, her child was nearly ready to enter the world. Alice, called the Reaper by empires and enemies alike, gripped the railing until the stone creaked beneath her claws. For the first time in years, she was not afraid of death. She was afraid of being late.

## Chapter One: Familiar Land, Different Shape

The first light of dawn touched Korland as the ship entered the harbor. Alice stood at the gangplank before it was fully lowered, cloak drawn tight, eyes fixed on the dock below.

Louis was there. He looked older than when he had left. Tired, thinner, with dust on his boots and the same gentle smile she had carried through every battlefield on Hurley. “Alice,” he said.

She descended before ceremony could catch her. No salute. No greeting to the officers. She stepped onto the dock and into his arms. For one moment, the Reaper vanished.

Louis held her tightly. “You made it.”

“I told you I would.”

“You say many frightening things with too much confidence.”

She pulled back just enough to look at him. “The child?”

His smile trembled. “Close. Very close. The midwives sent for me before dawn. I came only because I knew your ship was arriving.”

Alice’s breath caught. “Then why are we still standing here?”

Louis laughed softly and took her hand. “Carriage is waiting.”

They did not wait for the luggage. The carriage rolled hard through Korland’s lower streets, wheels striking sparks from the volcanic stone. Alice sat beside Louis, still wearing her travel-stained armor. Outside, the city rose in black terraces and crystal-lit arches, familiar and strange all at once.

Louis kept hold of her hand, “We still have not chosen a name,” he said.

Alice looked at him sharply. “You were supposed to narrow the list.”

“I did. To seventeen.”

“That is not narrowing.”

“It was thirty-four.”

Despite herself, she smiled.

Louis leaned back, thoughtful. “If a boy, I liked Marrec.”

“Too soft.”

“Veyron?”

“Too proud.”

“Alaric?”

“Too imperial.”

Louis gave her a look. “We are Kobolds.”

“We can still show restraint.”

He chuckled. “And for a girl?”

Alice looked out the window. “I thought of Liora.”

Louis grew quiet. He knew the name. He knew enough, “That is beautiful,” he said gently.

“It is heavy.”

“Most worthy names are.”

Alice swallowed and looked down at their joined hands. “I also thought of Maria.”

Louis nodded. “For your mother.”

“For the woman who saved me before I knew I needed saving.”

The carriage slowed as it climbed toward the family shrine. The city noise faded behind them, replaced by the low hum of sacred stones and the distant chanting of the Kedgelony attendants.

Louis whispered, “Perhaps we will know.”

Alice gave a quiet, doubtful breath. “That sounds like something Kael would say.”

“Then perhaps Kael was right.”

The carriage stopped and they stepped out together. The shrine was small by Korland’s standards, carved into the living rock of a hillside overlooking the harbor. At its center lay the birthing bed: a circle of dark, consecrated soil veined with faint turquoise light. The ground pulsed gently.

Alice froze, The soil moved. A crack opened in the center, not violent, but tender, like the earth itself was unfolding its hands. Warm light spilled upward. The midwives stepped back, heads bowed. Louis gripped Alice’s hand.

From the sacred earth emerged a tiny Kobold child, stone-soft and glimmering, with Alice’s shadowed eyes and Louis’s gentle brow. The child opened her mouth, took her first breath, and let out a small, indignant cry.

Alice fell to her knees. Louis knelt beside her. For a moment, neither spoke. Then the child opened her eyes. They shone like dawn striking crystal after a long night. Alice and Louis looked at one another, and the name came to them at once, not chosen, but given.

“Aurora,” Alice whispered.

Louis smiled through tears. “Aurora.”

Alice reached forward with trembling hands as the midwife placed the child in her arms. The Reaper held her daughter against her chest, and for the first time in many years, Alice did not feel like something made to end the world. She felt like something had begun.

## Chapter Two: The First Two Months

Alice had faced armies with steadier hands than the first time Aurora cried through the night.

“She is not wounded,” Louis murmured, half-asleep beside her. “She is hungry.”

Alice stood over the cradle-stone, rigid as a guard at execution. “She sounds wounded.”

“She sounds newborn.”

“That is a design flaw.”

Louis laughed into his pillow.

The first weeks passed in fragments: feeding, rocking, washing soft stone-skin with warmed mineral water, learning which hums calmed Aurora and which made her scream louder. Louis took to fatherhood like he had been quietly preparing for it all his life. He sang badly. He smiled constantly. He spoke to Aurora as if she were a visiting dignitary.

Alice, however, moved like she was disarming a bomb. She measured every touch. She watched every breath. When Aurora slept too long, Alice feared death. When Aurora woke too quickly, Alice feared pain. When Aurora gripped her claw with one impossibly tiny hand, Alice froze completely.

Louis found her that way one afternoon, kneeling by the cradle, unmoving, “She has you trapped?” he asked.

Alice whispered, “I do not want to pull away.”

“Then don’t.”

“She is very small.”

“Yes.”

“I have broken larger things by accident.”

Louis knelt beside her. “You are not breaking her.”

Alice looked down at Aurora, whose little ears twitched in sleep. “I do not know how to be soft.”

Louis rested his chin on her shoulder. “You are learning.”

The two months of leave seemed generous when granted. By the third week, it felt like a cruel joke. Days vanished. Aurora smiled for the first time while Louis was making tea. Alice missed it and nearly interrogated him for details, “She smiled,” he said.

“How?”

“With her mouth.”

“Louis.”

“With joy, I assume.”

Alice glared until he demonstrated poorly.

By the fifth week, Alice had learned the difference between Aurora’s hungry cry, tired cry, angry cry, and the mysterious cry that meant nothing except that existence had offended her. By the sixth, she could swaddle her daughter one-handed. By the seventh, Aurora would fall asleep only against Alice’s chest, tucked beneath the black cloak once feared across continents.

Louis found them there often: Alice seated by the shrine-window, armor set aside, cloak wrapped around both mother and child.

“You know,” he said once, “that cloak used to terrify officers.”

Alice looked down at the sleeping child. “It has found better employment.”

On the final night of leave, Alice did not sleep. She sat beside Aurora’s cradle-stone, listening to the soft rhythm of her breathing. Tomorrow, reports would come. Orders would come. The Empire always remembered its blades.

Louis entered quietly and placed a cup of stoneflower tea beside her, “You are afraid,” he stated.

“Yes.”

“Of leaving her?”

Alice nodded.

“And of returning to work?”

Another nod.

Louis sat beside her. “Because you think the Reaper will come back instead of Alice.”

Her jaw tightened.

He reached for her hand. “The Reaper was never all of you.”

“She was enough of me.”

“Yes,” Louis said softly. “But so is this.”

Aurora stirred, opened her bright dawn-crystal eyes, and fussed. Alice lifted her carefully, no longer stiff, no longer uncertain. She held her close, humming low in her throat, an old Kobold cradle-tone she had not known she remembered.

Aurora quieted.

Louis smiled.

Alice looked down at her daughter and felt something in herself yield: not break, not weaken, but open.

“I can do this,” she whispered.

Louis leaned against her. “Yes, Alice. You can.”

Outside, Korland’s mountain-heart pulsed beneath them. Inside, the Reaper rocked her child to sleep and, at last, became only a mother.

## Chapter Three: The Herald

Morning came too quickly. Aurora had only just settled after dawn, curled in the cradle-stone with one tiny hand tucked beneath her chin, when the knock came. Alice opened the door expecting an officer; instead, an imperial herald stood on the threshold.

He wore cobalt and gold, with the seal of the throne hanging from his neck. He bowed deeply, “Alice of the Black Vein,” he said, voice formal. “By order of Her Imperial Majesty, Empress Joan, granddaughter of Vercingetorix the founder, daughter of Chione of Kor’s Bane, Protector of the Lanyarui, sovereign of Korland, the Empire of Kobolds, and ruler of half the known world, you are commanded to appear before the throne today.”

Behind Alice, Louis went very still. Alice looked back once at Aurora. Then she said, “I will come.”

She dressed slowly. Not in the cloak of the Reaper, not at first. She put on her black formal armor, polished smooth and veined with silver. Louis fastened the clasps at her shoulders while Aurora slept.

“You think this is punishment?” he asked.

“No,” Alice said. “If it were punishment, they would have sent soldiers.”

“That is not comforting.”

“It was not meant to be.”

Louis finished the clasp, then rested his hands briefly on her shoulders. “Come back.”

Alice turned to him. “Always.”

The palace of Korland rose from the mountain like a crown grown from the earth itself. Its halls were vast, black-stone corridors lit by blue crystal and guarded by soldiers who straightened as Alice passed. Some saluted. Some stared. All knew the name they did not speak. At last, she was shown into the throne chamber.

Empress Joan sat beneath the great basalt arch of Rokesh, robed in imperial blue, her crown gleaming like frozen lightning. She was older now, but her eyes were sharp with the terrible inheritance of her blood: Chione’s stillness, Vercingetorix’s command. Alice knelt.

“Rise,” Joan said.

Alice obeyed.

The Empress studied her for a long moment, “You stabilized Hurley faster than my council believed possible,” Joan said. “You broke three insurgencies without open massacre. You turned enemy commanders into administrators. You ended what others would have prolonged for glory.” Alice said nothing. Joan leaned slightly forward. “You are very good at being feared.”

Alice’s jaw tightened. “Yes, Majesty.”

“And how is motherhood?”

The question struck harder than any accusation. Alice blinked. “Majesty?”

“Your daughter. Aurora, yes?”

Alice’s composure nearly cracked. “Yes.”

“How is she?”

Alice hesitated. “Small.”

Joan’s mouth curved faintly. “Most infants are.”

“She cries as if wronged by creation. She sleeps only when inconvenient. She grips my hand and refuses to release it.”

“And you?”

Alice looked down. “I am learning not to treat tenderness like a battlefield.”

For the first time, Joan smiled, “Good,” the Empress said. “Then you are ready.”

Alice looked up. Joan stood, and every guard in the chamber stiffened, “I did not summon you to send you back into shadows,” she said. “You have served long enough as a knife in dark rooms. From this day forward, you shall serve in the light.” Alice’s breath stilled. “You are hereby appointed personal guard to the Imperial Empress. You will remain in Korland. You will answer directly to me. No marshal, no intelligence office, no battlefield commander may command you without my seal.”

Silence filled the chamber. Alice understood at once; It was not merely an honor; it was protection; for her, for Louis, for Aurora.

Joan descended the steps of the throne and stopped before her, “The Empire has taken much from you,” the Empress said quietly. “It will not take your daughter’s first years as well.”

Alice knelt again, but this time her voice was not cold, “Majesty,” she said, “I will guard you with my life.”

Joan placed a hand on her shoulder, “I know,” she said. “That is why I chose you.”

## Chapter Four: The Courtyard of Stones

“Follow me,” Joan said.

Alice obeyed. They passed through three guarded halls, down a stair of polished basalt, and into a vast open courtyard carved into the mountain itself. No banners hung there. No courtiers waited. Only stone. Boulders covered the ground: some no larger than a helm, others as large as wagons.

Joan stepped into the center. “Tell me, Alice. What is the best way to learn about someone?”

Alice frowned. “Interrogation?”

Joan smiled. A boulder shot across the courtyard. Alice barely raised her hand in time. The stone shattered against a wall of rock she pulled from the ground.

Joan’s eyes gleamed. “Let’s see if you are as strong as they say.”

Alice stared. Then the courtyard erupted. Alice answered first with restraint: a sharp slab of stone, fast but controlled. Joan deflected it with a lazy flick of her wrist. Another came. Then another. Soon, the boulders were no longer obstacles, but ammunition, rising and flying through the air like cannon fire.

Alice felt the old instincts return: measure, counter, strike. But Joan was faster than expected. Much faster.

The Empress moved with almost effortless command, shifting stone as though the mountain were an extension of her own breath. A pillar rose beneath her feet. Alice split it in half. Joan laughed and sent the halves spinning toward her.

“Careful,” Joan called. “My council said you were terrifying. I would hate to find out they exaggerated.”

Alice caught one stone midair, crushed it into shards, and sent them back like a storm of knives.

Joan swept her hand. The shards turned to dust. “Better.”

Alice’s eyes narrowed. Now she stopped holding back. The courtyard shook. Boulders cracked. Stone walls rose and fell. Alice drove a wave of jagged rock across the floor, forcing Joan backward for the first time.

The Empress smiled wider, “There she is.” Joan slammed her heel into the ground.

The entire courtyard answered. Alice felt the movement beneath her too late. The earth opened, tilted, and folded beneath her feet. Her balance broke. Before she could recover, a ring of stone snapped upward around her, pinning her arms gently but completely. Alice froze.

Joan stood before her, unruffled.

“I yield,” Alice said, stunned.

Joan released her at once.

Alice stepped free, brushing dust from her armor. “You do not need a bodyguard.”

“No,” Joan said. “Not particularly.”

Alice looked at her, confused.

Joan walked to a cracked boulder and rested one hand on it. “My grandfather had Napoleon. Not merely a subordinate. Not merely a soldier. Someone who could challenge him. Contradict him. Sharpen him.” She turned back. “I have flatterers, ministers, generals, priests, spies, and terrified servants. I have very few equals.” Alice was silent. “I appointed you because I want someone near me who will not tremble every time I speak,” Joan said. “Someone strong enough to test me. Honest enough to oppose me. Dangerous enough that others remember I still choose restraint.”

Alice swallowed. “And Aurora?”

Joan’s expression softened, “I also want your daughter to grow up with her mother,” she said. “The Empire has enough orphans shaped by duty. It does not need another.”

Alice looked away. For a moment, the courtyard was very quiet.

Then Joan added, dryly, “Besides, you will need the practice. I expect you to last longer next time.”

Alice looked back sharply. The Empress smiled. And despite herself, Alice smiled too.

## Chapter Five: The Shape of a New Duty

Alice’s first day as personal guard to the Empress began with dust in her armor. After the bout in the courtyard, Joan had dismissed the attendants with a casual wave and led Alice back through the palace as if nothing unusual had happened. Alice followed two paces behind, still processing the fact that the ruler of half the known world had just buried her in a ring of stone like an overconfident academy student.

Joan did not look back, but Alice suspected she knew exactly what was happening in her mind, “You are irritated,” the Empress said.

Alice straightened. “No, Majesty.”

“You are.”

“I am surprised.”

“That is a more dignified word for irritated.” Alice said nothing. Joan smiled faintly. “Good. I prefer you irritated. You become too polite when you are uncertain.”

They entered a smaller audience chamber lined with blue crystal lamps and dark velvet banners. This was not the great throne room, but a working chamber: desks, maps, sealed dispatches, military markers, and trays of petitions. Ministers were already gathered there, speaking in low tones until the Empress entered.

Then they bowed. Alice moved instinctively to the side of the chamber, scanning exits, corners, windows, concealed alcoves, and every hand near a sleeve. It was habit. She knew which ministers feared Joan, which resented her, which merely feared being useless. Fear had a scent. Ambition had a posture.

Joan noticed, “Stand beside me,” she said. Alice obeyed, taking her place at Joan’s right.The ministers noticed too.That was the point.

The morning passed in reports. Hurley’s grain roads. A Lanyarui border dispute. A petition from the Kedgelony regarding old cult sites. A naval request for more basalt-keel frigates. Joan listened with patience sharper than anger. She asked few questions, but each one cut directly to the weakness in the report. Alice said nothing.

That is, until a military secretary mentioned Hurley, “The southern governors request authorization to increase punitive patrols,” he said. “There have been whispers of renewed insurgent organization near the old Alliance forts.”

Alice felt Joan glance toward her. “Speak,” Joan said. The room froze.

Alice looked at the secretary. “Whispers from whom?”

He blinked. “Local informants.”

“Paid?”

“Yes.”

“Then they are telling you what keeps them paid.” A few ministers shifted. Alice continued, voice calm. “If insurgents are gathering near the old forts, they want patrols increased. They want visible imperial force. That lets them measure response times, supply routes, officer discipline, and local sympathy. If you send more soldiers, you teach them how to fight you.”

The secretary flushed. “Then what would you recommend?”

“Send road engineers. Repair wells. Reopen the market route. Assign one quiet intelligence cell. If rebels are there, they will either expose themselves trying to disrupt it or lose the locals when life improves without them.”

Joan did not smile, but Alice felt her approval, “Do that,” the Empress said.

By midday, the ministers had stopped looking at Alice as decoration. By evening, they had stopped looking at her as merely a weapon. When the final council withdrew, Joan remained at the map table. For a while, she said nothing. Alice waited.

At last, Joan spoke. “Do you see now?”

Alice looked at her. “Majesty?”

“I do not need another shield. I need someone who has seen what my polished advisers discuss only on parchment.”

Alice considered that. “You also need someone they are afraid to lie in front of.”

Joan’s mouth curved. “That too.” A silence settled between them. Not empty. Testing. Then Joan said, “Tell me honestly. Hurley. Can it hold?”

Alice looked down at the map. She saw roads she had secured, forts she had emptied, towns she had spared when others demanded examples. She saw graves too. Many graves. “Yes,” Alice said. “But not if we treat silence as loyalty.”

Joan nodded slowly. “And Nuea? The colonies?”

That question was more dangerous.

Alice took her time. “The people of Nuea are proud and diverse. Proud things crack before they bend.”

Joan’s gaze sharpened. Alice did not look away. Then the Empress gave a small laugh. “Good. You will do.”

When Alice returned home, Aurora was crying. Not a gentle cry. Not a dignified cry. A furious, offended, world-condemning cry.

Louis stood in the middle of the room with their daughter against his shoulder, swaying with the grim determination of a soldier defending a doomed gate, “She has rejected sleep,” he said.

Alice removed her cloak. “On what grounds?”

“Unclear. She refuses to negotiate.”

Alice crossed the room and took Aurora into her arms. The child squirmed, fussed, then slowly quieted when Alice tucked her beneath her chin.

Louis stared, “That is unfair.”

Alice looked down at Aurora, whose tiny hands gripped at the edge of her collar. “She respects authority.”

“She respects your terrifying heartbeat.”

Alice almost smiled.

Louis touched the dust still clinging to her sleeve. “First day?”

“I fought the Empress.” He blinked. “She won.”

Louis blinked again. “I need you to begin that story earlier.” So she told him.

She told him about the throne room, Joan’s offer, the courtyard, the boulders, the ministers, the Hurley report. Louis listened while preparing tea, occasionally glancing at Aurora as though making sure their daughter understood the importance of the moment.

When Alice finished, he sat beside her, “So you are not being sent away,” he said.

“No.”

“And you answer only to Joan?”

“Yes.”

“And this keeps you in Korland?”

Alice looked down at Aurora. “Yes.”

Louis exhaled, long and quiet. Then he leaned his forehead against her shoulder.

“Thank Rokesh,” he whispered.

Alice closed her eyes, “I thought you would be pleased,” she said.

“I am.” His voice was rough. “I am trying not to collapse.”

“You may collapse after taking the child.” He laughed softly and did so.

The days that followed were not easy, but they were possible. In the mornings, Alice left before the second bell. Louis would stand in the doorway with Aurora wrapped against his chest, waving one of the baby’s hands at her.

“Say goodbye to your mother, little dawn.”

Aurora usually sneezed.

At the palace, Alice stood beside Joan through councils, petitions, strategy sessions, and private audiences. Sometimes she spoke. More often she listened. Joan tested her constantly, “What would you do with a governor who obeys too quickly?”

“Watch his treasurer.”

“What is the difference between rebellion and grief?”

“Leadership.”

“What should an empress fear most?”

Alice had answered that one after a long pause, “Being obeyed by cowards.”

Joan had gone quiet for nearly a minute, then she simply said, “Yes.”

In the afternoons, when court business thinned, they trained in the courtyard of stones. Joan still won more often than not, though not as easily. Alice learned that the Empress fought like a mountain thinking several centuries ahead. Joan learned that Alice fought like a knife that had discovered roots.

“You hesitate when you could finish,” Joan said after one bout.

Alice wiped blood from her lip. “You told me you wanted a challenge, not an assassination.”

Joan laughed. “That is the correct distinction.”

At home, the lessons were different.

Aurora taught Alice that strength could be useless at three in the morning. That no imperial technique could force an infant to sleep. That a child could spit up on ceremonial armor without fear of consequences. That tenderness was not a feeling but a discipline, practiced in repetition.

Some evenings, Alice came home with her mind still full of maps and threats. She would reach for Aurora too sharply, speak too coldly, stand too rigidly.

Louis would notice, “Take off the armor first,” he would say. At first, she thought he meant the metal. Eventually, she understood. So she began a ritual.

At the threshold of their home, Alice removed her cloak. Then her weapons. Then her gloves. Last of all, she would pause, breathe, and let the palace fall away. Only then would she enter. Only then would she take Aurora.

One night, after a particularly brutal council concerning executions in Hurley, Alice stood outside the door for a long time. Louis opened it quietly, “Alice?”

“I recommended mercy today,” she said. Louis waited. “They accepted it because they were afraid of what I would recommend otherwise.”

“That still counts.”

She looked at him. “Does it?”

He stepped outside and took her hand. “Yes. Sometimes Kallisto uses strange roads to bring people home.”

Alice looked through the open door. Aurora slept inside, one tiny foot uncovered, her face peaceful and utterly unconcerned with empires, “I do not know how to be both,” Alice whispered.

Louis squeezed her hand. “You are not two people. You are one person learning which parts must rule.”

The next day, Joan noticed the change, “You are calmer,” the Empress said as they walked toward the council chamber.

“My husband gave me advice.”

“Was it good?”

“Annoyingly.”

Joan nodded. “The best kind.”

Weeks passed, and the rhythm took shape. Alice became the shadow at Joan’s side, but not the old shadow. She was no longer sent ahead to silence rooms before the Empress entered. Instead, she remained visible, steady, watchful. Ministers learned that Joan might ask Alice’s opinion at any moment. Generals learned to prepare better. Petitioners learned that the Reaper did not always argue for death. And Joan, slowly, began to speak to her not as a weapon, nor even as a servant, but as someone who could bear truth.

One evening, after the council had emptied and the lamps had dimmed, Joan stood before the great window overlooking Korland, “My grandfather had Napoleon,” she said. “My mother had too many ghosts. I have inherited both.”

Alice stood beside her. “That is a crowded inheritance.”

Joan smiled faintly. “Yes. I sometimes envy my brother, who merely administers our mother’s city. Much less paperwork.”

Below them, the city glowed with veins of turquoise light. Somewhere far below, Louis was likely trying to convince Aurora that mashed root was not poison.

“I envy you,” Joan said.

Alice looked at her, startled, “Majesty?”

“You go home to something that does not care what title you bear.”

Alice’s expression softened. “Aurora cares very deeply about whether I am holding her correctly.”

“That may be healthier.”

Alice considered the Empress, the crown, the loneliness hidden beneath all that imperial stone, “You may visit,” Alice said.

Joan turned. Alice immediately wondered if she had overstepped. But Joan only raised an eyebrow. “Are you inviting the Empress to meet your infant?”

“I am inviting Joan to meet Aurora.”

For a moment, the ruler of half the known world said nothing. Then, quietly, “I would like that.”

That night, Alice came home later than usual. Louis was half-asleep in the chair, Aurora curled against him. The room was warm, lamplit, ordinary. Alice stood there for a moment, watching them. Then Louis opened one eye. “Should I be worried?”

“The Empress may come to dinner.”

Louis closed his eye again, sighing, “Of course she may.”

“You are not alarmed?”

“I married the Reaper. My sense of alarm has adapted.”

Alice smiled. Aurora stirred, making a small, questioning sound. Alice crossed the room and lifted her daughter into her arms. This time, there was no hesitation. No stiffness. No fear of breaking her.

Aurora settled against her. Alice kissed the top of her head. In the palace, she would stand beside an Empress. In this room, she would kneel beside a cradle. Both duties were real. Both demanded her whole heart. And for the first time, Alice began to believe that perhaps she did not have to choose between being dangerous and being gentle. Perhaps the same hands that could move stone could also hold a child. Perhaps the same woman who had once been called Reaper could become confidant, guardian, wife, and mother: not easily, not perfectly, but truly.

## Chapter Six: Dinner with the Crown

When Empress Joan came to dinner, she did not come alone. Alice had expected guards, attendants, perhaps one palace secretary pretending not to record everything. Instead, Joan arrived at the door in a simple blue cloak with only two companions beside her: her husband and her son.

The Prince-Consort was tall, sharp-faced, and dressed with deliberate restraint. His name was Étienne de Montcrag, though most of Korland knew him only as the unseen husband of the Empress. He bowed to Alice with flawless form and no warmth.

Beside him stood Crown Prince Matthieu, heir apparent of the Empire, young, polished, and already carrying himself as if the throne were a chair he had graciously agreed to wait for.

Joan smiled. “Alice. Thank you for receiving us.”

Alice bowed. “Majesty.”

Louis appeared behind her with Aurora in his arms. “Welcome to our home.”

Joan’s expression softened at once. “And this must be Aurora.” Aurora stared at the Empress, blinked, and grabbed at the air as if evaluating whether Joan’s crown was removable.

Louis smiled. “She has diplomatic instincts.” Joan laughed. Étienne did not.

Matthieu glanced around the room, taking in its modest furnishings, the simple hearth, the shrine alcove, the cradle-stone. “This is smaller than I expected.”

Alice’s eyes moved to him. Louis, mercifully, answered first. “Children are small. We thought we would start there.” Joan’s mouth twitched. Matthieu did not seem to understand he had been teased.

Dinner began carefully. Louis had prepared roasted root, riverfish, mineral bread, and a small dish of softened crystal-fruit for Aurora, who rejected it with imperial confidence. Joan accepted everything graciously and asked questions about the house, the shrine, the child, and Louis’s work before fatherhood.

Étienne answered only when spoken to. Matthieu spoke too often.

“So,” the prince said, turning toward Alice, “you truly were IKSS?” The table went still.

Joan’s eyes sharpened. “Matthieu.”

Alice set down her cup. “Yes.”

“And the stories are true?”

“Some.”

“Which ones?”

“The useful ones.”

Matthieu leaned back, intrigued in the way young nobles were often intrigued by blood they had never had to spill themselves. “Father says the Empire should be cautious about honoring knives too publicly. They cut whichever hand holds them.”

Étienne’s gaze remained on his plate. “I said tools must be kept in proper place.”

Louis’s hand tightened slightly around his fork. Alice noticed. Joan noticed too, “Alice’s proper place,” the Empress said evenly, “is at my side.”

Étienne lifted his eyes. “By your command, yes.”

“Not merely command.” That silence was colder.

Aurora chose that moment to slap both hands into her food. Louis looked down. “Thank you, little dawn. An excellent diversion.” Joan smiled again, but the warmth did not fully return.

For a while, conversation moved to safer subjects: trade, weather, the palace gardens, the difficulty of convincing infants to sleep. Joan asked Louis questions with genuine interest. He answered with humor and care, and Alice found herself grateful beyond words.

But Matthieu circled back, “Does it trouble you?” he asked Alice.

“What?”

“That my mother trusts you so much.”

Alice studied him. “It appears to trouble you.”

The prince flushed. “You are feared. That is not the same as being loyal.”

“No,” Alice said. “It is not.”

“Then why should we believe you are?”

Louis looked ready to speak, but Alice gently touched his wrist. She turned fully toward the prince, “You should not believe it because I say so,” she said. “You should watch, measure, test. Loyalty that fears examination is performance.”

Matthieu had no answer. Joan leaned back, satisfied. Étienne, however, gave a thin smile. “A polished response.”

Alice met his gaze. “A practiced one.”

“From interrogation?”

“From regret.” That finally silenced him.

Joan looked at Alice for a long moment, not as Empress now, but as the woman who had invited herself into this fragile home because she wanted something ordinary and perhaps did not know how to ask for it.

Then Aurora began to fuss.

Without thinking, Alice rose, lifted her daughter, and settled her against her shoulder. The motion was smooth now. Natural. Aurora quieted, one small hand gripping the edge of Alice’s tunic. Joan watched with something like envy. Matthieu watched with confusion. Étienne watched with suspicion. Alice felt all three.

“She knows you,” Joan said softly.

Alice looked down at Aurora. “I am trying to deserve that.” No one spoke for a while.

After dinner, Louis served stoneflower tea. Joan accepted hers beside the hearth. Étienne remained standing. Matthieu wandered too close to the mantle where Alice’s old black cloak hung folded over a chair, “Is this it?” he asked. “The Reaper’s cloak?”

Alice’s voice cut across the room. “Do not touch that.”

The prince froze. Joan did not correct Alice. Matthieu slowly withdrew his hand, embarrassed and angry, “It is only cloth,” he muttered.

“No,” Louis said quietly. “It is not.” The prince looked at him as if noticing him for the first time. Louis held his gaze, still gentle, but no longer soft. “Some things in this house are not trophies.”

Joan stood then, “I think,” she said, “we have imposed long enough.”

Étienne inclined his head. “A wise conclusion.”

Alice walked them to the door. Louis stayed behind with Aurora. At the threshold, Joan paused. “Thank you for dinner.”

“You are welcome anytime,” Alice said.

Joan’s eyes flicked briefly toward her husband and son. “I may come alone next time.”

Alice did not smile, but her voice softened. “That may be easier.”

“For whom?”

“All of us.”

Joan gave a quiet laugh. Étienne said nothing. Matthieu looked back once at Alice, not with respect exactly, but with the first uneasy awareness that the woman before him was not merely a shadow from war stories. She was a mother. A wife. A blade. A witness. And perhaps something more dangerous than all of those: someone his mother trusted.

When the imperial carriage rolled away, Alice closed the door and stood still. Louis came beside her, Aurora tucked against his chest, “Well,” he said, “that could have gone worse.” Alice stared at him. He nodded. “Yes. I hear it now.” Alice exhaled, long and slow. Louis leaned against her shoulder. “The Empress was kind.”

“Yes.”

“The husband was not.”

“No.”

“The son is a problem.”

Alice looked toward the door. “He is young.”

“That is often where problems begin.”

Aurora made a small sleepy sound. Alice reached out and stroked her daughter’s cheek. “She must never learn to admire cruelty because it wears a crown,” Alice said.

Louis looked at her. “Then we will teach her better.” Alice nodded.

Outside, the palace carriage vanished into the upper streets of Korland. Inside, the house grew quiet again, and Alice understood something new: serving Joan would not only mean guarding her from assassins, rebels, and enemies beyond the throne; it might also mean guarding her from the loneliness within it.

## Chapter Seven: The Inner Palace

The next morning, Joan said only, “Come with me.” Alice had learned quickly that the Empress rarely explained herself before necessary. So she followed. They did not go to the council chambers. They did not go to the courtyard of stones. Instead, Joan led her through a guarded bronze door Alice had never seen opened before, then down a corridor lined with living crystal and old imperial portraits.

“The inner palace,” Joan said. Alice stiffened slightly. Joan noticed. “You look as if I have led you into a trap.”

“You often do.”

“That is fair.”

They passed into a sunlit hall filled with voices. Not ministers. Children. Alice stopped. The room beyond was enormous, half nursery, half academy, half battlefield disguised as a family sitting room. Young Kobolds of every age occupied it: some reading, some sparring with wooden staves, some arguing over maps, some chasing a shrieking pierre d’amour beneath a table. Matthieu stood near the window, arms folded, looking as pleased to see Alice as he would have been to see a tax auditor.

Joan stepped forward, “My children,” she said.

Alice stared. *All of them?*

Joan’s mouth twitched. “Eight sons. Twelve daughters. The Empire prefers abundance in its succession.”

A younger daughter with jade-green stone-skin climbed onto a chair to get a better look at Alice. “Is that the Reaper?”

Another whispered, “She looks smaller than I thought.”

Alice turned toward Joan. “Majesty.”

“Yes?”

“You have twenty children.”

“I am aware.”

“And you brought me here because…?”

Joan’s expression changed then. The humor faded, replaced by something heavier, “Because one day, one of them will inherit the throne. The others will command armies, govern provinces, advise councils, lead temples, marry into dangerous houses, or be used by factions that smile while sharpening knives.” She looked across the room at them. “They have tutors. They have priests. They have weapons masters. They have etiquette instructors.”

Joan turned back to Alice, “What they lack is someone who understands survival.” Matthieu scoffed softly. Alice heard it. Joan ignored him. “I want you to train them,” the Empress said. “Not merely in combat. In judgment. Discipline. restraint. Reading danger. Refusing flattery. Knowing when mercy is strength and when it is vanity.” Her voice lowered. “I want my children to become worthy heirs. Not ornamental heirs. Not hungry heirs. Worthy ones.” Alice was silent.

A little son, perhaps four years old, toddled toward her and stared up with solemn intensity. “Can you kill a dragon?”

“No,” Alice said.

He frowned. “Why not?”

“Poor planning.” The child considered this, then nodded gravely and wandered away.

Joan smiled. “You see? They are eager.”

“They are terrifying.”

“They are imperial children.”

“That is what I said.” Joan laughed.

Matthieu stepped forward. “Mother, surely you cannot be serious.”

“I am.”

“She is an assassin.”

Alice looked at him. “Formerly.”

“That is not better.”

“No,” Alice said. “It is merely accurate.”

Joan’s gaze hardened. “Matthieu.” The prince lowered his eyes, but not his pride.

Alice studied him, then looked across the hall. Twenty children. Twenty futures. Twenty possible disasters. Twenty chances for the Empire to be better or worse than what had made her. She thought of Aurora. She thought of a child growing up under the shadow of power. Then she bowed her head. “I will train them,” Alice said. “But not as ornaments. Not as heirs who think blood makes them strong.”

Joan’s eyes gleamed. “Good.”

Alice looked at Matthieu first, “They will begin by learning how to fall.”

Several children exchanged confused looks. One of the daughters raised her hand. “Fall, Kir Alice?”

“Yes,” Alice said. “Everyone who rules will fall. The worthy learn how to rise without blaming the ground.” Joan folded her hands behind her back, deeply pleased. Matthieu looked insulted. Alice allowed herself the smallest smile. “Clear the center of the room,” she said.

The imperial children hesitated. Then Joan spoke, calm and absolute. “You heard your instructor.” At once, princes and princesses scrambled to obey.

Alice stepped into the middle of the inner palace, surrounded by the future of Korland. For years, she had been ordered to end threats before they could grow. Now Joan was asking her to do something far more difficult. To help children grow rightly. Alice removed her gloves. “First lesson,” she said. “Power does not make you worthy.” The room fell quiet. Alice looked at each of them in turn. “What you do when you have power does.”

## Chapter Eight: The Lesson of Falling

The first lesson went poorly. Not because the imperial children were weak. They were not. Joan’s children had been raised beneath stone vaults and military banners. Even the youngest among them knew how to stand straight, bow properly, recite ancestral names, and identify the sigils of every major noble house in Korland, but none of them knew how to fall. That became clear almost immediately.

Alice stood in the center of the inner palace hall with her arms folded behind her back. The furniture had been cleared away. Rugs had been rolled back. Servants stood anxiously near the walls, looking as if they expected her to begin throwing princes through windows. Joan remained nearby, seated in a carved basalt chair, watching in silence.

Matthieu stood at the head of the group, as heir apparent, looking deeply offended that he had been included at all. Around him stood his siblings: older sons with military posture, younger daughters whispering behind jeweled sleeves, small children bouncing on their heels with dangerous enthusiasm.

Alice walked slowly before them, “Every one of you has been taught how to stand,” she said. “How to enter a chamber. How to receive a bow. How to hold a blade. How to speak as if your words matter.” A few of the children straightened proudly. Alice stopped. “That is the easy part.” Matthieu’s jaw tightened. Alice turned toward him. “Strike me.” The room went still.

Matthieu blinked. “What?”

“Strike me.”

He glanced at Joan. Joan only lifted one eyebrow. The prince’s pride recovered faster than his judgment. He stepped forward, drew himself up, and swung. It was not a foolish strike. Alice gave him that. It was trained, direct, and fast enough to bruise someone unprepared. Alice was not unprepared. She turned aside, caught his wrist, shifted her weight, and sent the heir apparent of the Kobold Empire flat onto his back. The sound echoed through the hall.

Several of his younger siblings gasped. One small daughter clapped before a servant frantically lowered her hands. Matthieu stared upward, stunned beyond anger.

Alice looked down at him. “You were taught to attack from dignity.”

His eyes flashed. “I was not ready.”

“No,” Alice said. “You were not willing to be undignified.” She offered him a hand. For one second, it seemed he would refuse. Then, under Joan’s gaze, he took it. Alice pulled him upright. “You expected me to respect your station,” she said. “An enemy will not. A grieving subject will not. A starving province will not. A poisoned friend will not. The world does not care that you are heir apparent. The ground certainly does not.”

A murmur passed through the children. Alice turned to all of them. “So today, you learn to meet the ground properly.” For the next hour, Alice taught them to fall. At first, it was chaos. The older princes tried to make the exercise look graceful and failed. The younger children threw themselves down with unnecessary drama. One of the middle daughters, Mirelle, insisted she already understood the principle and immediately landed so poorly that Alice had to correct her shoulder. “Again,” Alice said.

Mirelle glared. “I did it.”

“You survived it. That is not the same.”

A boy named Lucien, perhaps ten, kept laughing every time he fell. Alice watched him closely. “Why are you laughing?”

“Because it is funny.”

“Good,” Alice said. “That may save you one day.”

He blinked. “Falling?”

“No. Not fearing embarrassment.”

By the end of the lesson, the room was full of dust, scuffed elbows, offended dignity, and several young royals breathing hard on the polished floor. Matthieu had fallen more than anyone. Alice had seen to that. He stood last, shoulders stiff, eyes bright with fury.

“Again?” she asked.

“No,” Joan said from her chair. Alice turned. The Empress rose. “Enough for today.” Several children collapsed in relief.

Alice bowed her head. “Majesty.”

Joan stepped into the center of the hall and looked over her children. Her face was stern, but Alice saw the satisfaction beneath it, “You have been taught many things by those who fear you,” Joan said. “Alice will teach you what fear cannot. Listen to her.”

The children bowed. Matthieu bowed last. When the others began filing out, Joan remained. So did Matthieu. Alice knew at once that this was intentional. The prince spoke first, “Was that necessary?”

Alice looked at him. “Yes.”

“You humiliated me in front of my siblings.”

“You humiliated yourself by thinking falling was humiliation.”

Matthieu stepped closer. “You enjoy speaking to me like that.”

“No,” Alice said. “I enjoy being alive. Speaking plainly helps.” Joan made no move to intervene.

Matthieu looked between them. “You are training us to distrust rank.”

“I am training you to survive without hiding behind it.”

“I am heir to the Empire.”

“Yes.”

“I must command respect.”

Alice stepped close enough that he had to look up slightly, “No. You must become the kind of man whom respect can safely obey.” The words landed harder than the throw. Matthieu said nothing.

Joan watched her son with unreadable eyes. At last, the prince gave a short, rigid bow and left. When he was gone, Joan exhaled. “He hates you,” she said.

“He hates the feeling that I might be right.”

“That is worse.”

“Indeed.”

Joan walked toward the open balcony overlooking the palace gardens. Alice followed, taking her place at her side. “He is not cruel,” Joan said after a time.

“No.”

“But he is proud.”

“Very.”

“That pride was cultivated.” Alice glanced at her. Joan’s face was still, but her voice had lowered. “By tutors. Nobles. His father. Men who believe an heir should enter every room already certain that he deserves it.”

Alice watched the palace servants below tending the crystal hedges. “Certainty is useful in battle.”

“And dangerous in rule.”

“Yes.”

Joan nodded slowly. “That is why I brought you here.” Alice understood then that Joan had not only asked her to train the imperial children. She had asked her to interfere in the formation of the next Empire. That was no small thing. It might be more dangerous than assassination.

That evening, Alice returned home later than she intended. Louis was seated near the hearth with Aurora nestled against him, reading aloud from an old children’s tale about a pierre d’amour that tried to become a mountain. Aurora was far too young to understand, but she watched his face with enormous seriousness, as if evaluating the plot.

After Alice explained her new assignment, Louis looked up and asked, “How many imperial heirs survived your first lesson?”

“All of them.”

“That sounds like success.”

“One may disagree.”

“Matthieu?”

Alice removed her gloves. “Yes.”

Louis shifted Aurora carefully. “Did you throw him?”

“Yes.”

“More than once?”

“Yes.”

Louis considered this. “And the Empress allowed it?”

“The Empress enjoyed it.”

Louis chuckled, “That family is complicated.” Alice sat beside him, suddenly exhausted. Aurora reached toward her. The movement was clumsy and small, but deliberate. Alice froze. Louis smiled. “She has been doing that all afternoon.” Alice slowly extended one hand. Aurora wrapped her tiny fingers around Alice’s claw. Alice’s day shifted. The palace, the training hall, Matthieu’s anger, Joan’s burden, the future of Korland; all of it moved back, not gone, but properly distant.

Alice leaned forward and pressed her forehead gently against Aurora’s. “I taught princes how to fall today,” she whispered. Aurora blinked.

Louis smiled. “And what did they teach you?”

Alice watched her daughter’s tiny grip tighten. “That the world is very heavy when children are involved.”

Louis’s expression softened. “Yes,” he said. “It is.”

Later that night, after Aurora had finally fallen asleep, Alice sat awake beside the cradle-stone. The house was quiet. Louis slept in the next room. Outside, Korland glowed beneath its volcanic sky. Alice thought of Joan’s children.

Twenty royal sons and daughters, born into power so dense it might harden around them before they ever understood its weight. She thought of Matthieu, angry and proud, but not empty. She thought of Joan, who had all the power in the world and still seemed terribly alone. Then she looked at Aurora. Her daughter had no title, no command, no court faction whispering into her cradle. But she too would inherit something. Alice’s name. Alice’s enemies. Alice’s silences. Alice’s scars. That frightened Alice more than war. She reached into the cradle and gently adjusted Aurora’s blanket. “I will learn first,” she whispered. “Then I will teach you better.”

The next morning, Alice arrived at the inner palace to find all twenty imperial children waiting. Some looked sore. Some looked excited. Matthieu looked furious.

Mirelle raised her hand. “Are we falling again today?”

Alice removed her cloak and set it aside. “No,” she said.

Relief spread across the room. Alice stepped to the center of the hall. “Today, you learn how to listen when someone weaker than you is speaking.” The relief vanished. From the doorway, Joan smiled.

## Chapter Nine: The Months of Stone and Dawn

The next few months taught Alice that training imperial children was more exhausting than surviving assassins. Assassins, at least, usually had one clear objective. Joan’s children had twenty. Some wanted approval. Some wanted victory. Some wanted their mother’s attention. Some wanted their siblings to fail. Some wanted nothing more than to prove that Alice was not as frightening as the stories claimed. They were all disappointed in different ways.

Alice divided their training into three parts. The first was the body. They learned how to fall, how to rise, how to fight without relying on ceremonial technique, and how to move when robes, armor, pride, or fear got in the way. The younger ones took to it fastest. Lucien, still delighted by every undignified tumble, became shockingly difficult to knock down. Mirelle discovered she had a gift for close-quarters grappling and immediately became unbearable about it. Two of the older sons, Henri and Bastien, preferred blade work and resented being made to practice empty-handed drills against their smaller sisters. Alice corrected that quickly. “The enemy will not ask if you prefer swords,” she told them. Then she made Bastien spar with his twelve-year-old sister Céleste, who promptly swept his legs out from under him.

The second part was the mind. This, Alice discovered, was far harder. She gave them scenarios. A starving village hoarding grain. A border captain executing smugglers without trial. A loyal governor lying to avoid panic. A rebellion led by someone with legitimate grievances. A treaty that would save ten thousand lives but humiliate the Empire. The children argued. Loudly.

Matthieu almost always answered first and almost always answered as a prince, “Punish the guilty.”

“Secure the road.”

“Assert authority.”

“Preserve imperial dignity.”

Alice would listen, then ask, “And then?” At first, he hated that question. Punish the guilty, and then their brothers take up arms. Secure the road, and then the village starves. Assert authority, and then everyone obeys while waiting for you to weaken. Preserve imperial dignity, and then bury the dead who paid for it. Slowly, painfully, the children began to understand that rule did not end when the order was given. That was only where the consequences began.

The third part was the heart. Alice never called it that. She called it “judgment under pressure.” Joan called it “the part my tutors forgot.” Alice brought servants into the lesson hall. Stable hands. Midwives. Old soldiers. Clerks. A cook who had once survived a mine collapse. A former prisoner from Hurley who spoke, with trembling dignity, about what imperial occupation felt like from the underside of a boot. The children were required to listen. Not interrupt. Not correct. Not defend the Empire. Listen. This lesson offended them more than falling. Especially Matthieu.

One afternoon, after a gray-haired Hurleyan mason described losing two sons in the war, Matthieu spoke too sharply. “My mother ended the war,” he said. “Without the Empire, Hurley would still be devouring itself.” The room went cold.

Alice turned to him. “Stand.” He stood. “Repeat what he said.”

Matthieu blinked. “What?”

“Repeat his words. Not your answer. His words.” The prince’s face tightened. Alice waited. At last, awkwardly, he repeated the mason’s account. Not perfectly. He had missed details. Alice made him begin again. By the third time, his voice had changed. Not softened exactly. But slowed. When he finished, Alice said, “Now you may answer.”

Matthieu looked at the old man. Then he sat down. “I have no answer,” he said.

The mason bowed his head slightly. Alice said nothing, but Joan, watching from the rear of the hall, did, “Good,” the Empress said, “that is the beginning of wisdom.”

As the children changed, the palace changed around them. Ministers grew wary of the training hall. Generals disliked the questions the older children began asking at dinners. Noble tutors complained privately that Alice was making the princes and princesses “overly familiar with low concerns.”

Joan read the complaint aloud to Alice one evening and asked, “What do you think?”

Alice replied, “Any concern that can kill your people is not low.”

Joan signed the complaint with a single word: Denied.

Alice’s place beside Joan also deepened. She no longer merely stood near the Empress. She sat in councils when Joan wished her to sit. She marked maps. She challenged reports. She became the person Joan looked to when polished language began to hide ugly realities. When a governor requested authority to “relocate disruptive populations,” Alice said, “He means remove families from profitable land.” When a priest of the Kedgelony warned of “spiritual softness among the youth,” Alice said, “He means they are asking questions.” When a general proposed “decisive pacification,” Alice said, “He means he has failed at governance and wants artillery to hide it.”

Joan valued this. Others did not.

In the outer halls, Alice began to hear whispers. Not about herself. Those she knew how to ignore. These were different. “Robert’s kin.” The phrase appeared first in a passing conversation between two naval officers near the southern archive.

Alice heard one say, “If the reports are true, Robert’s kin may be more fact than fiction...”

The other answered, “It still seems unreasonable though, don’t you think?”

When Alice turned the corner, both men fell silent.

At home, however, the world was smaller and more immediate. Aurora grew quickly. Too quickly, Louis insisted, though he said this every week. Her stone-soft skin began to take on a pale dawn-gray hue, with faint crystal veins along her cheeks that glowed when she was excited. Her ears twitched at every sound. Her eyes followed Alice everywhere.

She learned to laugh before she learned to crawl. Her first laugh came when Alice, exhausted after a late council, accidentally dropped a spoon and caught it with a tiny ridge of raised stone before it hit the floor. Aurora found this hilarious. Louis declared it her first appreciation of geomancy. Alice declared it a security risk.

Soon Aurora expected the spoon trick at every meal. Then the cup trick. Then the pebble trick. Then, disastrously, she began attempting to command stones herself by staring at them very intensely and squeaking.

“She is ambitious,” Louis said.

“She is six months old.”

“Still.”

Balancing court and motherhood became a discipline Alice had never been trained for. Some mornings she left home with Aurora’s milk on her sleeve and stood beside Joan while generals discussed troop readiness. Some afternoons she returned from instructing imperial heirs in moral restraint only to find her own daughter chewing on the edge of a sacred household charm. Some nights she answered sealed palace dispatches with one hand while rocking Aurora with the other. Louis helped without making her ask.

He learned Joan’s schedule nearly as well as Alice did. He prepared meals she could eat quickly. He knew when she needed silence and when silence would trap her too deeply in herself. He brought Aurora to the palace gardens twice, where Joan’s younger daughters gathered around her like she was a rare and miraculous creature.

Matthieu, watching from a distance, asked once, “Why does everyone become foolish around babies?”

Alice answered, “Because babies do not care about rank, and this confuses the ranked.”

He considered that. Then Aurora sneezed on his sleeve. For the first time Alice had ever seen, Matthieu laughed without guarding himself.It did not fix him, but it revealed something worth saving.

As the months passed, Alice became accustomed to living in three worlds. In the training hall, she shaped heirs. In the council chamber, she sharpened an Empress. At home, she was shaped by a child who cared nothing for her past and everything for whether she was near.

Yet beneath all three worlds, something moved. Reports from Hurley became more frequent. Certain old families vanished from census rolls. A courier from the western coast disappeared. A general requested emergency authorization, then withdrew the request before Joan could question him. Twice, Alice entered rooms where conversations ended too quickly. Always, eventually, the same phrase surfaced. Robert’s kin. One evening, after Aurora had fallen asleep and Louis had gone to bank the hearth, Alice stood at the window, looking toward the palace lights.

Louis came beside her. “You are far away.”

“No,” Alice said. “Something is coming closer.”

He followed her gaze. “Danger?”

“Perhaps only rumor.”

“You do not believe that.” Alice said nothing.mLouis took her hand. “Will it take you from us?”

That was the question beneath every question now.

Alice looked back at Aurora’s cradle. Her daughter slept with one hand open, as if trying to hold the whole room.

“No,” Alice said. Louis watched her carefully. Alice repeated it, more firmly. “No. Not this time.”

The next morning, Joan summoned Alice before dawn. No herald. No ceremony. Only the Empress in the small map room, surrounded by sealed reports, her face grave beneath the blue crystal lamps. On the table lay a map of Hurley. Several western territories had been circled in red. Joan tapped one marked region with two fingers.

“The generals are worried,” she said. Alice stepped closer. There, written beside the mark in a careful military hand, were three words: *Robert’s kin confirmed*. Joan looked up. “Find out whether this is a threat,” she said, “or an excuse.”

Alice’s eyes hardened. “Yes, Majesty.”

And for the first time in months, the old part of her stirred. Not the Reaper, not exactly, ut the woman who knew how to follow whispers into darkness and return with the truth in her hands.
