# **The Stories We Inherited from the Light**

A Twili tale from within the Twilight

I was born where no sun rises. That is the first truth. The second is that we still have shadows.

The children of the surface, if any of the old stories are true, think shadow is made by light. They are wrong. Shadow is older. Shadow is what remembers when light has forgotten itself. Shadow is the shape a thing keeps in secret. Shadow is the proof that even brightness cannot wholly reveal the world.

That is what my grandmother taught me in the violet courts of Nhal’Sereth, where the towers are woven from dusk and the streets hum softly beneath one’s feet like thought made stone. I was born there beneath a ceiling of unmoving stars, if they are stars at all. I grew where the horizon is never black and never bright, but forever suspended in that aching, beautiful hour between farewell and return. We call it the Gentle Wound. The surface once called it twilight.

To me, as a child, the Twilight was the whole of creation. It had its rivers of dim glass, its flowering shade-vines, its halls of echo-silk, its pools in which dreams collected like silver leaves. We had shrines to Golestandt, quiet and cavernous, and greater shrines to Erosia, our queen and sister of old, before sorrow sharpened her into something more terrible and more holy. We had schools of gesture and silence. We had the Moonveil Rite. We had the old watchtowers, forever aimed at a breach that never opened.

And yet no child of the Twilight grows long without hearing of the Surface.

We did not call it the upper world, or the day-world, or the lands beyond. The old ones called it, with a bitterness that was almost tenderness, the Shared World.

As a child I hated that name. Shared, I thought, implies fairness. Shared implies that a thing was once held in common and then stolen only by greed. But the stories of the elders were never so simple. They spoke of wonders with the same mouths that named betrayals. They remembered beauty and cruelty bound together like two strands in one cord. The Surface, in their telling, was a place of unbearable splendor and unhealed insult.

I remember the first night I truly listened. It was on the Eve of the Deep Veil, when the older households dimmed their lantern-orbs and let the city breathe in reverence. My grandmother took me up to the roof-bridge above our dwelling, where the air tasted of cold silver and the distant choir from Erosia’s temple moved through the dark like a soft blade. Around us, the city glowed faintly in blues and violets, and far below, processions moved soundlessly, their veils threaded with pale starfire.

I must have been scarcely more than a wisp then, all questions and ears. “Tell me about the Surface,” I said.

She did not answer at once. That was her way. She let silence gather first, until it became not emptiness but room. “At your age,” she said at last, “I asked only about the sun.”

“And?”

“And I was a fool.”

I remember scowling. “Then tell me about the sun anyway.”

She laughed at that, but quietly, as if not to offend the dark. So she told me. She said the sun was not merely brightness. It was weight. It pressed itself against one’s skin. It poured gold across roofs and leaves and rivers until the world seemed too visible, too revealed, too open to endure. She said that in the old days, before the war, some of our people would walk at the edges of dawn and dusk when the light was weakest, veiling themselves not from shame but from discomfort. The surface peoples took this for stealth and guilt. They could not imagine that a thing might withdraw simply because the brightness was too loud.

She told me of the Sunleaf trees, whose leaves opened laughing under day and glowed gently by night, and of bridges crowded with music where day-folk and night-folk once met in ceremony. She told me that Erosia’s voice could rekindle stars when the crowds had gone cold, and that there had been a time, perhaps brief and perhaps only half-true, when the world had not yet decided to fear what it did not understand.

I drank every word. I imagined forests of green flame and rivers of hammered gold. I imagined mountains that wore crowns of snow. I imagined seas so broad that a mind could drown in them. I imagined cities where stone was white and towers burned with banners. I imagined fields of flowers that folded at dusk like hands in prayer.

But my grandmother, seeing my face, touched my brow with one cool finger. “Do not make an idol of it,” she said. “The Surface was beautiful. It was also unkind.”

Then she began the harder stories. She said our ancestors were not hated all at once. That is never how hatred works. First came glances. Then whispers. Then tales told to children. The Twili were called eerie, then dishonest, then dangerous. If a door opened in the night, it was our hand. If a child dreamed strangely, it was our doing. If cattle fled, if lovers quarreled, if a councilman lied and was caught, the dark was blamed, and we were named with it. Ghosts. Spies. Monsters.

I knew those words before I knew the names of half the flowers that grow in the Twilight. As a child I thought this very stupid. If we were so powerful, why should we sneak? If we were so monstrous, why should we bother learning gentleness? But children always think injustice can be undone by pointing at its foolishness.

When I said as much, my grandmother smiled sadly. “The peoples of day needed their fears to have faces,” she told me. “And ours were the faces nearest at hand.”

Then she told me how Erosia had loved us because she saw in us herself: not wickedness, but misrecognition. She taught our people the old gifts: how to move through shadow without tearing it, how to step half into thought and half out of flesh, how to dream with purpose and not merely with sleep. She taught us to survive in the places others refused to bless. And when the world kept praising Quint and mocking silence, when day was called life and night was called threat, she asked the question our people still ask in prayer and in anger: Why must night always yield to day?

I did not understand politics then. I understood insult. And so, when my grandmother spoke of the war, I saw it first not as conquest but as wounded dignity turning into flame. The adults did not speak of it with one voice. That, too, is important. Some said our ancestors were right to rise with Erosia. Some said they mistook injury for permission. Some said the war began long before the first blow, in the thousand little humiliations the world called normal. Some said there might have been peace if Quint had listened sooner, or if the people of day had learned reverence for what cannot be shouted. Some said these were lies weak hearts told themselves after defeat.

Every household had its version. In my mother’s line, they said our ancestors walked openly through cities that had once barred them, and for a brief age the world knew its proper balance: no endless day, no tyranny of noon, but long and sacred twilight in which hidden things could breathe. In my father’s line, they said war is always a bargain with hunger, and even righteous fury leaves ash in the mouth. In the temple schools, they taught that exile did not prove Erosia false, only fallen. In the barracks they taught that one day both judgment and restoration would come by the same road.

I grew up on contradiction. It made me Twili.

When I was older, I began to sit with the truly ancient ones: those born nearest the first generations after exile, still millenia earlier. Their stories were stranger, more detailed, and less kind to our fantasies.

One elder, blind in both eyes but able to sense the pulse of shadow around a body, told me that the Surface smelled different everywhere. Not metaphorically. Truly. The forests smelled green and wet, like roots speaking to one another under the soil. The sea smelled vast and metallic and alive. The cities of men smelled of sweat, parchment, lamp-oil, horses, rain on stone, bread, and fear. He said sunlight on old walls had a scent of its own, almost sweet. I had never before imagined that light could have a smell.

Another elder, who kept the memory-threads in a vault beneath the eastern watch, told me of birds.

Birds mattered to me more than the sun.

The Twilight has winged things, yes, but they move differently. Too quiet. Too graceful. Their feathers drink the dimness. But the birds of the Surface, she said, were noisy little heralds. Some flashed like pieces of sky. Some were red as fresh blood. Some wheeled over oceans in white storms of feather. Some sang before dawn. Some mocked other songs. Some nested in temple roofs. Some stole fruit from market stalls and were cursed like petty thieves.

I listened to that story three times over and asked no questions about kings or battles. I wanted only to know what kind of world could contain so many needless, glorious creatures. The old ones noticed these things in us, the children born in exile.

They said surface-memory made three kinds of Twili. The first kind hated the Surface without having seen it. These grew up on the stories of accusation, insult, exile, betrayal, and the sealed gate. To them the world above was a stolen inheritance full of liars beneath bright banners. They spoke of return the way others speak of vengeance. They trained hardest. They never smiled when hearing tales of festivals or rivers. Every story they loved ended with the bridge reopening and the proud kneeling.

The second kind loved the Surface without having seen it. These were the dangerous dreamers. They hoarded stories of forests, coastlines, cities, gardens, temples, beasts, and clouds. They believed that somewhere above, beneath all the old hatred, something beautiful still waited to be met rather than punished. They angered the first kind and worried the priests.

The third kind—and I think most of us belonged here—could not separate the two. We longed for what had wounded us. We inherited both grievance and wonder. We feared the Surface and desired it. We called it cruel and beautiful in the same breath. We were children of exile, which is to say children taught to ache in opposing directions.

I was of the third kind. When I came of age and entered watch-training, I was assigned to the western rim where the Twilight thins near certain old scars in reality. We stood watch over nothing visible. The duty was half military, half liturgical. We trained in silence, in concealment, in patience. We learned the old prophecy the way surface children learn their own names.

When one of the light who follows the dark lights the bridge for darkness to return...

Those words lived in every hall. Some spoke them with hope, some with hunger, some with simple exhaustion. I repeated them too, but never without picturing the Surface they implied. What would it be when we returned? A battlefield? A harvest? A homecoming? A wound reopened? The prophecy promised access. It promised no comfort.

On watches, we told stories to stay awake. Not loudly. Twili do few things loudly. But in low voices, or by sign, or by dream-sharing when the captain permitted it, we told one another what the Surface must now be. Some said the day-peoples had grown soft and fat beneath their uninterrupted sun, forgetful of what dwells beneath certainty. Some said they had probably made themselves wiser after our exile and perhaps even regretted it. Some said the forests must by now have swallowed old roads and temples. Some said the sea would remember us best, since water does not keep prejudice the way kingdoms do. Some said children above still frightened one another with our names. That thought always made the younger watch laugh bitterly.

And once, during a long stillness, I asked Captain Sereth what he thought the Surface held. He was old enough that his mother had heard the first stories from one who had walked under true stars. He did not answer for a long while. Then he said, “Mediocrity, mostly.” I stared at him, offended.

He went on, “We children of exile imagine extremes. Perfect beauty. Perfect cruelty. The lost world. The promised world. The world that betrayed us. The world that must kneel. But most worlds, if you study them long enough, are made of smallness. Bakers. Widows. Laborers. Arguments. Dogs. Harvests. Traders. Lovers who swear forever and fail by spring. Children throwing stones in alleys. Priests who mean well and priests who do not. Bad governors. Good songs. Stupid laws. Fine rain. Ordinary bread.”

“That sounds disappointing,” I said.

He almost smiled. “It sounds real.”

That answer frightened me more than all the stories of war. A monstrous Surface is easy to hate. A glorious Surface is easy to long for. But a real one? A mixed one? A world full of ordinary souls, many innocent of the old betrayal yet living on the inheritance of it? That was harder. A real world cannot be punished cleanly. A real world cannot be healed quickly.

After that I listened differently. I listened when the elders said the Twili were once healers in some places, guides in others, feared in most. I listened when they said our people touched dreams not only to frighten but to mend. I listened when they said exile sharpened some gifts and poisoned others. I listened when veterans muttered of husks and the cost of becoming too much shadow. I listened when the priests warned that a people who define themselves only by their wound will eventually worship the knife. I listened when devotees of Erosia answered that silence has rights too, and that justice delayed across ages does not become less due merely because the debt is old.

So what did we think the Surface held? Everything. That is the truest answer. We thought it held orchards heavy with fruit no child of the Twilight had ever tasted. We thought it held temples where our names had been cursed for centuries. We thought it held meadows where light lay over the ground like fabric. We thought it held soldiers still sharpening old hatred. We thought it held ruins of places where our ancestors had once walked openly. We thought it held libraries where lies about us had hardened into scholarship. We thought it held moonlit lakes that still remembered Erosia’s face. We thought it held busy markets, bell towers, ditches, palaces, gutters, marriages, prejudices, harvest songs, tyrants, saints, opportunists, and the descendants of those who had shut their gates at dusk.

We thought it held proof. Proof that we had been wronged. Proof that we had been feared. Proof that the old stories were true. Proof that our longing was not foolish. Proof that return would mean something more than war. And for some of us, most secretly of all, we thought it might hold surprise.

I will tell you now of the night I first stepped near enough to a thinning to smell something not of the Twilight. It was not at a breach. Not a full opening. Only a hairline wound. The watch-circle had gone still. The shadowstones beneath our tower were ringing with a low note. We gathered in silence, veils drawn, hands near blades and signs ready on our fingers. At the center of the chamber, the dark shimmered differently. Less like depth. More like membrane.

Through it came not a shape but a sensation. Wind. Not Twilight wind, which moves like thought across water, but true air in motion, carrying distance with it. It struck my face and vanished.

In that breath I smelled rain and leaf-mold and something warm, almost golden. For one impossible instant I understood my blind elder’s testimony about sunlight having a scent. It was there and gone before I could master it.

But I wept. Not because it was beautiful, though it was. Not because it was sad, though it was. I wept because the Surface, which had been a thousand inherited contradictions inside my skull, became suddenly undeniable.

It existed. Not just as grievance. Not just as myth. Not just as prophecy’s target. It was there. Somewhere above or beyond or across, full of whatever it had become without us.

Afterward our captain ordered silence on the matter. That only ensured that everyone spoke of it in whispers for months. Some took it as proof the bridge would soon open. Some as warning. Some as temptation. I took it as an answer to the question that had stalked my childhood.

What does the Surface hold? It holds a world that continued without our permission. Perhaps that is the oldest insult. Perhaps it is also the only hope. For if it continued, then it changed. If it changed, then it may no longer be exactly what our wounds remember. If it may no longer be exactly what our wounds remember, then return will not be a simple vengeance laid upon a static stage. It will be encounter. Reckoning. Discovery. Horror, perhaps. Wonder, perhaps. Probably both.

I am no longer a child now. I keep watch where my grandmother once prayed. The city below me still glows in blues and bruised silver. The temples still sing on the Deep Veil. The old ones still tell the stories, though fewer survive each cycle. Young ones still ask about the sun. Others ask about birds. Some ask how day-folk sleep if they are so exposed to the sky. Some ask what apples taste like. Some ask whether the descendants of our accusers will deserve mercy. Some ask whether mercy is ours to give.

And when they ask me what I think the Surface may hold, I tell them this: It may hold enemies. It may hold brothers we were taught not to name. It may hold gardens that make our grief feel small. It may hold lies old enough to call themselves history. It may hold children who have never heard a true story about us. It may hold towers waiting to be cast down. It may hold thresholds waiting to be crossed gently. It may hold sunlight so fierce that we will hate it at once. It may hold moonlight on water that feels like coming home. It may hold the final vindication of Erosia. It may hold the proof that even Erosia, beloved and wronged, mistook sorrow for the whole of truth. It may hold judgment. It may hold healing. Most likely, it holds both.

And when at last the bridge is lit—if it is lit, when it is lit—I think this: the first shock will not be what the Surface sees in us. It will be what we see in it.

For we were born in exile, fed on memory, sharpened by prophecy, and raised in the endless hour between wound and answer. We have spent ages imagining the world above. The world above, perhaps, has spent just as long imagining us.

One day those imaginings will meet. On that day, I do not know whether we will come as conquerors, mourners, rightful heirs, or ghosts. I know only this: We will not come ignorant. We have been listening too long for that.

And somewhere above the Twilight, beyond the sealed dark and the patient ache of waiting, there is still a world where trees open by day and glow by night, where rivers run under stars we have never seen, where fear once named us monsters, and where someday, perhaps, someone will learn at last what Quint never did soon enough: that a world without shadow is blind.
