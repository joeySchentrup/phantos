# Preface

This story starts in the year 1 AC and takes place on the continent of Korre. Korre consists of a large crescent mainland, that surrounds a large sea with numerous islands, including a really large one, called Korland which is a volcanic one where the capital of the Kobold Empire is headquartered, The large island is split by three nations, the Gnomish Republic to the North, The Dwarven Kingdom in the South, the the Elves in the forested center. There is also a large plains and river region between the Dwarves and Elves that many Dwelves live. These three nations have formed a Federation in defence against the Kobolds who have built a powerful empire ever since they were united under the Emperor Vercingetorix. The story will explore the first almost thirty years of Chione’s life and her struggles and adventures as well as her friends.

# Book One: The Child of Prophecy

A steady drizzle fell upon the polished deck of the KIS Imperial Beauty, its dark stone hull slicing cleanly through the northern straits. The scent of salt and iron filled the air, mingling with the low hum of geomantic engines pulsing deep within the ship’s belly. Above, the sky cracked with a muffled growl, the clouds like bruised parchment over the churning sea.

Kaptain Brice stood near the prow, arms folded behind his back, eyes narrowed at the horizon. His stony skin, marked by the faint shimmer of obsidian veins, caught the occasional flash of lightning—though his thoughts were far from the storm.

“Kir!” called a young officer breathlessly, his boots striking the deck in a frantic rhythm. “Message just came through—urgent tremor from Special Kagent Denice herself. She says the baby’s coming. You need to go. Now.”

Brice didn’t speak at first. He simply turned. The hard lines of his face softened imperceptibly.

“Kolmander,” he said, with the authority of a man who had barked orders through cannon fire and firestorms alike, “take the bridge. Hold our heading until my return.”

Without waiting for a reply, he stepped toward the transport sigil etched into the deck—a masterwork of geometric precision bound with runes of instantaneous travel. With one final glance toward the sea, he activated the seal.

In a flash of sapphire light, Brice vanished.

He blinked into existence on the stone platform outside the imperial birthing hall of Korland’s northern capital, the cold air biting at his uniform still damp with salt spray. Inside, the warm scent of cedar and incense met him—along with the gentle, rhythmic cooing of the pierres d’amour nesting in wall cradles, their glowing cores pulsing in anticipation of new life.

He hurried past ceremonial guards and reverent midwives until at last he saw her—Denice—sitting upright in the delivery alcove, her strong arms wrapped around a bundle of swaddling crystal-thread. In her hands, nestled against her chest, was not just a newborn Kobold, but a child unlike any Brice had seen. Her skin was smooth as polished granite, faintly blue along the ridges of her cheeks, and her eyes gleamed like twin sapphires cracked with starlight, and she was smiling.

“A girl,” Denice whispered as Brice knelt beside her. “And she came out laughing.”

Brice, for once, had no words. He reached out with trembling hands and cradled the infant. She was warm. Lighter than he expected. But heavy with the weight of something more—something he could not name.

“I wanted to name her Chione,” Denice said softly. “It means snow-heart, in the old dialect. Cold-born, but not unfeeling. Strong in stillness.”

Brice nodded, though his throat burned. “Then Chione it is.”

And in that quiet moment—amid the rustle of sacred stones and the silent gaze of ancient statues—father, mother, and child sat together beneath the watchful eyes of Rokesh, as thunder rolled beyond the walls and the prophecy, still unknown to them, stirred in its slumber.

The halls of the Kedgelony twisted beneath the surface of Korland like the roots of an ancient tree, vast and unyielding. Torchlight shimmered across walls carved with stories of earth and empire—tales of the Metabold and the sacred trials that separated the common from the chosen. Brice and Denice walked together in silence.

Chione, now a year old, rested in her mother’s arms. Her crystalline eyes scanned every flickering shadow and every carved symbol with eerie stillness, too quiet for a child her age. Ahead, a Sarkent in polished basalt armor stood guard before a sigil-marked door. He straightened at the sight of them, awe flickering in his jade-green eyes.

“At ease,” Brice said curtly, though not unkindly. “We’ve come for the child’s testing.”

The guard saluted, tail flicking nervously. “It is an honor, Kaptain Brice, Special Kagent Denice. I read of your campaign at Lanyaru Bay—brilliant maneuvering—and of your cleansing of the meta-squatters in the lowlands. Your service brings pride to all Kobolds.”

Denice offered a practiced nod. “Gratitude, Sarkent. But we mustn’t keep the elders waiting.”

The Sarkent bowed low and gestured forward. “Of course. The Kedgelony awaits.”

They followed him through winding corridors, the air growing cooler and heavier with each step. Finally, they reached a great obsidian door veined with living crystal. The Sarkent stepped forward, placing his clawed palm against a circular rune. The doorway groaned open, revealing a chamber steeped in sacred stillness.

At its center sat a triad of ancient Kobolds—elders of stone and spell, draped in ceremonial robes woven with pierre-d’amour threads. The one in the middle, a female with skin like amethyst and cracks glowing faintly with turquoise light, beckoned. “Bring forth the child.”

Denice hesitated only a moment before stepping into the circle, laying Chione gently upon the stone altar, carved in the shape of Rokesh’s sigil. The child looked up—not afraid, not even confused. Simply… watching.

The Kedgelony began to chant in voices older than cities. Earth trembled beneath their feet, and runes ignited along the chamber floor. Whispers of arcane judgment stirred the air like dust in a tomb.

Then the center elder’s voice deepened—becoming something not her own. “Stone-born, yet not bound. This child walks paths not meant for her kind. Pain and suffering shall follow her. While she is compatible… those who bore her shall not see her reach her fullness. She will find herself far from home, in lands not of Kobold make. She will gather others—but all shall fall. She will fall—and yet not. She shall be born anew… in the enemies of those she once called kin.”

The glow faded. The silence was suffocating. Chione blinked. Her lip trembled. Then she began to cry—not in fear, but as if echoing something unspoken in the room. Brice moved first. He swept her into his arms, shielding her from the sudden cold. Denice’s jaw was clenched tight. “Is she… compatible?”

The amethyst elder nodded solemnly. “Yes. The venom will take. But at great cost.”

No farewells were spoken. The couple turned and left, their footsteps echoing through the stillness. The prophecy clung to them like a shadow that would never leave. Beneath her father’s arm, Chione pressed her cheek to his chest—and quietly stopped crying.

Five years have passed since the prophecy was revealed and the wind had shifted. Kaptain Brice stood on the command deck of the KIS Imperial Beauty, his sharp gaze fixed on the gray northern horizon. The ship creaked softly beneath him, a marvel of geomantic engineering and Kobold craftsmanship—marble-plated hull, magma-tempered ballast, and a spine of obsidian-threaded stone that pulsed faintly with Rokesh’s blessing, but the sea was uneasy.

Behind him, officers waited in practiced silence. The crew had long ago learned not to interrupt their captain when he stared into the mist. Brice’s instincts were rarely wrong. He’d sensed ambushes in fog, tracked phantom ships by echo alone, and once, in the trenches of the Cracked Coast, had ordered a retreat just seconds before a Gnomish resonance mine split the earth.

This time, the dread came not from intuition—but from information. A runner had arrived two nights ago with word from Imperial Intelligence. The Gnomish Republic was mobilizing fleets again.

Officially, they were neutral. In truth, the Gnomes had been arming the Elves and skirmishing Kobold patrols along the northern straits. They’d denied it with smiles and trade promises—until now.

Now, Brice had orders to intercept a suspected Gnomish convoy crossing into contested waters. But as the fog broke and the shapes ahead resolved, Brice realized they weren’t merchants. They were warships. Eight of them.

Sleek, low to the water, and bristling with cannon arrays and technomagical ballast. Gnomish designs—rounded, bulbous hulls with gleaming crystal turrets and rune-cannons that could fire in arcs no Kobold vessel could match.

“General alert,” Brice said quietly. “Full battle stations. All runes to offense posture. Prepare the forecoil. We will not fire unless fired upon—but we will not flinch.”

“Aye, Kir,” came the response.

The signal flags rose. The gnomish ships answered with silence.

Then, the lead vessel—a crescent-hulled monstrosity bearing the sigil of the Iron Assembly—unleashed a volley.

Brice did not hesitate. “Return fire! Pattern Delta-Seven! Helm, cut across the wave front!”

The Imperial Beauty surged into action—runestones glowing, cannons spitting fire and stone into the mist. The two sides clashed with thunder and fury, the sea churned with broken metal and stone shards, and the sky turned black with smoke.

The Kobolds fought well. But the Gnomes had been planning this. Their warships outnumbered Brice’s by two and flanked fast—propelled by vented aether and tri-core engines. Their spellcrafters fired mirrored hex-bolts that bent gravity and pierced through hull plating like glass. A second volley caught the Beauty’s portside stabilizer.

“She’s listing!” shouted the helmsman. “We’ve lost equilibrium!”

Brice gritted his teeth. “Redirect all coil-feed to the auxiliary ley-spine. Divert the Pierre reserves into the ventral ballast. We hold.” The engineers obeyed, but the ship groaned beneath their feet.

Then the enemy flagship turned. It fired a concentrated null-bolt—a weapon that broke enchantment, not stone. It slammed into the core runes along the keel, and in an instant, every geomantic channel flickered and died.

Silence fell. The Imperial Beauty floated powerless. Brice stood tall. “Ready boarding deterrents,” he ordered. “And prime the keel-seal.”

The crew froze. “You mean to—”

“Yes.”

“But Kir,” the quartermaster said, breath catching. “That’ll collapse the hull. We’ll go down with—”

“Better that than give them our core. Or our dead. Or our pride.” They looked at him and saw no fear. He looked toward the bow. In the smoke, he saw the gnomish flagship advancing. He could almost make out their admiral—short, stocky, wrapped in technomantic armor, peering through an ocular lens at the wreck he was about to claim. Brice raised his hand. “By Rokesh,” he said, “let this stone not crack in shame.”

He pressed the seal. A flash of blue light erupted from the ship’s spine—followed by a deafening rumble as the internal channels reversed. The keel collapsed inward, folding the ship like a wounded serpent, and in seconds, the Imperial Beauty sank beneath the waves—taking her captain, her runes, and her secrets with her. There were no survivors. Only wreckage and silence.

In Korland, the news would arrive three days later. A folded banner. A carved stone. A letter. Denice would not weep where others could see. But that night, alone in her quarters, she held Chione close.

The bell tolled across Korland. Its voice was deep and cold, a single note carved from the mountains themselves. It rang not only through the stone halls of the Citadel but into the trembling hearts of every citizen, soldier, and soul loyal to the Empire.

This was no ordinary death. This was the death of Kaptain Brice—veteran of the Northern Offensive, scourge of the Lanyarui coastlines, and shield-bearer of the Empire’s western front. He had not died of illness or age. He had fallen at sea, brought down by a surprise assault from the Gnomish Republic.

It was the first major loss of an officer of his prestige in years, and it would not be answered with silence. The Hall of Ash was dressed in glory—banners of House Brice, banners of the Kledgelony, and above all, the crimson-standard of the Empire itself, its stone-dragon sigil crowned in gold and obsidian.

At the center lay no body—there had been none to recover—but a great altar bore Brice’s shattered breastplate, his command medallion, and his cube of record. Behind it stood his wife, Denice, dressed in the ceremonial armor of her station, and at her hip: their daughter, Chione, wrapped in a blue shawl and watching the world with wide, unblinking eyes.

The hall grew still as the last echo of the bell faded, and then, from the shadows of the high arch, came the Emperor: Vercingetorix the Unbroken.

He walked slowly, with neither haste nor pomp, his crimson-trimmed cloak dragging behind him like a banner of fire across snow. His crown of carved onyx gleamed beneath the runelight.

When he reached the altar, he said nothing at first. He stood before the relics of Brice’s life—stone meeting stone—and then turned to face the gathered multitudes. “My people,” he began, his voice low and strong, “today we do not mourn. Today, we remember. And we awaken.” A murmur rippled through the crowd.

“Brice did not die in peace. He did not die in bed. He died with his command at sea, standing between his people and the falsehoods of the Gnomish Republic. They call themselves merchants. Peacemakers. But beneath their glass towers and polite treaties, they hide blades—and we have now seen the sharpness of their betrayal.”

He stepped forward. “We will not answer treachery with compromise. We will not weep and then wait. We are the sons and daughters of Rokesh. We do not forget. We do not forgive without cost.”

Denice stiffened. She had expected this. But hearing it from the Emperor’s lips made her feel the ground shift beneath her boots.

“The world watches,” Vercingetorix said. “And let them see this: we do not falter when struck. We strike back.” A roar erupted from the crowd—soldiers pounding their fists to their chests, officers raising weapons skyward. Even many of the Kedgelony nodded gravely. The people were with him.

The Emperor turned once more to the altar. He reached out a hand—calloused, ringed with stonesteel—and placed it upon the command medallion. “Brice,” he said, not raising his voice, “you were a sword in our hand. Now you are a flame in our memory. May your name be carved upon the Hall of the Thousand. May your blood ignite the forge of vengeance.” He stepped back. The ritual was complete, but the war had only just begun.

That night, after the crowd dispersed and the banners fell still, Denice remained behind. Alone. Chione slept in her lap, unaware that her father’s death had become a prelude to conquest. She looked at the shattered breastplate. The Emperor’s words echoed in her mind. And she whispered—quiet, bitter, resolute—“This was not the world he wanted for her.”

A week later, Denice would vanish from the capital. And so, too, would the child whose name was never spoken in the Emperor’s speech—though she would come to shape the fate of empires.

Chione liked the way the ship rocked her to sleep. The hammocks creaked gently, like someone whispering old songs. Above her, shadows of lanterns danced across the wooden beams, flickering with each breath of sea wind that slipped through the cracks. Somewhere, deep below, the stones that powered the ship’s movement hummed softly—like the pierres d’amour back home, though these ones didn’t sing. She missed the ones that sang. Her mother hadn’t let her bring any of them.

“Too many eyes,” Denice had said. “Too many ways to be found.”

Chione hadn’t asked what that meant. She had learned not to ask things that made her mother’s voice tighten. Instead, she clutched the small ribbon her father had once tied in her braid. It was worn and faded now, but she could still smell salt and metal on it. And something else—something like stone after rain. A memory, maybe. She never asked about him, either.

They had been on the ship for three days. Her mother called it The Gallant Mare, though Chione couldn’t see why. It didn’t look like a mare. It didn’t gallop. It groaned, it creaked, it smelled of rope and old potatoes. But her mother said they were going somewhere safe, “Where the Emperor can’t reach us. Where no one remembers your name.”

Chione had asked, “Which name?”

Denice hadn’t answered.

The ship jerked hard. Chione woke with a gasp, hitting the side of the hammock. Shouting. Heavy boots. Metal striking wood. Voices that didn’t speak the same words the sailors used. Then—her mother’s hand. Swift, firm, wrapping around her wrist. “Stay quiet.”

They ducked low, moving fast across the narrow lower corridor. The sailors were already shouting. Chione heard a crash. A scream. A voice—deep, rough, like rocks grinding. “By order of the Dwarven High Command, this vessel is under seizure!”

She didn’t understand the words, but she understood the fear. Her mother cursed under her breath and yanked open a maintenance hatch. She shoved Chione inside. It was dark. Too small to stand. Smelled like rust.

“Stay here,” Denice whispered, kneeling beside her. “Don’t speak. Don’t move. Not until the ship stops. Not until someone opens this hatch and says your name. Do you hear me?”

Chione nodded, but her lip trembled. “Will you come in too?”

“I’ll follow you. I promise.”

But Denice didn’t follow. Instead, she pulled the hatch almost closed—just enough for Chione to see her face, shadowed by the flickering flame of a wall-lantern. Her mother looked tired. Not the kind of tired from walking or fighting, but the kind Chione saw in statues—still and cracked.

“I love you, snow-heart.” Then she was gone.

The sounds grew louder. Running. Fighting. Something broke. Someone shouted her mother’s name. Then silence. Then marching. And nothing. Chione stayed. She counted the breaths. Counted them again. When she lost count, she started again. She wanted to cry, but her mother had said not to. She bit the ribbon instead.

At some point—she didn’t know when—the ship stopped moving. No one came. The hatch stayed closed. Until hands opened it—not her mother’s hands, but rough, calloused ones that pulled her out into a world of beards and axes and unfamiliar eyes.

A voice muttered, “What in the hollow is a child doing aboard?”

Another: “No mark of war on her. Clean. We’ll take her.”

She tried to speak, but her voice wouldn’t come. She looked past them, scanning faces. Her mother wasn’t there. And deep in her chest, Chione knew— She never would be again.

The Dwarven ship did not sway like the Gallant Mare had. It did not hum. It did not creak. It did not sing. It moved with purpose, like a stone sliding across glass. Its engines were deeper, colder, louder—more like growling beasts than friendly stones. Everything here smelled of oil and iron and salt. The walls were dark metal. The lanterns burned blue.

No one told Chione the ship’s name. She’d asked once. Just once. A Dwarven sailor had looked down at her with eyes the color of lead and said nothing. Not unkind. Not cruel. Just silent.

That was the worst part. The silence. She sat on a crate in the storage bay—her knees pulled to her chest, her ribbon tied tight around her fingers. Someone had given her a bowl of stew the night before. She hadn’t eaten it. It tasted like firewood and water. She waited. And when the waiting became too heavy, she asked the question again, “Where’s my mother?”

The dwarf standing guard near the door didn’t even look at her. She stood, walked closer, tugged at the hem of his armor. “Please. She said she’d follow me.”

Still nothing. “I was in the hatch,” she said, louder now. “She said—she said she’d come. After. She said she’d follow.” The dwarf shifted slightly, but still said nothing. His eyes remained fixed on the door ahead.

“Do you even know her name?” Chione asked, her voice cracking. “She’s tall, and she fights, and she—she’s better than anyone here. You would’ve seen her. You had to.”

No response. The girl shook with a frustration that felt too big for her body. “SAY SOMETHING!” The words bounced off the walls and fell to the floor like broken stones.

At last, the dwarf spoke—just three words: “You should rest.” Then silence again.

That night, Chione curled on the cot in the engine alcove they had given her. The air was cold. The metal vibrated under her spine. She counted again—breaths, thoughts, pieces of ribbon thread. Her mother had said she would follow. She always did. She always had. Except now. Now, no one followed. No one answered. Only the engine did—thrum, thrum, thrum—like a heartbeat too far away.

The thrum had stopped. Chione woke with a jolt, the silence as loud as any alarm. For days, the deep, constant growl of the ship’s engine had been the only sound that never left her. Now it was gone. In its place: voices. Dozens. Maybe hundreds. Barked commands in Dwarven tongues. The rhythmic thud of boots against steel. Chains rattling. Ramps lowering. A horn blew somewhere far above—deep and low, like the call of a beast too old to remember its own name.

She slipped off the cot, feet touching cold metal. The alcove smelled of oil and old steam. Her hands trembled slightly as she tied the ribbon tighter around her fingers. Someone knocked on the bulkhead beside her. “On your feet,” said a voice—not cruel, but clipped. She looked up. A dwarf in an officer’s coat stood in the doorway, armored in polished black and bronze, his beard braided tight with copper wire. His face was stern, unreadable, marked by long years of order and discipline. “We’re docked. Come.”

Chione followed. The corridors were busier than ever before—lines of soldiers in tight formation, engineers hauling crates marked with strange sigils, clerks with clipboards and rune-seals moving like ants in armor. No one looked at her. No one spoke her name. They passed through an arched hatch onto the upper deck.

The sun hit her like a slap. For a moment, Chione blinked furiously, tears stinging her eyes. It had been days—maybe more—since she’d seen sunlight. The sky was pale and cold, streaked with cloud. The port spread out before her, all iron and smoke and stone towers—cranes swung over loading bays, airships hummed in the distance, and massive chains lowered freight down into the waiting hands of customs officers in slate-colored uniforms.

“Where are we?” she asked. The officer said nothing. They moved quickly down the ramp, past more dwarves, past the staring faces of human laborers and gnome traders, past a row of guards with rifles tipped in runed steel. Eventually, they reached a squat stone building near the edge of the docks. The officer opened the heavy door and gestured for her to enter. She hesitated.

He looked down. “Inside. You won’t be here long.”

Chione stepped into a small, windowless office with walls of gray stone and a single wooden desk. A cot had been set in the corner, along with a pitcher of water and a folded blanket. The door shut behind her with a dull thud. She stood still for a moment, then slowly walked to the cot and sat down. The room was quiet. Too quiet. She took the ribbon from her hand, held it in her lap, and stared at the door. *They don’t know what to do with me.* She didn’t know who they were. She didn’t know what they would do. But she knew what her mother had said. *Stay quiet. Wait.* And so she did.

As the footsteps outside rose and fell, and the port of iron moved without her, Chione waited in the little stone room, wrapped in silence, holding the last warmth her mother had left behind.

The door scraped open with a sound like splitting stone. Chione sat still. She had counted the hours. Fourteen since they brought her here. No windows. No clock. Only the slow drip of condensation from the ceiling and the quiet ticking of the rune-stone set on the desk across from her.

The dwarf who entered was older than the rest—his beard graying in streaks of iron, his shoulders broad beneath a polished officer’s coat. His eyes, however, were sharp. Measuring. Curious. He glanced once at her and did not flinch. Of course he wouldn’t. She was unmistakable.

Her skin, veined with pale blue like cracked marble, caught the room’s cold light. Her face bore the angular, canine grace of her kind—short snout, obsidian ridges across the brow, ears twitching faintly at the echo of boots beyond the hall. She was a Kobold. That much required no discussion. It was everything else that didn’t make sense. The dwarf sat.

“I am Command-Scribe Rurik, of the Fourth Coastal Legate. You're aboard a war-seized vessel. You were hidden. You’re alone. And you are—” he gestured, “—not where any Kobold child is expected to be.” Chione said nothing. “Name?”

She hesitated, then said: “Chione.”

Rurik scribbled the name onto a tablet of rune-etched stone. “House?”

“I don’t have one.”

He looked up sharply. “All Kobolds have a house, child. Even stonecastes without noble backing have tribal affiliation. You're no alley-foundling.”

Chione’s claws gripped the hem of her tunic. “I don’t have one.”

“Very well.” He wrote something else.

He leaned back. “Your accent is northern. Korlish. Your bones are lean—military caste, not mining. And your eyes,” he paused, “are imperial.”

Chione stiffened.

“We’ve seen that hue before,” Rurik added, quietly. “In certain families.”

She said nothing.

“Your mother?” he asked.

“She was on the ship.”

“Not when we boarded.”

“She told me to wait. She said she would follow.”

“She didn’t.”

The words sat heavy in the room. Rurik folded his hands. “Did she serve in the KSB? Kedgelony? Was she a metabold?” Chione looked away. He frowned. “She went to great lengths to hide you. You were placed inside a sealed vent shaft. You were guarded by silence spells. That is not the work of a common smuggler’s daughter.” Silence. “Child,” he said, voice lowering, “I need you to tell me who she was.”

“She was my mother.”

He drummed his fingers on the desk. “You realize how serious this is, don’t you? A Kobold child—imperial by appearance—found alone, on a vessel in Gnomish waters, with no documentation, no markstone, and no name but her own?” Chione didn’t move. “We’ve had defectors before,” he said softly. “Spies. Escaped captives. Loyalists to the old cults. Which one are you, I wonder?”

She turned her gaze on him—sharp and sudden. “I’m just trying to do what she told me.”

Rurik nodded slowly, gaze cool. “You’ll be held in Wardship,” he said. “Until we know more.” He stood, but paused before leaving. “One more thing.” She looked up. “Why do you think she hid you so well?”

Chione’s hands tightened around the old ribbon in her pocket. “Because someone worse than you,” she whispered, “was looking for me.”

Rurik didn’t reply. The door shut behind him. And the questions began to pile in the silence.

The carriage rattled over stones for what felt like days. Chione sat on a bench of cold wood, wrapped in a woolen cloak too large for her. Her feet didn’t touch the floor. Opposite her, two dwarves rode in silence—helmets on their laps, short swords at their hips, eyes always watching but never meeting hers.

Outside, it was snowing. She could hear it whispering against the canvas walls of the carriage, soft and steady. The window flaps were tied shut. No sky. No trees. No mountains. Just the creak of wheels, the jolt of the road, and the smell of sweat, pine tar, and wet leather.

She tried to sleep once, but the carriage always jerked just as her eyes closed. So she sat still, hands tucked into her sleeves, ribbon wound tight between her fingers. She didn’t ask where they were going. She already knew no one would answer.

They arrived in the gray light of dawn. The carriage rolled through a gate of stone and iron, past rows of posted dwarven sentries in snow-dusted armor. Chione peeked through the flap: towers carved into the cliffside, low slate buildings, walls lined with frost-covered wards. A place buried beneath cloud and rock. The sign above the gate bore no name. Only a rune etched in old Dwarvish: “**Order Endures.**”

When the carriage stopped, a waiting official opened the door. He wore robes instead of armor, but his face was no softer. “She’s the unclaimed one?” he asked.

One guard grunted. “Name’s Chione. No house. No insignia. Spoke of a mother who vanished at sea. We haven’t confirmed if she was military.”

The official gave a small nod, then turned to her. “This way.”

They crossed a small courtyard. No snow here—just hard stone and thin ice. No trees. No flowers. Only walls, chimneys, smoke, and the distant clang of metalwork. Overhead, ravens circled. The building they entered smelled of parchment and boiled herbs. Children’s voices echoed from far corridors, muffled and low.

He led her down a hall, past locked storerooms and shuttered windows. At last, he stopped before a door and opened it. Inside: a narrow room. A cot, a basin, a single cabinet. No colors. No decorations. Just gray.

“You’ll stay here,” the man said. “This is Sector Three. Breakfast is at first bell. You’ll attend lessons with the others. If you follow the rules, you’ll earn time in the open yard. If you don’t, you won’t.”

Chione nodded.

“Someone will come tomorrow to ask questions,” he added. “Real ones.”

Then he left, and she was alone. She sat on the cot, blanket pulled into her lap. The wind outside howled softly through a slit window too small to crawl through. She didn’t cry. Instead, she unwrapped the ribbon from her sleeve and held it in both hands. The last thing her mother had touched. She stared at it for a long time. And whispered, barely audible: “I'm still here.”

The courtyard was ringed in silence, frost, and eyes. It wasn’t much—just a patch of worn flagstones behind the main hall, fenced by a shoulder-high iron gate and watched by two guards in plated leathers. The children were let out twice a day. Not for play. For movement. For routine.

They were not prisoners, the staff always said; just the children of criminals. So the fences weren’t called fences; they were “boundaries.” Chione didn’t care what they called them; she knew a cage when she saw one.

She walked out alone, a day after arriving, her breath misting in the air. Dozens of others were already there—bundled in patchwork tunics and coats, boots clacking against stone, eyes half-lidded with boredom or cold.

Most were dwarves, squat and scowling, shoulders hunched like the world owed them pain. A few had the narrow, sharp features of gnomes—clever-eyed and twitchy. And there were two or three elven children: tall, quiet, watching everything while saying nothing.

The moment Chione stepped into the yard, the atmosphere shifted. Conversations stilled. One year passed. In a place like the wardship, seasons didn’t change so much as shift in tone. The courtyard turned from frost to rain to dust. The meals stayed gray. The rules stayed strict. Faces aged slowly, grudgingly. The new ones came in scared. The old ones left harder.

Chione changed, too. She didn’t talk much more, but when she did, her words were steady. The road curved like a ribbon of stone through narrowing valleys and rising green. Chione had never seen so many trees. Elandir nodded once. “No child of war should grow to be a weapon simply because no one claimed them.”

That night, as Chione lay curled beneath a blanket made of cloud-fleece, her window open to the rustling leaves of a tree older than the wardship itself, she stared at the ribbon in her hand. For the first time, she wondered if she might live long enough to pass it on.

The village was called Thaloroen, which meant place of branches turned toward the moon. He spoke politely to vendors, nodded to elders, answered greetings when they were offered. But most were not.

When they reached the herb stall near the central fountain, an elven woman with sun-dark skin and gold-threaded braids narrowed her eyes, “You’ve brought her here?”

“She’s with me,” Finn said.

The crowd stilled.

They didn’t speak much on the way home. The seasons passed slowly in Thaloroen, but Chione learned to read them not by weather, but by silence. Silence, she learned, had flavors. There was the silence of Elandir’s study—thick with old books, the scent of ink, and the rustling of ancient pages. There was the silence of morning sparring drills with Finn—broken only by breath, motion, and the occasional laugh when one of them slipped in the dew. And then there was the silence that came the first time she cast frost from her hands. The silence shifted, however, in the third winter.

She had been standing beneath the moonbridge, practicing breathing exercises Elandir had taught her for focus and control. The air had been sharp with cold. A bird had startled from a tree. She flinched—and the world froze. The grass around her frosted in an instant. The stream turned brittle at the edges. Her own hands shimmered with frost that cracked and spread like spiderwebs of snow. She hadn’t meant to do it, but when Elandir arrived minutes later, he didn’t look surprised, he immediately sent for someone.

Her name was Valenra—an old elven spell-weaver who lived alone on the high ridge. She wore robes made of cloud silk and smelled like mint and winter moss. She spoke little. Taught less. But when she placed her hand on Chione’s shoulder and said, simply, “You are of the still-water path,” something in Chione clicked. Ice magic wasn’t about rage. It was about containment; about holding, not bursting; enduring, not exploding. It fit her perfectly.

For ten years, Chione trained. By day, she studied history, diplomacy, and elven language under Elandir’s firm but patient eye. She learned how to speak when silence failed, how to move like a shadow without shame, how to carry herself not like a girl of stone, but a woman of control.

By dusk, she practiced with Valenra—hours spent drawing water from air, sculpting frost-blades that could split bark, conjuring mirrored barriers of ice that shimmered like still lakes. It hurt at first. Magic always did. But she grew into it like a tree growing into its own shape.

And always, Finn was there. They trained together. Ate together. Fought. Laughed. He was the fire beside her cold—the smirk when she forgot how to smile, the hand that caught hers when she stumbled too close to doubt.

People in the village noticed. They didn’t call her outsider anymore. They called her Elandir’s ward; ice-gifted; quiet-spear. Some called her Moon-Daughter, for the way she walked the frozen glades in winter, unflinching.Not all liked her. But most respected her. And that, to Chione, was enough.

By her seventeenth winter of life, Chione stood as tall as many elves, her obsidian-blue skin etched with soft lines of silver rune-ink. Her eyes were sharper now—still like ice, but no longer afraid of their own reflection. She carried herself like someone who had been broken, reforged, and polished in silence. She no longer waited for someone to come find her. Now, she was ready to seek what she had lost. And the world, once so distant, had begun to call her name again.

“I don’t understand it either,” Chione admitted, frowning. “I’ve always been able to feel temperature shifts in stone, sense ice in the earth—but I thought that was part of my frost magic.”

“But it wasn’t,” Thedden said. “You bent rock like a Metabold.”

Grenna raised a brow. “You ever been tested with Snawk venom?”

“No.” Chione shook her head. “Elandir didn’t exactly let me explore my lineage much. And no one suspected me to be... one of them.”

Thedden looked at her with new wariness—and a little awe. “Well, you are. The stone answered you like it answers only a few.”

“I didn’t ask it to,” she said quietly. “I wasn’t thinking. I was scared. And it responded.”

Finn glanced at her. “Sometimes that’s when our true self shows. In the dark.”

Grenna huffed. “Well, whatever happened, you saved us. We’d be dead if not for that outburst.”

Chione gave a faint, tired smile. “I’m glad it helped. But if this really is some sort of Metabold awakening… I’ll need guidance. Training. Something.”

“Well,” said Thedden, gesturing with his staff to the path ahead, “Embercrag has an old apothecary who studied Kobold magic for years. If anyone in this region knows more about this kind of power—and maybe our strange blue-skinned friend too—it’s him.”

Chione nodded. “Then we hurry. We owe her that much.”

The woman stirred faintly in Finn’s arms, her fingers twitching once before falling still again.

Chione looked ahead.

Whatever lay at the end of this road—answers, danger, or something stranger—it had already begun to change her. And it wouldn’t stop here.

The trail wound down out of the forest into a narrow, stone-lined valley where the village of Embercrag nestled like an ember between blackened hills. Thin plumes of smoke rose from squat chimneys, and the scent of dried herbs and damp stone filled the air. The village was quiet but not unfriendly—simple folk here, mostly miners and gatherers, their homes carved partially into the mountainsides.

At the village’s heart stood a crooked building of brick and timber, its windows glowing with orange light and its front sign reading in faded paint: “Auntie Briar – Apothecary, Midwife, & Minor Miracles.”

Finn knocked lightly with his foot, still carrying the unconscious woman. The door creaked open before he could call out. An older woman stood there—short and wiry, with a braid of silver hair draped over one shoulder and a pair of spectacles perched low on her nose. Her eyes narrowed the moment she saw the woman in Finn’s arms.

“Well,” she said, stepping aside with surprising speed, “don’t just stand there with death in your arms. Bring her in!”

They did. The interior was warm, filled with the crackling of a hearth and the tang of dried moss and crushed petals. Shelves lined every wall, stuffed with tinctures, crystals, bones, and bundles of herbs. The apothecary motioned for Finn to lay the girl on a padded bench, already setting to work without waiting for explanation.

She moved with swift confidence, checking pulse, breath, eyes. Muttered under her breath. Slipped a glowing shard beneath the girl’s tongue. Lit incense with a flick of her fingers. At one point, she pricked the woman’s skin and frowned at the blood—dark, almost violet. Still, she didn’t comment. Time passed. Eventually, the girl’s breathing deepened. Her color improved. The strange markings on her skin faded slightly in brightness, though they remained visible. She did not wake.

“She’s stable,” Auntie Briar said at last, wiping her hands on a linen rag. “She was poisoned by corrupted mana and close to a full collapse of spirit and body both. But whatever you fed her down there bought her time.” She looked over her shoulder. “And whoever shook the earth like a thunder god probably cleared out the source.”

Grenna smirked. “That’d be Chione.”

Auntie Briar turned fully, her gaze sharp now. She walked up to Chione and studied her carefully, eyes lingering on the faint silver traces still etched across her hands and forearms.

“Show me,” she said simply.

Chione hesitated—then pressed her palm to the stone floor. A quiet rumble passed through the room. The stones around her hand shifted ever so slightly, reshaping themselves into a perfect ring. The old woman’s eyes gleamed. Auntie Briar studied Chione’s face a moment longer, then gave a quiet nod.

“You’re a Metabold, no question. The stone wouldn’t have answered you like that otherwise. Whatever you stirred down there—that wasn’t just instinct. It was birthright.”

Chione’s fingers brushed her forearm, where the silver traces of earth-magic still faintly glimmered beneath her skin. “I didn’t know,” she said softly. “I’ve always felt something... under the surface. But I thought it was just ice.”

“It’s deeper than frost,” Auntie Briar said. “You don’t just feel the land. You belong to it.”

Grenna crossed her arms, her voice gruff but fond. “Well, it’s a good thing we were already heading to Korland then, isn’t it?”

Thedden gave a small smile. “Looks like the road knew where we needed to go before we did.”

Chione met their eyes—Finn’s steady gaze, Thedden’s cautious reassurance, Grenna’s fierce loyalty—and then looked back to the pale girl resting on the apothecary’s bench.

“We were heading there for answers,” she said. “Now we have more questions—but at least we know where to ask them.”

Auntie Briar gave a wry smile. “Korland’s a hard land. Proud and deep. But if the Kledgelony take you in, they’ll show you how to wield what’s waking in you.”

She glanced at the girl again. “And when your friend wakes, I suspect she’ll have questions of her own. Questions tied to deeper places than you’ve yet walked.”

Chione stepped forward and gently rested a hand on the girl’s arm. “Then we’ll bring her with us. She deserves answers too.”

The party gathered their gear, spirits sobered but unified. The road to Korland still lay long before them—but now they walked it not only with purpose, but with a calling. The land itself had begun to speak, and they were ready to listen.

The party spent the night in Embercrag, and the next morning a gentle drizzle fell over it, softening the air and masking the sound of the party’s footsteps as they approached the apothecary’s crooked little house. The scent of warm herbs and baked bread drifted through the air—Auntie Briar was already up, of course, as she always was.

Chione knocked politely before opening the door. A bell chimed above them as they stepped inside. The hearth crackled steadily. The strange girl lay where they had left her, now covered with a fresh wool blanket. But this time, she wasn’t asleep.

She was sitting up. Her eyes—an eerie shade of violet-gray—watched them with calm wariness. She had pulled the blanket tightly around her thin frame, her silver-white hair now combed and tied loosely behind her shoulders. Her blue-tinted skin shimmered faintly in the firelight, and the tribal markings along her collarbone and arm pulsed ever so slightly—like veins of starlight.

Chione stepped forward. “You’re awake.”

The girl’s eyes narrowed faintly, then flicked between them—Chione, Finn, Grenna, Thedden. She blinked once. Then again. “I... suppose I am,” she said. Her voice was soft, almost melodic—but distant. “But I don’t know who you are.”

Grenna frowned and folded her arms. “We found you collapsed in a fissure. Nearly dead.”

“You’ve been unconscious for two days,” Thedden added. “Some kind of mana poisoning, the apothecary said.”

“I see,” the girl murmured, looking down at her hands. Her fingers trembled slightly. “That explains... this ache. But I don’t remember anything.” She looked up again, eyes uncertain. “Not you. Not how I got there. Not even my name.”

Chione stepped closer, kneeling gently beside her. “It’s alright. You’re safe now. You’re in Embercrag.”

The girl hesitated. “Embercrag...” She repeated the word slowly, as if tasting it. “It doesn’t sound familiar.”

“Then we’ll help you remember,” Chione said firmly. “We’re on our way to Korland. You’re welcome to come with us, if you want to find out where you came from. Or what you are.”

The girl looked to each of them again. This time, something flickered behind her gaze—cautious trust. A fragile thread of connection. “I don’t know where else to go,” she said at last.

Grenna nodded. “Then that settles it.”

Finn smiled faintly. “What should we call you until you remember?”

The girl was quiet for a moment. Then her hand gently brushed the strange black markings across her ribs. “…Call me Mira,” she said at last. “Until I remember something else.”

Chione gave a small, hopeful smile. “Mira it is.”

The road from Embercrag to the coastal city of Duskwatch took five days. The terrain shifted from mossy forests to low, wind-swept hills that rolled toward the gray expanse of the sea. The party traveled on foot, stopping only for brief rests and meals. Mira rode most of the way on a borrowed mule, still weak from her recovery, but stronger with each passing day.

Though she remembered nothing of her past, Mira adapted quickly. She asked thoughtful questions, listened attentively, and seemed especially drawn to Thedden’s discussions of nature and magical balance. Sometimes, she would stop on the trail and simply stare at the sky or the way wind moved through tall grass, as if searching for a memory just out of reach.

Chione, too, was quieter on the road. Her awakening had changed something. At night, while the others slept, she would often press her hands to the ground in meditation, listening to the subtle thrum beneath the soil. The earth no longer ignored her.

When at last they reached Duskwatch, the port city sprawled before them like a cracked shell along the cliffs, its harbor arms reaching into the churning gray waters of the Crescent Sea. Waves crashed constantly against the black-stone piers, and the sky above was streaked with gulls and smoke.

The city was alive—traders shouting over crates, sailors cursing the tide, and Kobold officers inspecting manifests. Duskwatch had become a border town between cultures. Stone-carved Imperial banners hung beside older wooden placards, and its markets were full of goods from both Korre and Korland.

The party made their way through crowded wharfs toward the harbormaster’s post. Ships rocked at anchor—sleek cutters, galleons with Kobold etchings, and one or two older vessels bearing faded federation colors.

Grenna eyed a thick-shouldered man with a weather-worn face and a clipboard hanging around his neck. “Looks like he’s in charge.”

They approached. “Looking to book passage,” Finn said.

“To Korland,” Chione added.

The harbormaster gave them a long look, his gaze pausing briefly on Mira—then Chione. He chewed the edge of his pipe before responding. “Not many heading to Korland,” he said at last. “Especially not this late in the season. Storms’ve been rolling in strong. But…” He flipped through a sea-warped ledger. “One ship’s still bound that way, The Tide’s Mercy, merchant vessel, small crew, leaves at first tide tomorrow.”

Grenna crossed her arms. “What’s the cost?”

“Two gold a head,” the man replied, “but I won’t lie—it won’t be a pleasant ride. Ship’s light, and Korland waters don’t take kindly to the uninvited.”

Chione stepped forward and laid the coin down. “We’re not uninvited.”

The man looked up at her again, gaze lingering. “No,” he said quietly. “I suppose you’re not.”

He stamped a slip and handed it to her. “Show this to the Tide’s Mercy’s quartermaster. Dock seven, sunrise. And may the dragons keep your keel steady.”

As the party turned to go, Mira hesitated by the edge of the dock, watching the churning sea below. She shivered slightly. “Korland,” she whispered. “It sounds… familiar.”

Chione stepped beside her. “Let’s hope it remembers you too.”

Duskwatch’s sea air carried a bite, but the warmth inside the Broken Gull banished the chill. The tavern was carved into the side of the cliff, its walls reinforced with driftwood and salt-stained stone. Nets hung from the beams, alongside rusty harpoons and dragon-toothed trophies. Sailors roared over dice games, a fiddler played a half-forgotten tune by the hearth, and the scent of sea-spiced stew clung to the air.

The party sat around a splintered corner table, drinks in hand—Chione nursing a dark spiced wine, Finn sipping carefully from a clay cup of grain liquor, Grenna with a full tankard of froth-smothered ale, and Thedden with a steaming herbal infusion that smelled faintly of pine. Mira, still hesitant around alcohol, held a mug of watered cider and quietly watched the other patrons, absorbing the scene like someone learning a language.

Grenna leaned back, balancing her chair on two legs. “So,” she said, “what do we expect to find in Korland? Besides sharp stones, proud Kobolds, and food that tastes like dirt.”

“Architecture that outlasts memory,” Thedden replied, swirling his cup. “Temples built from veins of living earth. If the Kedgelony accept Chione, she’ll be trained beneath the Pillars of Resonance.”

Chione raised a brow. “That sounds very dramatic.”

He smiled faintly. “It is.”

Finn leaned forward. “I’ve only read about it in military reports. Korland’s divided—nobility against the cultists, whispers of rebellion. And if Mira’s people are involved somehow…”

They all glanced at her. Mira met their eyes calmly. “I don’t know what I’ll find,” she said. “But I have the feeling Korland is not as far from my past as I’d like it to be.”

Grenna took a long swig. “I’m just excited to see one of those big snake-things you all talk about. The ones they use in the rites?”

“The Snawks,” Thedden muttered. “They’re sacred. Not pets.”

“Oh, I’m not planning to pet one,” Grenna said with a grin. “Just hoping one tries something. Been a few weeks since a good fight.”

They laughed, even Mira—though hers was quiet. Chione looked around the table and allowed herself a rare moment of ease. They were heading into mystery, possibly danger, but tonight, they were warm. Fed. Together.

She raised her cup. “To Korland,” she said.

The others joined her, voices overlapping. “To Korland.”

Dawn rolled over the town wrapped in fog. The waves were quieter than usual, as if the sea itself held its breath. The party stood at Dock Seven, gear packed, weapons oiled, cloaks drawn tight against the sea breeze. Before them floated The Tide’s Mercy, a sleek cutter with a dark hull and reinforced sails of stitched gray hide. She looked fast—and lightly armed.

A tall, broad-shouldered woman with braided gray hair and a flint-colored coat waited at the gangplank. A carved coral dagger hung from her belt, and her eyes—one of them clearly mechanical—scanned the group with brisk scrutiny.

“You the ones for Korland?” she called.

Chione stepped forward. “We are.”

“Then I’m Captain Ysolde. This here’s my ship. She’s lean, swift, and not meant for sightseeing. You’ll sleep on hammocks and eat hardtack when the winds rise.” She nodded toward a red-scaled Kobold who was coiling ropes behind her. “That’s Voski. Don’t make him angry unless you like swimming.” Voski gave them a toothy grin and a mock salute with three fingers.

Ysolde pointed at the deck. “Get aboard. We leave with the turning tide—and we don’t turn around.”

The party exchanged glances. Then they stepped aboard. The ropes were cast off. The wind caught the sails. And The Tide’s Mercy turned its prow toward the sea—toward Korland. Toward fate.

The cutter moved swiftly through the Sea of Teeth, its hull slicing through the waves with purpose. For all her ragged sails and weather-worn deck, The Tide’s Mercy was a fine ship—lean, fast, and surefooted, even under moonless skies.

The first night, the wind howled, and the rain lashed against the deck. Chione stood at the bow, cloak drawn tightly, watching the horizon with furrowed brows. She could feel the tension beneath her feet—not fear, but anticipation. The sea whispered differently here.

Below deck, hammocks swayed with the rhythm of the waves. Grenna snored like a warhorn. Thedden whispered prayers to the wind spirits and rubbed oils into his joints. Mira, unable to sleep, spent long hours watching the sky through a cracked porthole, tracing constellations she couldn’t name.

Captain Ysolde kept to herself at first, tending to charts and the steady rhythm of command. But on the second night, as the sea calmed and lanterns glowed soft along the railings, she sat near the party with a cup of dark rum and a look that spoke more than her words.

“You’re headed to Korland,” she said plainly, her mechanical eye glinting in the lanternlight. “Not many do these days unless they’re smugglers, pilgrims, or fools.”

Chione looked up from where she was seated, her hands wrapped around a clay mug of broth. “We’re travelers,” she said evenly. “Looking for someone. And something.”

Ysolde raised a brow. “That right? Because I’ve ferried enough ‘travelers’ to know when someone’s walking toward something bigger than they admit.”

Grenna met her gaze. “We’re not planning trouble.”

Ysolde gave a small grunt. “I’m not the law. But I like to know if I’m ferrying a curse aboard.” She looked at Mira, whose silver hair shimmered in the low light, then back to Chione. “Or a storm.”

Chione didn’t reply. Neither did Mira. Ysolde let it go with a shrug and returned to the helm, humming a low sea chant that drifted across the deck like fog.

On the third evening, Chione was at the aft rail alone, watching the sea glitter under the waxing moon. Voski approached from the side, moving quietly for someone with stone-hardened feet.

“Cold night,” he said in accented Common, his claws tapping lightly on the railing. “But the sea’s steady. She likes you, I think.” Chione nodded slowly but didn’t turn to face him. Voski continued, more tentative. “I saw the way you moved. The way the earth answered you. You’re one of us.”

“I’m traveling,” she replied, guarded.

He tilted his head. “Stone recognizes stone, Chione. You don’t need to hide it.”

She finally turned, meeting his amber eyes. “I’m not hiding. I’m… still learning.”

Voski’s expression softened. “Then maybe when we reach Korland, you’ll let the mountains finish your sentence.”

She said nothing. After a pause, he dipped his head respectfully and walked away, leaving her alone again under the moonlight. She watched him go, then returned her gaze to the sea. There was comfort in its distance.

The fourth day at sea dawned clear and bright. The wind had shifted in their favor, and The Tide’s Mercy cut through the waves like a blade, her sails full and taut. It should have been a smooth day—until the call came from the crow’s nest:

“Sails ahead! Black-and-gold!”

Captain Ysolde cursed under her breath and snapped her spyglass open. “Imperial Navy. Looks like a cutter squadron.”

The black-and-gold sails of the Kobold Empire’s western fleet soon filled the horizon—sleek, disciplined, and unmistakably official. The ships moved in a slow arc to intercept, their prows shaped like snarling draconic maws. One vessel, slightly larger than the rest, bore the sigil of the Kledgelony’s maritime inspection wing: a serpent coiled around a crystal tower.

Within the hour, the naval cutter Empress's Fang drew alongside them, matching speed with precise ease. A gangplank was lowered with mechanical assistance, and three officers crossed onto The Tide’s Mercy—stone-armored, uniformed, and flanked by two silent Kobold soldiers carrying polearms etched with geomantic runes.

The lead officer, a steel-eyed woman with quartz tattoos along her temple, stepped forward. Her voice was curt, official, and cold. “You are under routine inspection by the Kledgelony Naval Authority. Present identification and travel documentation.”

Ysolde gestured to the party. “Passengers, not crew. Bound for Korland. All legitimate.”

Chione stepped forward and produced the stamped harbor slip from Duskwatch. Thedden handed over their sealed writ of passage signed in Embercrag. Finn produced identification from his ranger charter, and Grenna simply held up a carved bone token that marked her as a former war volunteer.

The officer examined each carefully. Her gaze lingered on Chione’s name, her brow lifting just slightly—but she said nothing. Then her eyes turned to Mira. “And this one?”

The group tensed. “She’s recovering from illness,” Thedden said evenly. “We found her near death. She’s under our protection.”

The officer stepped closer, looking Mira over with professional scrutiny. “She doesn’t have a Federation stamp. No name on your manifest. Her features… are unusual. She’s not elven, is she?” Mira said nothing, eyes downcast.

Chione stepped between them, calm but firm. “She’s recovering. She’s with us.”

Grenna added, “You have our papers. We're not hiding anything that matters.”

A long pause. The officer’s jaw shifted as if weighing something. Then she turned sharply to her guards. “Very well. No violations. But know this—Korland is under internal review. The Emperor does not tolerate chaos on his ancestral soil. Do not stir unrest.” She looked at Mira again. “And watch your companion. The last thing this continent needs is another unknown.” Without another word, they stepped back onto the gangplank and vanished across it.

The moment the Empress’s Fang peeled away from their course, the tension bled out of the crew like steam from a kettle. Captain Ysolde exhaled through her nose. “Knew they’d sniff something out.”

Chione stared after the ship until it was a distant shadow. “They didn’t trust us,” she said softly.

“No,” Thedden agreed. “And something tells me they’ll remember us.”

Finn checked the rigging absently, then looked back at Mira. “We’d best keep a low profile once we land.” Mira wrapped her arms around herself and gave a silent nod. The ocean remained calm, but in each of their hearts, a cold undertow had begun to form.

By the dawn of the sixth day, the cliffs of Korland rose from the morning mist like the bones of the world. Gray and green, sharp and unyielding, they loomed over the sea with an ancient, silent power. Towering spires of basalt arched toward the sky, riddled with bridges and carved terraces where birds circled like living sigils. Veins of glowing mineral ran through the stone like starlight trapped in flesh—geomantic lifeblood of the land. Even Mira, who remembered nothing, stared as though something old and familiar stirred deep within her bones.

“This,” Thedden whispered, “is Vael’Roch—the gateway to Korland.”

The Tide’s Mercy cut its speed as she entered the harbor inlet, which resembled less a bay and more a jagged canyon of black stone shaped by tectonic hands. A massive arch of obsidian rose above the inlet, engraved with circular runes that shimmered faintly with Kobold magic. Beneath it, the sea passed into the harbor proper—a sprawling stone basin carved directly into the cliffs.

Every structure seemed grown rather than built—shaped from living rock by geomancers of old. Pillars twisted like tree roots. Docks rose on crystal-laced platforms. Kobold ships, sleeker and heavier than anything from Korre proper, sat in still water like silent predators. Above it all stood the Citadel of Earth’s Embrace, a fortress-palace of hanging gardens, stone towers, and slowly turning monoliths suspended mid-air. This was not a city made to dazzle—it was a city made to endure.

Captain Ysolde steered them into berth with practiced ease. As the gangplank dropped, she turned to the party one last time. “Welcome to Korland,” she said. “If you make it back to Hurly in one piece, tell them Ysolde doesn’t sail into myth—she lands it.”

Grenna smirked and clapped her on the shoulder. “Best captain I’ve ever not been thrown overboard by.”

Voski gave Chione a small nod as she passed. “The stone is louder here. You’ll hear it if you listen.”

Chione nodded but said nothing, gaze fixed ahead. They stepped onto the dock. Immediately, a reception patrol approached—four guards in black-and-rust colored armor, each bearing the crest of the Kledgelony, the religious-political caste that governed Korland. Their armor bore not only rank sigils but engraved scripture from Rokesh’s covenant. They did not raise weapons, but their presence was rigid and ceremonial.

The lead guard, a noble-faced Kobold with a band of violet crystal woven into his helm, gave a shallow bow. “You are the travelers registered from Duskwatch?” he asked, voice even but firm.

Chione stepped forward and held up their writ. “We are.”

His eyes lingered on her face, then dropped to the faint silver tracing still visible on her forearm. His brows lifted ever so slightly. “…You are of the blood,” he said, almost reverently. “Then the earth knew before we did.”

He gestured for them to follow. “The Kledgelony will wish to receive you.”

The group exchanged glances—uncertain, wary, but resolved. Chione stepped forward. “Then take us to them.”

The Kledgelony guard led them through Vael’Roch’s lower levels, away from the bustling merchant tiers and into the heart of the city’s sanctum. The stone underfoot changed subtly as they walked—shifting from rough-hewn basalt to polished black marble shot through with amber veins that glowed faintly with geomantic resonance.

They arrived at a towering gate of petrified roots and twisted rock, its surface carved with ancient symbols older than the written languages of Korre. Pillars flanked it—each depicting a past Metabold locked in communion with the earth: some triumphant, some broken, all changed.

“This is the Hall of the Stoneborne Echoes,” the lead guard said. “The Kledgelony await her.”

He turned to Chione. “You alone may enter. The trials of the earth are not meant for those who have not heard its voice.”

Grenna’s mouth opened to protest, but Chione touched her shoulder gently. “It’s alright,” she said. “I need to do this.”

Finn gave a quiet nod. “We’ll be right here.”

Mira stood with arms wrapped around herself, silent but watching intently.

The gate creaked open—not by hinges, but by command. The marble parted like roots withdrawing into soil. Chione stepped forward into the dark, and the doors sealed behind her with a deep, resonant thrum.

The chamber beyond was vast and circular, domed in shimmering layers of stone and crystal. Natural light did not reach here, yet the walls pulsed with inner light, like veins of fire deep within the world. The air smelled of petrichor and copper, and every step Chione took echoed like the earth was listening. At the far end stood the Kledgelony: seven figures, robed in copper-threaded cloth and masked in carved stone. Each mask bore a different rune, representing aspects of Rokesh—Weight, Patience, Rupture, Binding, Memory, Burden, and Inheritance.

The center-most figure stepped forward, removing her mask. Her face was etched with age and wisdom, and her eyes gleamed with mineral light. Her voice was deep and solemn. “Child of frost and stone,” she intoned, “you walk the old path unbidden—but not uncalled.”

Chione bowed instinctively, unsure if she was following a rite or simply listening to her heartbeat. “I didn’t know what I was,” she said. “Not until I felt the earth answer me. It saved my friends. I… need to understand it.”

The woman nodded slowly. “The stone does not answer by chance. It knows those who carry its breath in their blood. You are of the line of Brice, honored kaptain, death reaching him early. You bear the signature of venom—but it lies dormant no longer.”

Another robed figure stepped forward. This one’s voice was softer, more intimate. “And yet, you are not raised in our rites. Your power is raw. Dangerous. If left untended, it will unmake you as easily as it shields you.”

Chione lifted her chin. “Then teach me.”

The elders exchanged glances. Then the first one raised a hand. “We will. If you are willing to undertake the Stonewake Rite. A journey beneath the bones of Korland, where the true voice of Rokesh speaks.”

Chione hesitated only a moment, then nodded. “I am.”

The woman stepped back. “Then it begins.”

Outside, the others waited in silence. Mira’s gaze remained fixed on the sealed gate. “She’s been in there a long time,” she said softly.

Thedden folded his arms. “Kobold tradition doesn’t rush. Especially not with power like hers.”

Grenna grunted. “Still don’t like this. Feels like they’re deciding something for her.”

Finn said nothing. He was watching the guard—the same one who had escorted them—who now stood at the edge of the stairs, glancing at them just a little too often. A bad feeling lingered in the air. Like a stone just beginning to crack.

The chamber shifted. At the elder’s gesture, the central dais split open in concentric rings, revealing a staircase of glistening obsidian descending into darkness. Faint whispers rose from below—like wind over stone, but layered with meaning. Chione couldn’t understand the words, but she felt them. They pulled at her bones.

The lead elder handed her a small token—a disk of polished shale, carved with the rune of Inheritance, glowing faintly where it touched her skin. “You will descend alone,” the elder said solemnly. “Speak to no one. Take no light. Trust only the stone.”

Chione’s heart beat faster. “What am I meant to find down there?”

The elder’s reply was quiet, but firm. “Yourself.”

With a breath, Chione stepped forward. The stairs groaned beneath her feet—not from weight, but from recognition. The moment she crossed the threshold, the stone disk warmed in her palm, and the light above dimmed. The gate resealed above her, plunging her into subterranean stillness.

The spiral stairs closed behind her, and Chione was swallowed in warm, resonant dark. The air buzzed—not with sound, but with intention. The deeper she went, the more the stone pulsed with a presence that felt both vast and intimate. The light from her escort token faded, yet her path remained visible—not through sight, but through feeling. The stone guided her feet. The earth did not need to see her—it knew her.

At last, she emerged into a hollowed chamber. It was natural and yet impossibly precise—walls shaped in spirals and intersecting domes, crystals hanging like stars from the ceiling. In the center lay a pool of still, mirror-dark liquid. The reflection it held was not her own. She stepped toward it.

The pool shimmered—and formed two Chiones: On the left stood a woman crowned in geomantic regalia, her robes inlaid with quartz and stone. Her face was calm, proud, and powerful. The markings across her skin glowed like constellation lines, and the ground beneath her responded to every breath. She stood alone. Behind her were statues of cities raised, wars ended, and lands healed. But no living figures walked beside her. No faces in memory smiled. She had become more than mortal—and less than kobold.

On the right stood another Chione—shoulders hunched, armor battle-worn, face dirtied with tears and ash. But behind her stood Finn, wounded but alive; Grenna, one arm in a sling and laughing; Thedden, weary but smiling; Mira, hand on her shoulder. This Chione bled. She carried sorrow in her gaze. Behind her stood burned fields, ruined villages, and graves. She had not stopped the suffering. But she had never left her friends.

Two paths. Two truths. The stone whispered: “Choose.” One offered greatness without connection. The other offered love that would cost everything.

Chione stood between them, hands trembling. “I won’t choose,” she whispered. “Not like this.”

The ground shifted. “You must.”

She clenched her fists. “There’s always a third path. I refuse to believe power demands isolation, or that love guarantees ruin.” She stepped between the reflections.“I will walk the space between. I will try to do both.”

The pool went still. The chamber dimmed. The images faded—not vanished, but withdrawn. The ground no longer welcomed her steps. It observed them. And it did not answer.

The gate to the chamber opened hours later. Chione emerged—steady, marked with a subtle lattice of earth-veins glowing faintly along her neck and wrists, but her eyes held neither triumph nor loss.

The seven masked elders waited in silence. The lead figure stepped forward. “Have you heard the voice of Rokesh?” she asked.

“I have,” Chione replied.

“Have you chosen your stone?”

Chione paused, then spoke clearly. “No. I have chosen the path between stones.”

The air in the chamber shifted. A few elders looked to one another. “You reject the Trial?” asked the Mask of Inheritance, voice unreadable.

“I reject its limit,” Chione answered. “There is no truth in either path if it denies the other. I won’t sacrifice my soul for power. Nor my power for passivity. I will walk the middle way. Forge it if I must.”

The lead elder’s expression hardened. “That path is untested. Dangerous.”

Chione met her gaze. “So was yours—once.”

The silence that followed was long and cold. At last, the elder raised her hand.

“The Rite is incomplete. The bond is unstable. But the earth will judge your steps in time.” She turned to the others. “Let her walk her chosen way. And let the stone remember what she refused.”

The circle parted. And Chione walked out—alone.

The stone doors slid open with a heavy thrum, and Chione stepped into the open air. The warm light of Korland’s late afternoon cast long shadows across the courtyard, where her companions waited in quiet anticipation. They rose immediately at the sight of her.

Grenna was the first to speak, arms crossed but eyes searching. “Well? You alright?”

Chione gave a faint smile. “I’m here.”

Thedden’s eyes flicked to the faint lattice of glowing lines still visible beneath her skin. “You… look changed.”

“I am,” she said simply. “But I’m still me.”

Finn approached, gaze steady. “Did the trial go as expected?”

Chione hesitated, then she shook her head. “I don’t want to talk about it. Not yet. There’s too much I don’t understand.” They respected the boundary. The group fell quiet.

Then Mira stepped forward. She hadn’t spoken much all day—unsettled by the city’s stoneward glances and the silent awe Korlandians showed Chione. But now she looked at her with something like hope.

Chione reached out and took her hand gently.

“Whatever I faced down there,” she said, “one thing became clear: we need to help you next.”

Mira blinked. “Me?”

Chione nodded. “Your memory. Who you are. Why you were in that fissure. Why your body reacts to corrupted mana and dreamstone like it remembers something you don’t.”

Grenna exhaled and rubbed her temples. “Finally. Someone’s saying it. She’s not just some lost traveler.”

“She's a question,” Thedden added softly, “that might rewrite a few truths we thought were settled.”

Chione looked to the others, voice firm.

“The Kledgelony won’t help us with this. They’ve judged me already. So we’ll find answers ourselves.”

Finn raised an eyebrow. “And where do we start?”

Chione turned toward the carved eastern archway, where the city gave way to mountain roads.

“There’s a temple archive two days from here—built into the cliffs above the Stoneveil River. Older than the empire. Some say it holds texts that even the Kledgelony have forgotten.”

She looked at Mira. “Maybe something there will recognize you. Or maybe you'll recognize yourself.”

Mira’s voice was quiet, but steady. “And if I don’t?”

Chione gave her hand a gentle squeeze. “Then we keep looking.”

The road north from Vael’Roch wound like a scar through Korland’s interior—carved from obsidian-veined stone, shadowed by cliffs that whispered with wind and old song. For two days, the party traveled in relative silence. Chione remained distant, still turning over the unresolved trial in her mind. The others followed her lead, each preoccupied with questions no one could yet answer.

Mira seemed the quietest of all. Though she walked without complaint, something gnawed at her—moments where she’d pause mid-step, eyes distant, breath caught as if on the cusp of memory… but always falling just short. Chione watched her closely, gently guiding her forward.

They camped on the third night beneath an overhang of crystalline basalt overlooking a narrow valley. A slow river wound far below. The fire was low. The air crisp. They slept under the stars. And in the deep hours, something moved.

It began with a crack—not of wood, but of stone fracturing beneath weight. Finn was the first to stir, hand already moving to his bow—but he didn’t reach it in time. From the shadows stepped a figure cloaked in flowing black and gold, their armor etched with serpentine runes. No sound announced their approach. Only the scent of scorched stone and crushed crystal clung to the air. Then came the second—and the third.

Three masked agents. One crouched by Mira before she even opened her eyes. Another held Thedden aloft with a wall of rising rock. Grenna shouted and swung her axe—but it struck only mist. She was thrown backwards into the cliff wall, the wind knocked from her lungs.

Chione’s eyes flashed with instinct. She summoned a wall of stone—but the lead agent countered her spell with a flick of the wrist. The earth ignored her. *He’s stronger than me.*

“Don’t fight,” the masked figure said, voice mechanical and distorted. “This is a sanctioned extraction.”

“Who are you?” Chione demanded, forcing herself upright even as blood trickled from her temple.

The mask tilted toward her. “We are the IKSS. You know what that means.”

She did. The Imperial Kobold Secret Service—the hidden arm of the Emperor. Metabold masters trained for assassination, retrieval, and containment. Ghosts in the stone. Executioners in the name of order.

“She’s innocent!” Chione shouted. “She hasn’t hurt anyone!”

“She’s unknown,” the agent replied. “And the unknown is a threat.”

Mira struggled as another agent pinned her arms. “No! I haven’t done anything!”

“You don’t know what you’ve done,” the agent corrected coldly.

Chione lunged—but the lead agent sent a shockwave through the stone that rippled up her spine and collapsed her knees.

“Do not follow,” he said, stepping backward with Mira in his grip. “The Emperor has questions.” With a twist of his hand, the earth swallowed them. In seconds, they were gone.

The fire hissed low in the silence that followed. Finn was crumpled beside the embers, blood streaked across his face. Thedden groaned from where he lay, crushed beneath gravel. Grenna, battered but breathing, punched the stone beside her. Chione sat motionless, her fists clenched so tightly her palms bled. They had taken her. And not even the earth had listened.

Morning came slowly. The fire had burned to embers, the sky above turning a bruised gray. The shattered remains of their camp lay scattered across the rock—bedrolls torn, gear flung, stones cracked where magic had struck. The wind was hollow, carrying the echoes of failure.

Finn sat with one arm in a sling, quietly fletching a broken arrow. Thedden tended to Grenna’s bruised ribs with trembling hands, whispering a half-prayer more out of habit than hope. Chione stood at the cliff's edge, arms folded tight, eyes fixed on the horizon.

“They took her,” Grenna growled. “They took her and we let them.”

“No one let them do anything,” Thedden snapped, sharper than he meant to. “It was the IKSS, Grenna. They didn’t just beat us—they humiliated us. We weren’t ready. We’re still not.”

Grenna sat up, wincing. “Then what, we wait? Pretend it didn’t happen?”

“No,” said Chione, her voice low. “But we also don’t go running blind into the jaws of the Empire.” She turned to face them. Her eyes were hard—but focused. “I want her back too. But think. Why take Mira? Why now? Why in Korland—where they could’ve watched us quietly for days instead of risking a strike?”

Thedden nodded slowly. “There’s something about her. Something they recognized.”

Chione gestured toward the north. “That archive—the one we were heading to—it’s still our best lead. If Mira’s tied to anything the Empire fears, it’ll be in records older than the current regime.”

Grenna scowled. “We’re just going to let her sit in some prison cell while we go look through scrolls?”

“No,” Chione said. “We’re going to arm ourselves—with knowledge. Then, when we do move, we’ll do it right.”

She looked at each of them in turn. “We couldn’t stop them. But we can still outthink them.”

Finn tightened the strap on his sling. “Then let’s start by not wasting daylight.”

The party packed in silence, and with heavy hearts—but sharpened minds—they turned their steps once more toward the mountains, toward the ancient archive above the Stoneveil River. They would find out who Mira was, and then they would take her back.

The Stoneveil Archive did not welcome them. It loomed like a broken crown over the edge of a cliff, built into the sheer rockface where the Stoneveil River cascaded into a mist-wrapped gorge. The approach was narrow—half-forgotten stairs chiseled into the stone, worn smooth by wind and time. Moss grew between runes no one had read in centuries.

The main doors were not locked. They simply waited. Inside, the archive was colder than expected—lit by shafts of light from high windows and faint glows from aged crystal sconces. A silence hung there, thick with memory and dust. Shelves upon shelves of carved-stone tablets, etched vellums, and brittle tomes stretched into the gloom.

“This is older than any Kobold temple I’ve seen,” Thedden murmured. “Some of these scripts predate the Kledgelony itself.”

The archivist—an old, half-blind Kobold named Lareth du Moltren—hardly seemed surprised by their request. He simply waved a gnarled claw toward the back wing and muttered, “Look where the dust gathers thickest. The ones you want were buried long before the Emperor ever drew breath.” They searched for days.

Grenna combed through warfare logs, trying to trace patterns in imperial conflict. Finn scanned poetic texts for hidden records in metaphor. Thedden translated older magical treaties, often struggling with dead dialects. Chione searched alone—following not logic, but instinct. Her stone-marked hand often brushed along the walls, as if something buried might call to her the way it once had in the trial chamber, but nothing came.

Days passed. Mira’s absence was a weight at every meal, every quiet moment. None of them said it aloud, but the fear was there—*What if we never find anything? What if we’re already too late?* Then, on the fifth day, in the deepest chamber of the archive, Chione found it.

She pulled a layered tablet from a rusted slot—two pieces of slate bound by ancient wax sealant. The script was archaic, spiraling inward like a labyrinth. She brought it to Thedden, who turned pale as he translated aloud.

“When the Shadow Twin rose, she brought forth her children not of flesh, but of memory. They were not born—they were shaped: of dream, of dusk, of silence given form. The Twili, she named them. And in the twilight they took root.”

The room went still. Thedden continued reading, voice barely above a whisper.

“Feared by the sun-children, they were hunted, exiled, sealed in realms between night and breath. Not for crimes committed, but for what they were: echoes of Erosia’s sorrow and mirrors of her truth.”

Grenna leaned back slowly. “Twili. That’s what she is.”

Finn frowned. “I thought the Twili were myth. Just a poetic name for shadow elves or dusk spirits.”

Chione shook her head. “Not myths. Not anymore.” She tapped the end of the text.

“It is said that when the Twilight Realm opens once more—when a soul of light walks willingly into shadow—Erosia’s children shall return. And with them, the balance long denied.”

Silence.

Then Thedden spoke, voice heavy. “The IKSS didn’t just take Mira because she was unknown. They took her because she’s a prophecy.”

Mira awoke in a room of smooth obsidian and humming quartz. No windows. No visible door. The walls pulsed faintly with geomantic veins—like arteries, living and slow. The air was warm, dry, and thick with the scent of powdered stone and sterilized metal. Her wrists were bound in soft restraints etched with runes—not to hold her body, but to suppress something deeper.

She sat on a cold, stone chair at the center of a circular chamber. A single thin beam of white light illuminated her face. She didn’t remember how she got here. Only pain, motion, and darkness. Then, a soft scrape echoed as a figure emerged from the wall itself, as though shaped from it. He was tall, draped in black and bronze robes. His mask was polished stone shaped like a serene, smiling face—without warmth.

IKSS. The same voice that had crushed the camp now spoke with velvet precision. “Ah. You’re awake.”

Mira said nothing.

The agent stepped closer. His hands were clasped behind his back like a tutor pacing a classroom. “Do you know where you are?”

Still nothing.

The smile on the mask did not change. “No? That’s alright. You’ve been here before.”

Mira blinked. “What?”

“Hmm.” He circled her now, steps slow and deliberate. “I suppose it’s fair that you don’t remember. That was the point, wasn’t it?”

He paused behind her. “You were never meant to wake up before the integration was complete.”

Mira’s breath caught. “Integration?”

“Ah. There it is. Curiosity.” The agent moved to face her again. “You’ve escaped before, you know. That’s how you were found in the fissure. That place was an overflow site—half-sealed, bleeding corrupted energy. And yet there you were, clinging to life like a stubborn flame.” He tilted his head.

“Where are your manners, Mira?” he asked softly. “After all, we rescued you. We freed you from the Twilight. From the night that birthed you.”

Mira’s voice came slow, wary. “You call this freedom?”

He chuckled. “You’d prefer exile in the dark? Forgotten in a realm that doesn’t know time, abandoned by Erosia and her broken dream?”

“You don’t know anything about her.”

“On the contrary.” He leaned forward. “The Emperor knows all. And we—I—am merely a sculptor. You were a shattered idol, Mira. A lost fragment of Erosia’s design. But we have remade you. Rewritten your blood to serve the light.” He stood and began pacing again. “The question is… how much of the old self is still inside you? How deep does the shadow run?”

Mira closed her eyes. The room seemed to pulse—faintly. She could feel the bindings suppressing her power. But something deeper stirred. The agent stopped suddenly, his tone sharpening. “Tell me, Mira. Do you dream?”

She met his gaze now—quiet, trembling, but defiant. “…Yes.”

He stood still for a long moment. Then turned. “Good. Let us see what else you remember.” The walls dimmed, and the chamber rumbled.

The sun had dipped behind the high ridges of the Stoneveil, casting long shadows across the canyon and filling the archive’s chambers with a cold, amber light. The old crystal sconces sparked to life with faint magical glow, but the air inside remained heavy, filled with a tension none of them could shake.

The tablet lay in the center of their worktable, its spiral inscriptions still fresh in their minds: Twili… shaped of dream and dusk… prophecy of return.

Chione stood with arms crossed, staring at the far wall as if willing a solution to appear.

Finn leaned on the table beside the tablet. “If Mira’s one of these… Twili,” he said slowly, “then she wasn’t just taken because she was unknown. They knew exactly what she was.”

“And if she really did come from the Twilight Realm,” Thedden added, “it means the Empire is tampering with things they once feared enough to seal away forever.”

Grenna paced behind them, boots clunking heavily on the stone floor. “Twili. Prophecy. Shadows. Fine. But I don’t care if she’s made of moonlight and old poems—I care that they took her.”

The room went silent for a breath.

Chione turned around. Her face was calm, but every word came with weight.

“We have to get her back.”

Grenna nodded immediately. “Finally.”

But Thedden hesitated. “Chione… it was the IKSS. The Imperial Kobold Secret Service. We're not just talking about a rogue commander or some overzealous governor. These are elite agents with clearance to do anything in the Empress’s name.”

Chione met his gaze. “I know.”

“I don’t think you do.” Thedden’s voice lowered. “They don’t make arrests. They make things disappear.”

Finn looked up. “Which is why we don’t face them directly—not yet.”

Chione nodded. “We can’t charge in blind. We did that once. We lost. This time, we need to understand what we’re up against.”

She gestured to the tablet. “This archive goes deeper than we’ve explored. There might be more texts—about the Twili, about the sealing of Erosia, about how the Empire learned to breach the Twilight at all.”

Grenna scowled. “So more reading, then?”

“For now,” Chione said. “Every piece of history we uncover is another weapon they won’t expect us to have.”

Thedden exhaled and rubbed his temples. “And when we do move?”

Chione’s voice was steady.

“Then we’ll know exactly where to strike.”

However, the deeper into the archive they went, the older the records became—and the less consistent: tablets written in serpentine, archaic Draconic contradicted scrolls penned centuries later in Imperial script. Each document painted the Twili differently. No single truth emerged—only the sense of a fractured history, deliberately obscured, like dust swept to cover bloodstains.

Finn sat on a high ledge, flipping through a book with brittle pages etched in silver ink. “This one claims the Twili were forged by Erosia herself—woven from the breath between thoughts and the space between stars. They weren’t born—they arrived.”

Grenna tossed down a stone-bound war journal. “And this one says they were mistakes—abominations birthed by Golestandt in a moment of defiance, made to counter Quint’s light. It says they were emotionless, cold. Parasites.”

Thedden stood before a mural—half-effaced by time—showing shadowy forms drifting like vapor between cities, half-blended with walls, fountains, even people’s silhouettes. His voice was hushed.

“They could become shadows,” he said. “Slip into them. Travel through them. Possess those who stood too long in their own. Some sources say they could even… unravel souls.”

Chione knelt beside a cracked relic bound in binding rings of petrified bone. She stared at a passage freshly translated: “To stand near a Twili is to doubt one’s own mind. They speak rarely. They touch dreams. And when they walk, they do so across the backs of the unwary.”

She closed the tome, her voice level. “And yet some records show them as healers. Teachers. Guides who only turned against the world after being betrayed.”

Thedden gestured at another text. “Others say it was the world that turned first. Feared them. Branded them monsters before they'd lifted a hand.”

Grenna snorted. “So which is it, then? Ghosts, gods, or demons?”

“No one agrees,” Finn muttered. “But they all feared them.”

A heavy silence fell over the chamber. Then Chione spoke, her voice colder than before. “The husks.”

Thedden looked up. “What?”

She rose to her feet, holding a narrow codex in both hands. The text had been hidden behind a panel in the floor, buried with care.

“This one says the Twili didn’t just create the husks. They became them—when they were wounded beyond repair. Mindless remnants of their consciousness, driven by shadow and silence. Used for war. For terror. Entire cities fell without ever seeing a single attacker.”

Grenna muttered under her breath. “So... Mira’s one of them.”

“No,” Chione said firmly. “She’s not a husk. She fought to stay herself.”

Finn leaned forward. “But that’s what scares me most. If the Twili could do all this—become all this—then why would the Empire want her back? Why risk reopening a door sealed in legend?”

Thedden closed his book with a shaking hand. “The Twilight was sealed for a reason. And now… it’s been punctured. Ripped open, even if just enough to let one through.”

Grenna frowned. “Then what does Mira have to do with it?”

Chione’s eyes were distant. Her voice was heavy with realization. “Maybe she didn’t escape the Twilight.” She looked at the others. “Maybe she was sent.”

The room fell still. Chione opened the codex again, turning to the final passage: “The children of Erosia return not as conquerors, nor as servants, but as seeds—planted in foreign soil, watered by fear, and harvested by those who think themselves gardeners of fate.”

She looked up. “The Empire didn’t just find her,” she said. “They harvested her. And we need to find out what they’re growing.”

The party continued deeper. The deepest vault of the Stoneveil Archive was not listed in any directory. Chione had found its sigil etched faintly behind a broken pillar: a rune of twilight and root, half-buried beneath lichen. It took them down a winding staircase, behind a door that hissed open not with keys—but with presence.

There, lit only by a single lantern of shimmering mica, sat the oldest Kobold they had ever seen. His skin was like shale—cracked, black-gray, and dusted with fine sediment. Quartz jutted from his brow like horns worn by time. He sat cross-legged on a heap of furs, wrapped in layers of ancient cloth embroidered with symbols none of them could read.

He did not stir when they entered. “I was wondering,” he said in a low, gravel-thick voice, “when the dust would bring truth-seekers back to me.”

Thedden stepped forward carefully. “You knew we were coming?”

“No,” the Kobold said, one eye half-lidded and the other glazed with a film of crystal. “But I knew that someone would. They always come—when the world forgets too much.”

Chione stepped forward, the glow from her earth-marks catching in the lantern light. “Who are you?”

“I am Voth,” he said. “Once a flamekeeper of the Khel’Khal tribes. Once a memory-holder before the Stone Crown. Now? Now I am rot with a name.”

Grenna frowned. “You were alive before Vercingetorix?”

Voth smiled, and it was a dry, mossy thing. “I walked the rivers when the tribes still quarreled over spirit-stones and soil. I watched Vercingetorix take the mantle of the Chosen—and I watched the scribes begin rewriting.” He leaned forward. “So tell me, children of question: what unclean truth do you dig for?”

Chione did not hesitate. “The Twili. What they were. What they are. Why the Empire wants one.”

At the word, something changed in Voth. His smile faded. His tone lowered into something reverent. “You found one, didn’t you?”

Chione nodded slowly. “She was taken. By the IKSS.”

The old Kobold closed his eyes and hummed—something between a mourning song and a chant. “Then the harvest begins again.”

“What do you know?” Thedden asked.

Voth took a deep breath. And spoke. “They were not monsters. They were not rebels. The Twili were gift.” His voice rang gently through the stone. “When the world was still raw and damp with dragon-breath, each Primal gave life to balance. Light for day. Fire for forging. Ice for stillness. Earth for strength. Arcane for wonder. And then came Golestandt. Most think he birthed decay. Shadow. Fear. That he made beasts for the dark. But Golestandt, the Dragon of Darkness, was not cruel. He was lonely. His realm was the space between others—between moments, between truths. And from that loneliness, he shaped the Twili.” Voth’s hand moved slowly over the floor, tracing invisible lines. “They were meant to be the people of the quiet. Not of evil, but of between. Dwellers of dusk, as elves dwell in day. They were to be the complement to all others. Observers, dreamers, walkers of forgotten paths., but the people of the day saw them emerge from shadow—and recoiled.”

Thedden whispered, “They saw a threat.”

“Yes,” Voth said. “And when fear grew louder than wonder, they were hunted. Called deceivers. Thieves of warmth. Plague upon flesh.”

“Erosia,” Chione murmured. “Where does she fit into this?”

Voth’s gaze sharpened. “She saw the injustice. She did not make them—but she loved them. She walked among them, veiled in twilight, and listened to their mourning. When the fires of persecution came, she gathered them close, hid them in veils of dusk, and spoke a promise: ‘I will never let them burn.’ But her pity became her crime,” he continued. “The people called it betrayal. Quint turned away. The dragons did not intervene. And so, the war began—not of conquest, but of protection. Erosia led her Twili not to take the world—but to carve a space where they could exist.”

Grenna exhaled slowly. “And for that, they sealed her away.”

“Yes,” Voth said. “They call it justice. I call it silence.”

The room was silent.

Finally, Finn asked, “And Mira? Why would the Empire want her?”

Voth’s expression hardened. “Because they have spent the last hundred years trying to reach the Twilight. To study it. To own it.” He leaned forward, voice grave. “If they brought a Twili through… it means they succeeded.”

“And if she still remembers who she was?”

“Then she is not just their experiment,” Voth said. “She is their weapon… or their curse.”

The lantern's glow flickered as Voth drew breath through cracked lips. The party remained motionless, listening to the last of his tale echo through the chamber like wind through ancient stone. Silence followed—dense and reverent. Then Voth shifted with a grunt and reached behind him. From beneath his furs and woven prayer-robes, he produced a thin roll of parchment sealed in wax so old it crumbled at the edges. He held it out toward Chione. “This map was not made by the Empire,” he said. “It was carved by hands who remember the world before it was tamed.”

Chione accepted the scroll and unrolled it with care. The parchment was dry, but the lines were vivid—etched in a faded blue pigment that shimmered faintly when turned in the light. The map showed the surrounding countryside of Vael’Roch and the Stoneveil, but near its edge—carved between hills and granite veins—was a circle marked in ancient runes.

Finn leaned closer. “What’s there?”

Voth’s voice was steady. “An outpost. Hidden. Guarded. Beneath the ground, shrouded by veins of shadowstone. Built atop an old Twili shrine—the kind the Empire once claimed to destroy. They didn’t destroy this one. They buried it. And now they use it.”

Thedden frowned. “Why would the IKSS operate out of a forgotten ruin?”

“Because no one dares ask what lies in places the Empire says are cursed,” Voth replied. “And because the stone there still remembers the Twilight. It pulses differently. It listens.”

Grenna already had her hand on the pommel of her axe. “Then we go in, take down the guards, and get Mira out.”

“No,” Voth said, shaking his head slowly. “You go there… and wait.”

Grenna narrowed her eyes. “Wait? Wait for what? We don’t wait, old one, we fight.”

Thedden looked uneasy as well. “We barely survived the IKSS last time. Waiting just gives them time to move her again.”

Chione, silent until now, fixed her gaze on Voth. “You know something.”

The old Kobold smiled softly. “I know faith.”

He tapped his forehead with a claw. “The Empire might call it superstition, but we who walked under moonless sky remember differently. The Twilight may be hidden, sealed, suppressed—but it is not gone.” He pointed to the circle on the map.

“That place resonates with the old magic. If Mira is awakening—if she is calling to Erosia, even unconsciously—then the Mourning Queen will answer.”

Grenna raised an eyebrow. “So your plan is: sit in the grass and hope a god shows up?”

“No,” Voth said. “You sit in the grass… and trust that a daughter will rise when the night is darkest.”

Chione rolled up the map and nodded. “Then we go.”

Grenna threw her hands up. “Great. Twilight prayer circle it is.”

But Chione’s voice was calm and firm. “Grenna—if we storm that place again, we lose. But if Mira wakes up inside that outpost, and she remembers who she is…?”

Finn’s eyes sharpened. “They won’t be ready for her.”

Chione turned to Voth. “And if we’re not there when she escapes?”

Voth’s smile returned—soft, almost wistful. “Erosia always helps a Twili in need.”

They had tried to drug Mira again. The IKSS agent had returned with a whispering vial, spoken with sweet poison, and left her alone in her darkened chamber, but Mira did not sleep. She remembered.

There was no sun. No moon. Only a constant, soft glow like distant candlelight diffused through endless mist. The sky was not blue. It was a deep indigo shot through with pulsing veins of silver—like memories drifting across water. The land was not whole. It was broken—fragmented into vast floating islands suspended by threads of shadow and gravity, interconnected by crystal bridges, root-tunnels, and stone arches that drifted through air like ghost-ferries. This was the Twilight, her home.

Mira saw herself again—small, running barefoot across a field of thistle-grain, chasing a pet seedling with legs and twitching leaves. The crops bent gently under weightless wind, and above her, streaks of drifting island clusters passed like whales in the sky. Everything was quiet in the Twilight. Too quiet. There were no birds. No seasons. No morning.

Just... time. Unmoving. Patient. The crops grew slow. The water tasted thin. Lightstones flickered more often than not. They had no sun to help the plants. No warmth but what they could kindle from the soil with magic. Everyone was hungry.

But they smiled. Somehow, they smiled. They made it work. Her mother—gray-skinned and strong—used to say: “We live because She wills it. Not the dragons. Not the day-folk. Her.”

Mira remembered learning to recite the hymn of the Hollowlight:

> “She gave her breath to hold our sky.\
> She gave her light to feed our seed.\
> She sleeps in pain to keep us free.”
>
> Erosia. The Mourning Queen.

Mira had never seen her. No one had. But everyone knew: the Twilight existed only because Erosia gave everything to sustain it.

She remembered the scent of thistlegrass. The sharp tang of dust and phosphor wind. She had wandered too far beyond the seed rows. A root-bridge had collapsed a few days before, and no one had cleared the area yet. She was laughing—laughing—as she chased the seedling.

“I do,” Mira said. Her voice was clear. Not loud. Not soft. Like water across stone.

Broadmantle shifted in his basalt seat, beard bristling. “That sounds like a warning.”

“No,” Mira said. “It sounds like honesty.”

A murmur stirred in the upper terraces. Mavveen raised a hand and the noise stopped.

Mira tilted her head. “A person.”

Mavveen smiled slightly. “A person with… remarkable traits.” Silence again.

Velassar spoke next. “You are not known to our records. You do not appear in any birth rolls of the elven tribes, nor gnomish settlements, nor dwarven strongholds. Nor human archives, for that matter. And your… aura… defies classification.”

“I was not born in your light,” Mira said. “But I have lived in your lands.”

Broadmantle’s tone grew firmer. “You don’t answer plainly.”

“Because you don’t ask plainly,” she answered.

Velassar narrowed his eyes, stepping closer to the edge of his platform. “Then let me ask plainly: Are you Twili?”

The word dropped like a hammer in the Circle. Even the wind outside stilled. Mira did not look away.

“I am.”

Gasps. Two aides dropped their quills. A Watcher stepped forward but froze at Velassar’s subtle hand gesture.

“I am Twili,” Mira said again, slower. “But not bound. Not sent. Not summoned. I serve no queen, no cult, no empire. I chose my own path. I found my own name.”

Mavveen’s lenses stopped turning. “How long have you been in our world?”

“Long enough to save it more than once,” Mira answered.

Another silence.

Chione stepped forward. “She has stood with me through every trial. In the wilds, in battle, in grief. She has never once betrayed us.”

“Her is noted,” Velassar said, voice icy.

“Her loyalty is earned,” Chione replied.

Mira looked across the council. “You can try to cage me. Or you can learn why I chose to walk among you.”

Velassar nodded slowly. “We will deliberate. You will remain in guest quarters. Your movements—supervised.”

“That would be a mistake,” Elandir said from the edge of the chamber.

Velassar raised an eyebrow. “Are you threatening us?”

“I’m reminding you,” Elandir said, “that every time you tried to hide the truth, it found its own way into the light.”

Broadmantle stood. “The Assembly is adjourned for the day.”

Mira turned without waiting to be dismissed. Chione stepped beside her. The two walked from the Circle as one, and behind them, the great River Assembly sat shaken—not by war, but by something far more dangerous: the unknown, now walking in the light.

The chamber behind the River Assembly's high platform was lined with silence wards, woven by elven sages in a time when the gods still walked. It was said no echo could escape its stone. Tonight, the quiet was not magical—it was born of fear.

Velassar stood unmoving at the tall river-glass window, his silver-and-indigo robes untouched by the wind that stirred outside. Mavveen sat beside a desk cluttered with scrolls, half-unraveled and abandoned. Broadmantle paced the floor, his boots striking the basalt tiles with slow, deliberate weight.

“She said it openly,” Mavveen murmured. “She’s Twili. No deflection. No disguise.”

“She had no reason to lie,” Broadmantle said, voice low.

“No,” Velassar added softly, “and that’s the most terrifying part.”

They fell into silence again. The stream below whispered on, unaware of what had just been spoken above it.

Mavveen looked up from her scattered records. “How old was the Sealing?”

“Ten thousand years,” Velassar replied without turning. “Older than the Confederation. Older than written law. Before the Dwarven schisms. Before the first Gnomish Assembly.”

“Ten thousand…” Mavveen repeated. “And we forgot.”

Broadmantle let out a long breath, heavy as stone grinding in a tunnel. “We didn’t forget. We buried it. Left it to myth. The sealing wasn’t just a wall—it was a mercy. The kind of mercy you don’t speak of afterward.”

Velassar turned now, face drawn with a rare, cold clarity. “A mercy that was never meant to be undone.”

“Which means,” Mavveen said slowly, “someone—something—undid it.”

“And not from within,” Velassar confirmed. “If the legends are even half true, the Twili couldn’t break it themselves. The seal was wrought by the Primal Dragons and locked with divine will. It was not a door. It was an end.”

Broadmantle lowered himself into a chair. “You’re saying this Mira… she didn’t claw her way out.”

“She was let out,” Velassar said.

The room darkened, as though the shadows themselves recoiled. Mavveen reached for a scroll, hands shaking slightly. “Even the fragments we have—the Kedgelony transcripts, the Dragon-Stone fragments, the epics of Sil'Sanar—they all agree on one point: the seal is inviolable from within. You would need outside force. Ancient magic. Or worse—consent.”

“No empire today has that power,” Broadmantle said. “Not even the Kobolds. Surely, the Kedgelony couldn’t forge a spell strong enough to disturb that kind of warding.”

Velassar’s gaze grew distant. “Then we must ask the question none of us dares say aloud.”

Mavveen whispered it anyway. “Who broke the seal?”

“And why now?” Broadmantle added.

“The timing is not random,” Velassar said. “It’s precise. Mira appeared just after the war with the Empire, in a time of imbalance, uncertainty. The Twili are not known for subtlety. But this… this is subtle. It’s surgical.”

Mavveen’s voice had a hint of awe. “She’s not an army. She’s a test.”

Broadmantle growled, “Then we’re the ones being studied.”

Velassar nodded once. “We need every record on the Sealing—no matter how buried, banned, or believed lost. And we need to know who among us still remembers how to touch that kind of magic.”

Mavveen looked down at her notes, then back at the closed door. “And if we find out?”

“Then we pray it’s the first and last,” Velassar said.

The wind stirred again, and for a moment, it sounded like something far older than air moving. Like something waking.

The River Assembly met again under a stiller sky. The chamber was cooler, the river slow. No court heralds announced the party this time. No pomp. Just Mira and Chione entering in quiet step, shadow and frost side by side. The Circle no longer viewed the Twili as a myth. Mira was proof: unwanted, undeniable.

Velassar of Siona stood before his chair, pale fingers folded like the pages of a closed book. Broadmantle sat heavy in his basalt throne, no longer pacing, no longer scoffing. Mavveen’s lenses had been replaced with crystalline readers designed for truth-gauging, though they clicked with discomfort rather than confidence.

Mira stood at the center. “I am ready,” she said.

Velassar’s voice was low. “Mira, yesterday you told us who you are. Today we ask how you came to be.”

Mira nodded once. “I remember… falling.”

She looked up. Her face was calm, but something darker stirred in her voice.

“I woke in chains.”

That caught the room.

“The Kobolds,” she continued. “I didn’t emerge into the wilds or wander into villages. I was taken. They were already waiting. I don’t know how they found me—but they knew I was different. They pulled me from a crater of glass and shadow. I was weak. Barely conscious. They bound me. Took me underground.”

Mavveen leaned forward, voice cautious. “How long were you held?”

“I don’t know. The first days bled together. Interrogation. Testing. Observation. They called me specimen before they called me Mira. They didn’t know what I was—not exactly—but they knew enough to fear it.”

Broadmantle grunted. “And yet… they let you go?”

Mira looked to Chione, then back. “No. I escaped.”

“You escaped the Kobold Empire?” Velassar asked slowly.

“With help,” she said. “I don’t know from who. One day, the guards were gone. The locks were undone. I found a corridor unguarded. At the end was the sky.”

Her voice broke, barely.

“It was the first sky I ever saw.”

The Circle held still.

Chione stepped forward. “When I found her, she was half-dead. She hadn’t spoken for days. She didn’t ask for pity. Only silence.”

“And you gave it?” Mavveen asked.

“I gave her space,” Chione said. “That’s more than most would.”

Velassar paced now, slowly. “So. The Twili are not merely returned—they were intercepted. Experimented on.”

“Not an army,” Mavveen murmured. “A specimen.”

“Which means,” Broadmantle growled, “the Kobolds knew something.”

Chione nodded. “Or someone within their ranks did.”

Velassar turned to Mira. “Do you believe they broke the seal?”

“I don’t know,” she said. “All I know is I didn’t leave the Twilight on purpose. One day, I was there. The next, I was… falling. Something opened a way and I was drawn through. Not summoned. Not pushed. Just… called.”

Mavveen slowly took off her readers. “Then someone wanted you out.”

“And someone else was ready to catch you,” Velassar said.

He looked to the others. “We are no longer dealing with a mistake. This was planned.”

Broadmantle’s voice rumbled low. “By who?”

Mira said nothing.

Chione did. “That’s what needs to found out.”

The Assembly sat in silence. Not in judgment, not yet—but in the recognition that the world had already changed, and that someone else had written the first chapter of what was coming.

Deep beneath the capital of Korland, past corridors carved by ancient geomancy and sealed by rites older than most bloodlines, lay the Vantemar Vaults—home to the Empire’s most secretive magical research. Unlike the grand academies of the Confederation or the temple-labs of the Kedgelony, the Vantemar facilities had no formal nameplate. Their doors were unmarked. Their purpose whispered.

Today, they were alive with tension. In a spherical chamber built of blackened stone veined with obsidian and soulglass, five figures stood around a containment platform, its edge lined with etched runes and iron spikes soaked in mana-binding ichor. Above them, suspended by geomagnetic tethers, floated a single orb—pulsing darkly with oily wisps of semi-living shadow.

Dr. Leraine Vautrix, senior arcanist of anomalous phenomena, stood at the head. Her mineral-white scales shimmered faintly beneath her blackstone labcoat, though her eyes betrayed only fatigue. “This is the twelfth run,” she muttered, not looking at anyone in particular.

“Thirteenth,” corrected Archivist Gurren, a lean Kobold with cracked emerald scales and a habit of correcting everything.

“Let’s hope the number's luckier than the last twelve,” grunted Research Lead Brakk Vell, whose earthen-red claws hovered over the glyph matrix.

Vautrix narrowed her eyes at the shadow orb. “This sample was recovered from the Mira breach site. Faint residuals, barely active. We have exactly one chance before the decay renders it inert.”

One of the younger technicians, still barely a junior adept, raised a hand hesitantly. “Permission to speak freely?”

“No,” Vautrix said without blinking. “Begin the resonance spike.”

The runes lining the platform lit—first green, then violet, then black. The orb twitched. Vell adjusted the flow. The room dimmed, and then, without warning, something answered.

The shadow within the orb stretched outward—not violently, but with sudden certainty. It coiled like smoke and cracked like lightning, flickering through shapes that could not exist in any natural geometry. A whisper filled the chamber. Not in sound, but in knowing.

“Is that… a consciousness signature?” Gurren whispered, voice uncharacteristically shaken.

“No language,” Vautrix said, eyes locked on the growing form. “Not communication. It’s reacting.”

“To what?” Vell asked.

“To recognition,” she breathed.

The orb pulsed once—and then collapsed into itself, leaving behind a single, flickering glyph burned into the containment plate. None of them spoke for several seconds.

Finally, Vautrix stepped forward, bent, and examined the mark. “It’s not Twili,” she said softly. “Not pure, anyway. It’s hybrid. Ancient... but touched by something foreign.”

Gurren checked the readings. “Mana resonance is off the scale. Source imprint suggests... not internal.”

Vell blinked. “You mean it’s not Mira’s magic?”

“No,” Vautrix said. “It’s something older. Something that opened the way.” They stared at the glyph—still warm, still pulsing. “For ten years we’ve failed,” she murmured. “But today… something looked back.”

What began as rumor had become truth, and truth, once uncovered, has a way of unraveling the world behind it. The River Assembly did not reconvene for open debate. There was no more space for careful phrasing or diplomatic delay. The revelation of Mira's capture and of the Kobolds' secret experiments reached the Confederation’s inner circles not through rumor, but through stolen evidence delivered by terrified hands.

It was enough. The elves called it a violation of cosmic law. The dwarves named it a breach of sacred boundaries. The gnomes, quietly, said what all were thinking: If they opened the Twilight once, they can do it again.

And so the decision was made—not with banners, not with horns, but with the red wax of consensus pressed into orders of mobilization. In chambers behind closed roots and veined stone, the Confederation declared its intent: to confront the Kobold Empire, to force answers, to contain the threat by force, if needed.

Armies that had not marched in a decade began gathering. Skyships were pulled from ceremonial stations. The gnomish artillery wheels were reforged. Elven seers wove battle wards. Dwarven stonecutters spoke the old songs of war for the first time in generations, and across the land, the old alliances stirred.

Chione’s party did not speak for a full hour when the message came. They sat in the quiet of their chamber, Mira watching the clouds from the window, Chione at the table, her hands wrapped around a cup gone cold. Thedden stared at the ceiling. Grenna polished her axe with uncharacteristic care. Finn paced the hall, bow slung loose. The offer was clear: the Confederation had asked them to join. Officially. Not as mercenaries, not as emissaries—but as champions.

They did not ask Mira. They did not ask forgiveness. But they asked for Chione. “We go,” she said, voice low but certain. The others turned to her. “We know what they’ve done to Mira,” she continued. “And if they’ve done it once, they’ll do worse of that the seal is broken. I won’t stand back and wait for another ‘accident.’”

Thedden nodded slowly. “If this is the war we’ve been walking toward... then better we choose when it begins for us.”

Grenna smirked. “I’ve been waiting to cave in a few of those polished stone helmets anyway.”

Finn said nothing, but tightened his bowstring until it sang like a blade.

Mira looked at Chione. “You don’t owe them this.”

“I don’t,” Chione said. “But I owe you.” And so it was decided.

For the first time, Chione’s party walked not as wanderers or healers or peace-brokers, but as soldiers: not for conquest, not for glory, but because some wars aren’t about sides; some wars are about who decides what future survives.

# Book Four: Embers Beneath the Stone

Ash drifted down like slow snow on the eastern ridges of Korre’s crescent mainland. Below, the coastal village of Serith Vale smoldered quietly—its watchtowers snapped, its harbor pitted with stone-bored craters, and its defenders buried beneath a landslide that hadn’t been there two days ago.

Chione stood at the precipice, cloak rippling in the salt-stung wind. Her gloves hummed with faint frostlight as her palm brushed a warped granite outcrop. The stone beneath her hand pulsed faintly. Still warm. Still whispering.

Korland rose in the distance—jagged, volcanic, and ominous. The Empire’s capital island, crowned by obsidian peaks and the glow of geomantic furnaces, sat like a clenched fist just across the strait. Chione’s breath steamed in the cool dawn air as she watched its outline sharpen.

Behind her, the others emerged from the morning mist. Finn moved first, silent as always, bow unstrung across his back. “Federation scouts report no survivors,” he said grimly. “Serith Vale fell in less than an hour. The ground split under them before they saw the sails.”

“Standard Kedgelony breach tactic,” Grenna muttered as she swung her axe into her back loop. “Open the faultline, flood the defenders with stoneborn. Hit the command post first. It’s not conquest—it’s pruning.”

Thedden arrived last, struggling with a heavy satchel of healing implements and half-damp powder herbs. “It’s not just tactical,” he said. “The stone’s wrong. The land isn’t just broken—it’s changed. Rewritten. That’s not normal geomancy.”

Chione nodded slowly. “Because this isn’t just a war of territory.”

It had been nearly a year since the first fire fell from the sky. The Kobold Empire had retaliated with brutal efficiency, striking back against the Federation and clearing the last of the islands still under its control, but when they struck the mainland again, this time without warning, and with Vercingetorix’s full legions at their back—something was different.

They weren’t just expanding; they were looking for something.

“We were warned,” Finn said. “Back in Embercrag. The fissure was just the start.”

“And now it’s our problem,” Chione replied, brushing soot from her coat. “Because whatever they’re unearthing over there—whatever the Kedgelony thinks they’ve found beneath this land it’s connected to the Twili, to Mira.”

Thedden frowned. “You think they’re awakening something?”

Chione looked eastward again, toward the ever-burning peaks of Korland. “I think they already have.”

A cry rang from below. Finn dropped to a crouch, eyes narrowing. “Rider,” he said. “Federation courier—barely holding on.”

They met the rider near the broken slope. A young elf, face bloodied, half-conscious in his saddle. He thrust a soaked scroll case into Grenna’s hands before slumping forward.

Chione broke the seal with her thumb. Inside, hastily scrawled across two bloodstained pages, were orders from the Southern Front: “Kedgelony geomancers have breached a Foundation Fort under the island of Vael’s ruins. The Federation’s Seventh Assembly believes it to be a site of pre-Imperial power—possibly predating the Old Accord. Imperial detachments are massing near the crater. You are authorized to proceed and disrupt further excavations. Bring the Metabold.”

Grenna glanced at her. “They mean you.”

“I’m not their pawn,” Chione said.

“You are their only chance.”

She stared down at the ruined village below—the quiet dead, the shattered ground, the smoldering memory of what once was—and then toward Korland’s outline beyond the sea. The Empire wasn’t just here to make a point. This war had opened something ancient beneath Korre. Something the stone itself remembered, and the one person who might survive what lay below had once been hidden for that very reason.

Finn drew his blade. “If we go, we go together.”

Grenna cracked her neck. “Let’s teach the Empire that the earth doesn’t belong to them.”

Thedden rolled his eyes. “I knew I should’ve stayed with the wardens of Mirebridge.”

Chione turned, the frost curling from her hands as the wind began to rise. The path ahead was burned, broken, and treacherous—but it was theirs, and this time, the stone would not break her, it would answer.

The path to Vael wound through scorched forest and fractured hills. Where once rows of terraced orchards dotted the southern interior of the Federation crescent, now charred husks clawed skyward, their roots torn up by geomantic quakes. Roads had buckled. Streams ran black with ash. The land bore the mark of more than war. It bore memory.

Chione dismounted near the edge of the collapsed crater that had once been Vael’s upper district. What remained of the city now clung to the lip of a vast sinkhole—a wound in the earth nearly two hundred feet across, its interior veiled in curling steam and cold fog.

They had arrived just before dusk. The wind carried the distant clash of steel and the thump of distant artillery further up the ridge, where Federation and Imperial skirmishers fought over control of the old watchtowers. But here, beneath the long shadows of shattered towers and half-buried statues, there was only silence.

No Federation troops had made it this far. Chione could see why. At the crater’s edge, a deep stairwell had been carved directly into the stone—geometrically perfect, with no tool marks. It spiraled downward in angular symmetry, slick with condensation and lit faintly by embedded glow-runes pulsing blue along the walls.

Grenna crouched beside the nearest glyph. “Not Federation work. This is fresh geomancy. Their Kedgelony’s been here recently—maybe still is.”

Thedden pressed a hand to the ground and winced. “The leyline here’s… wrong. Not broken, just stretched. Like something down there is feeding on it. And it’s not done.”

Finn scanned the crater’s perimeter, bow drawn. “No scouts. Either they’re confident we won’t follow—or they want us to.”

Chione stepped to the stair’s edge. Her breath misted in the air, though the stone radiated faint warmth. A low hum vibrated in her boots—not sound, but pressure. Old pressure. Like a heartbeat under stone. “I know this pattern,” she murmured. “I saw it once in the Hall of Testing, before the venom rite. The design,these curves, this wasn’t made for Kobolds. This predates the Kedgelony.”

Grenna’s brow furrowed. “Then what the hell were they doing down there?”

“Following a map only they can read,” Chione said. “But we’re going anyway.”

“Good,” said Grenna, standing. “I like stealing secrets from people who think they invented history.”

Thedden pulled a binding rune from his pouch and etched it into the edge of the descent. “I’m leaving a trace lock. If we don’t come back up, someone’ll know which hole swallowed us.”

Finn nodded grimly. “Let’s make sure that’s not needed.”

They descended into the crater. The spiral stair led them deeper than expected—at least ten stories below the surface. The air thickened, not with dust, but memory. Murals lined the inner walls—faded reliefs of creatures and symbols Chione didn’t recognize. And yet, as she brushed her hand over one—the image of a three-eyed figure holding stone and flame—her frost-glove pulsed. She recoiled.

“You alright?” Thedden asked.

“Yes,” she lied. “But this place… it remembers me.”

They reached the base of the stairs. The chamber beyond opened like a cathedral, its vaulted ceiling held aloft by ribbed pillars of obsidian and crystal. Arcane lighting shimmered along ancient channel-lines in the floor, forming a sigil of concentric rings and jagged spokes, and at the far end, beyond a half-shattered altar, stood a Kobold.

Tall, veined in crimson stone and armored in ceremonial plate, he wore the crescent-mask of the Kedgelony. A staff of silvered basalt rested in his claws. He was not surprised by their arrival.

“Daughter of Brice,” he said softly, his voice echoing across the hollow. “We feared we’d find your corpse on a foreign altar. But Rokesh does not forget his children.”

Grenna growled. “Chione. Say the word.”

The Kobold removed his mask, revealing eyes flecked with amethyst. “I am Kagent Yorrik of the Second Circle,” he said. “And I come not to kill you, Chione, but to welcome you home.”

Chione stepped forward, her magic coiling faintly around her fingers. “This isn’t home.”

Yorrik’s gaze was steady. “It will be. Once you remember what lies buried—beneath this vault, beneath your veins, beneath your name.”

He turned and tapped his staff once to the stone. The sigil on the floor flared. The vault began to open. The sigil flared blue, then deepened into an eerie violet as the concentric rings rotated in opposing directions. The floor shuddered—slow at first, then with a rhythmic groan. Stone ground against stone, and a wide circular platform in the center of the chamber began to descend like an ancient lift.

Yorrik stepped back. “The path has been sealed for thousands of years,” he said. “Not by time, but by fear.”

Chione felt her frostgloves react before she willed it—raw, silent power stirring from somewhere deeper than training. She didn’t step back. She stepped forward, onto the platform.

Grenna’s axe scraped free. “Are we really walking into a pit opened by a robed fanatic?”

“Wouldn’t be the first time,” Thedden muttered, already following Chione. “But this one hasn’t thrown spells at us. Yet.”

Finn followed in silence, his bow gripped but not drawn. He walked beside Chione, his shoulder brushing hers as if to anchor her.

The platform sank slowly into darkness, torches flaring to life along the descending shaft. The air thickened—not with heat, but a weight. A presence.

Yorrik stood at the edge, watching them lower. “There were others like you,” he called. “Before the exile. Before the bloodlines thinned. You are not an aberration. You are a return.”

Chione didn’t answer. Her breath steamed in the cold. The descent ended with a sudden halt that echoed through unseen chambers. Before them stretched a vast underground chamber, a hollow dome carved not by tool, but by force. The walls pulsed with embedded veins of crystal, red, violet, and gold, and ancient murals, faded but still potent with imagery, but it was the tree that silenced them.

At the center of the chamber, growing directly from the stone, stood a massive, leafless tree—its bark obsidian-black, but shot through with glowing fault lines of dull orange, like cooled magma still dreaming of fire. Its roots burrowed into the stone floor and rose again, arching like ribs. At its heart: a heart-shaped crystal, half-buried in its trunk, pulsing with slow, deliberate light.

Grenna whispered, “What in all the islands is that?”

Thedden fell to one knee, breath caught. “It’s alive.”

Chione stepped forward. The moment she passed the first ring of glowing runes around the tree’s base, her frostgloves went dim. The ice retreated and something else woke up.

The heartbeat in her ears no longer matched her own. It was deeper, older, rooted. The tree responded to her presence, not with motion, but with memory. Her vision blurred, then flashes: A circle of masked Kobolds, blood poured into carved sigils, a child held aloft in silence. the earth swallowing a scream, acity buried in flame, stone buildings turning to dust as vines of crystal strangled the towers, and beneath it all… the sound of a name never spoken aloud: her name.

She staggered.

Finn caught her. “What did you see?”

Chione’s voice was dry as stone. “This isn’t a vault.”

Yorrik’s voice echoed from above—he was descending now, walking calmly along a secondary spiral path.

“It is a seed,” he said. “Buried here long before Korland ever rose. Before the Empire. Before the Kedgelony were more than flame-keepers and tomb-tenders.”

Grenna raised her axe. “And you woke it?”

Yorrik nodded. “Because the Empire will not survive what comes next if we don’t. The bloodlines are thinning. The venom grows erratic. The stones have begun to die. But she—” he looked to Chione “—is proof the line endures. That the root remains.”

Thedden shook his head. “You don’t want to awaken her. You want to bind her.”

Yorrik’s eyes gleamed. “I want her to choose. Korland needs a queen of stone and frost—one who understands both paths.”

Chione turned back to the tree. The heartstone pulsed again, stronger now, mirroring the rhythm of her own.

“This isn’t about thrones,” she said. “It’s about rewriting what we are.”

Yorrik stepped forward. “So write it with us.”

Finn drew his blade. “She already has a family.”

The tree’s heart glowed brighter. A deep rumble passed through the floor, this time not from machinery, but from below. From something moving beneath the roots. The walls flared, old sigils igniting. The tree stirred.

Chione’s eyes widened. “It’s not just alive.”

Grenna raised her shield. “What is it?”

Chione whispered, “It’s waking up.”

And from beneath the stone, something vast and ancient began to rise. The tremor grew. It was not violent, not yet, but steady, deliberate. Like the breath of something immeasurable stirring after a long sleep. Dust rained down from the arching dome above. The heartstone embedded in the black tree pulsed like a beacon, its dull orange glow deepening to crimson with each beat.

Chione stood unmoving, eyes fixed on the bark as cracks formed outward from the crystal core. Not fractures, but veins, like lightning burrowing through obsidian. The runes on the chamber floor began to rotate, ever so slightly, clockwise in some places, counterclockwise in others.

Thedden stepped back. “That’s not geomancy,” he whispered. “That’s a convergence point. A god-marked anchor.”

Grenna lifted her axe. “That tree is not a tree.”

Finn drew close to Chione. “We need to leave. Now.”

“No,” she murmured. Her voice was distant. “We can’t run from this.”

The bark of the tree split down the middle with a deep, wet crack. What emerged was not wood, nor stone—but a form caught in-between. A humanoid shape, half-grown and half-bound in molten crystal and vine. Its limbs unfurled slowly, like they remembered how to move. Its surface shimmered with mineral sheen, but it breathed.

Yorrik dropped to one knee. “Praise be to Rokesh and Lotus,” he whispered. “The Firstborn lives.”

Chione took a step forward. “What is it?”

Yorrik’s voice trembled. “A Titan. Not of war—but of origin. One of the Stonebound. The first generation of Kobolds… before we were split by venom, caste, and empire. A true synthesis.”

The creature—twelve feet tall, glistening with fire-veined crystal and slick black bark—opened its eyes. They were mirrors, not reflecting the room, but the person gazing into them. Chione gasped. In that mirror, she saw herself—not as she was now, but as she could become: tall, cloaked in flowing mineral-veined robes, her skin laced with glowing runes, and her eyes burning with knowing cold. It saw her.

Then it spoke—not aloud, but within. “You bear the shard. The line unbroken. The memory, carried forward through silence and steel.”

She staggered back. Her head spun. It wasn’t language. It was memory shaped into thought. A message carved into stone so ancient it hummed through her bones.

Grenna stepped between them, axe raised. “Is it hostile?”

The being did not move. It simply looked—at all of them—and then turned to the black tree it had emerged from.

It placed its palm on the bark.

The earth beneath them split—not as a wound, but as an invitation. The floor opened, and beneath the chamber lay another space. A labyrinth of roots, glyphs, and suspended crystal arrays, all turning slowly, impossibly, like an astrolabe powered by earth’s breath.

The creature walked to the edge, then turned.

“This world forgets. But I remember. And you, daughter of fracture, are the first to come bearing both frost and flame.”

Chione felt a pressure behind her eyes—not pain, but recognition. She stepped forward.

“I don’t know what I am,” she whispered.

“You are the answer to a question no longer spoken aloud.”

And with that, the Stonebound dropped into the depths below—vanishing into the vault it had just opened.

Yorrik looked stunned. “It... accepted her.”

Finn grabbed Chione’s arm. “We need to go after it.”

Grenna growled. “Or seal this place and report it. That thing just ripped open the world’s oldest scar.”

But Chione was already walking. “I’m not running,” she said. “I think I’m supposed to follow.”

Thedden sighed and touched his satchel. “Of course. Into the sacred labyrinth with the ancient mineral demigod. Lovely.”

Together, they stepped to the edge. Below them, the spiraling chambers shimmered—half-mechanical, half-mythic. The legacy of a people forgotten even by their gods. The vault wasn’t just a tomb or a seed. It was a forge, and what it had begun to shape... was not done yet.

The descent twisted deeper into the bones of the earth. Stone walls shifted from smooth-cut vaultwork to something older—chambers formed less by hand and more by force. The path no longer felt constructed. It felt grown, drawn downward by the slow gravity of memory.

Chione and the party walked in silence until the corridor opened suddenly into a vast chamber—a hollow sphere carved into the world’s mantle. At its center hovered the Stonebound, suspended in a lattice of golden veins and stilled energy. The air tasted of ash and something colder—like the breath of a god long dead.

Forges ringed the walls, unlit but humming, as though waiting. Above them, metal and crystal filaments coiled like roots pulled inside out, converging on the central plinth.

The Stonebound spoke—not aloud, but into Chione’s mind. *It has been millennia since one of your blood crossed the threshold unbroken. You are not the first to arrive. Only the first to be found worthy.*

Chione stepped forward. The frost on her gloves dimmed, not from weakness—but reverence.

Grenna whispered, “This place... it’s not a forge. It’s a grave.”

“No,” Chione said. “It’s a story.”

The Stonebound lowered gently to the plinth. With its descent, the forges lit—one by one—casting long shadows across the walls. And on those walls, scenes began to move. Carved reliefs animated by light and time, telling a tale forgotten even by the Kedgelony.

*Long ago*, the Stonebound intoned, *the Kobolds were cast out from the highlands and heartlands of the world. Driven from the great rivers of the west. Cut off from the forests of the north. Banished from the molten plains of the south*. *They were stranded on Korland. Volcanic. Isolated. Surrounded by salt and enemies.*

The walls showed images of tribes carving shelter into black stone, of boats shattered on razor reefs, of Kobolds cradling dying children as the ground trembled with hunger.*They did not despair. They listened.*

The scene changed: a young Kobold—thin, stone-skinned, afraid—creeping into a canyon pulsing with unnatural heat. There, he saw the first Snawk—a serpentine horror, coiled in molten light, venom dripping from crystalline fangs. *The Snawks were sacred to Rokesh. Living blood of the Earth. Some bites killed. Others corrupted. But a few—just a few—survived.*

One image lingered: a Kobold, bitten and burning, writhing in the dust—only to rise, veins glowing, earth bending to his will. *Those survivors became more. Not by chance—but by pattern. A pattern the old shamans studied. A pattern the Kedgelony learned to imitate.*

The images turned darker: rows of Kobolds chained and drugged. Ritual circles smeared with dust and bone. Screams beneath stalactite halls. *Thousands were sacrificed. Hundreds transformed and died in madness. But in time... the Metabold was born.*

A new form rose from the carving: tall, refined, veins of luminous earth glowing across its arms. It carried stone not as burden, but as crown. *Not just survivors. Instruments of stone. Architects of war. Priests of power. Their blood built the Empire.*

Thedden recoiled. “They bred an ideal.”

Finn said quietly, “No. They forged it.”

Chione stepped toward the plinth. “And that knowledge—it was buried?”

The Stonebound nodded. “Because it was born of suffering. Because even the Empire knew shame. They erased it from scripture. Wrapped it in ritual. Gilded it in empire, but stone remembers.”

The final image hovered in the flames: a single flame-handed Metabold standing between two fallen continents, one foot on burning soil, the other on rising crystal. In its eyes—Chione saw herself. The forges dimmed. Silence returned.

Chione turned to the Stonebound. “Why show me this?”

“Because the Kedgelony have forgotten. Because the Empire builds imitations in desperation. Because you carry the truth in your blood—and you must decide whether it becomes memory... or reckoning.”

Chione stood still. Not as a questioner. But as an heir.

The silence that followed the forge’s dimming was immense. Not empty—just waiting.

Chione stood unmoving at the heart of the chamber, her frostgloves shedding faint steam. The air tasted of fire-sweat and something sweeter, like cooled stone after rain. Behind her, Finn, Grenna, and Thedden waited—not for orders, but for understanding.

And then, slowly, Yorrik stepped forward. The Kedgelony agent, masked no longer, crossed the threshold of the inner ring. He approached the plinth with measured steps, the runes on his robes still glowing faintly with residual power. Then he knelt. Not out of defeat. But devotion.

“I am Yorrik of the Hollow Circle,” he said quietly, voice low and reverent. “I was never sent to stop you, Chione. I was sent to bring you here.”

Grenna stiffened. “What is this?”

Yorrik did not move. “We are remnants of a forgotten order. A cult, yes—though not to Rokesh or Lotus or Empire. We serve the root, the truth. The original path. We’ve waited centuries for the signs—visions in fractured stone, in venom that burned but did not kill, in frost blooming from earth.”

Thedden took a slow step back. “You’re saying this was planned?”

Yorrik looked up at Chione. “You are the first worthy in millennia to survive the venom and answer the stone. We have tried... so many times. We have failed. But now, we see. The line endured. You are not a mistake. You are a return.”

He placed both hands on the ground. “We offer ourselves to your will. To your leadership. To the renewal of what the Empire should have become.”

Finn reached for his blade. “Don’t.”

But Chione raised a hand. Not in warning—in clarity. She looked at Yorrik, and past him—to the forges, to the murals, to the truth seared now into her bones.

She stepped off the plinth. “I will lead,” she said, her voice calm, cold, and clear.

Yorrik exhaled.

“But not your Empire.” The room shifted.

Yorrik blinked. “What?”

“I’ve seen what empires do,” she said. “What they sacrifice. What they rewrite. You want to revive something that broke the world—and call it greatness.”

Grenna smirked.

Chione continued. “I won’t be your Empress. I won’t sit on a throne of ash and call it justice. If I lead, it will be through the stone. Through truth. Not control.”

Finn lowered his blade. “There it is.”

Thedden let out a quiet breath. “Thank the gods.”

Yorrik was still kneeling—but his hands now trembled. “You would refuse what is owed you?”

“I would reclaim what was taken from all of us,” Chione said. “Not as a ruler—but as a beginning.”

A long silence followed. Then Yorrik bowed his head again. “Then so be it,” he murmured. “Not an Empress. A new root.”

The forge flames dimmed entirely, and from the still air, something ancient approved.

The city of Korezza loomed like a molten wound carved into the flank of Korland’s largest caldera—obsidian towers and lava-etched spires humming with geomantic energy. Beneath its brilliance, shadows ran deeper and older.

Mira moved through those shadows like she had always belonged to them. Her cloak reeked faintly of refinery soot and salted fog as she descended into the slums below the vent-chambers of Korezza’s eastern district. The wealth flowed uphill here, and the runes grew dimmer the further one strayed from the Imperial Parade.

She reached the safehouse through a crawl space behind a half-collapsed smelting wall, slipping through the rusted frame without disturbing the veil talisman set in the arch. It was a good place—quiet, wet, forgotten. The Empire rarely patrolled its own decaying roots.

Three figures waited inside: Arlen, the grizzled war-scout turned Federation spy, leaned against the wall with arms folded, breathing through a bad lung. Lysa, pale-eyed and quiet, flicked through illusion-hardened scrolls with fingers trained to decode enchantments, and Kael, former logistics clerk turned informant, who never blinked when he lied and never repeated a story the same way twice.

“You cut it close,” Arlen rasped. “Curfew’s tightened.”

“Don’t blame me,” Mira replied, sliding into the circle. “Blame the Empire’s engineers. They rethreaded the rune-grid south of Dock Seven. Nearly fried a contact trying to climb the wrong pipe.”

Lysa narrowed her eyes. “Then we were right to pull the asset.”

Kael passed her a folded strip of mirrored glass. “Courier intercepted this near a civilian cargo intake. Look familiar?”

Mira turned the glass in her hands. It shimmered faintly—but not with light, with memory. The enchantment was crude, incomplete. A ripple of recognition scraped the back of her mind.

She frowned, “This is a Twili signature,” she said. “But wrong. Inverted.”

“Corrupted?” Arlen asked.

“More like... mimicked,” Mira muttered. “Someone’s copying shadow-weaving techniques. Not Twili-born. Too unstable. The layering fractures after three layers of memory. Whoever did this doesn’t fully understand what they’re channeling.”

Lysa leaned forward. “That’s the third fragment we’ve seen in a month. All recovered from deep-shielded transport crates. All unmarked. All bypassing KSB customs.”

Kael nodded. “No ley-trace. No ship logs. And no explanation why half the ports in southern Korezza are running cold right when we’re meant to be reinforcing the Crescent front.”

Mira stared at the mirrored shard. “Whatever it is—it’s not part of the war effort.”

Arlen exhaled. “Then what is it?”

Mira didn’t answer at first. She pressed her palm against the shard, and a faint flicker of something slid across her vision: a blurred hallway, stone lined with crystal thorns, voices in languages she didn’t know—but one word, half-buried, echoed back: reflection.

She dropped the shard, “It’s a failed ritual,” she said. “A gate, maybe. Or an echo of one. They’re trying to reach across, to pull power from the same places the Twili once drew from, but it’s unraveling.”

“Unraveling how?” Lysa asked.

“Dreams bleed,” Mira said. “So do boundaries. Whatever they’re touching, it’s not sleeping anymore.”

Kael rubbed his jaw. “You think the Emperor knows?”

“No,” Mira replied. “But someone in the Kedgelony does. This reeks of their deeper sects. Something beneath even the KSB’s reach.”

Arlen growled. “Let’s say it is shadowcraft. What’s the goal? Power? Weapons?”

Mira shook her head. “No, fear.”

Lysa’s gaze sharpened. “Then they’re planning something.”

“They always are,” Mira murmured. “And we’re running out of time to find out what.”

Kael unrolled a map—a repurposed maintenance schematic of Korezza’s lower industrial tiers. He pointed to a location marked only as Siltmark Wasteport.

“There’s an outbound shipment scheduled from here in two nights. No manifest. No imperial inspection tag. The glyph signature is warded against lunar calibration. That’s shadow-aligned.”

Mira studied the mark. “Then that’s where we go.”

Arlen’s brow furrowed. “You’re sure?”

Mira glanced once at the mirrored shard, still pulsing faintly on the table. “I’ve followed trails that led to blood and silence,” she said. “This one leads to both.” She pulled her hood back up. “Pack light. Move quiet. We intercept the shipment, break its lock, and trace it to the vault. If we’re wrong, we’ll be hunted. If we’re right…” She trailed off.

Kael finished for her. “Then we find out what the Empire is really waking.”

Outside, the city rumbled. Forges bellowed. Lightning flashed far above the caldera, and beneath Korezza, shadows stirred in mirrors that were never meant to reflect the living.

The Siltmark Wasteport stank of rust and rot. Once a slag yard for obsolete war machines, it now served another purpose—quiet, sealed, and unlisted. Even the geomantic veins bypassed this place, as if the land itself refused to remember it.

Mira crouched atop a half-buried scaffold, her breath shallow. Below, a small crew of armored Kobolds—four regulars, two marked with Kedgelony sigils—loaded crates onto a low-slung cargo skiff. No fanfare. No banners. Only silence and sweat. Arlen and Kael crouched beside her. Lysa, further back, monitored the perimeter illusion net.

“They’re early,” Kael whispered.

Mira frowned. “That means they’re spooked.”

Arlen tapped his blade against his thigh. “Orders?”

“Intercept,” Mira said. “We hit fast. Kael cuts the tethers. Arlen and I draw them into the kill zone. Lysa collapses the shielding net to blind any watchers.”

Kael nodded. “Once we have the shipment?”

“We crack one crate,” Mira said. “And we run. No heroics.”

She dropped silently from the scaffold, cloak folding around her like shadow. A moment later, Arlen followed, and Kael slipped into the shadows near the cargo crane.

They moved like wind. The first Kobold didn’t even have time to cry out before Mira’s blade severed the rune-chain at his waist. Arlen tore through the next with a wide slash. Lysa's spell detonated above the site, sending out a flare that shattered their illusion net and pulsed silence through every nearby scry ward.

The Kedgelony reacted instantly—one slammed a staff into the ground, sending a tremor of magnetic force through the gravel. Arlen was caught mid-step, thrown backward with a sickening crack.

“Arlen’s down!” Kael shouted, loosing a quarrel into the chest of a guard.

Mira didn’t hesitate. She moved like smoke—parrying a blow from a stoneforged saber, ducking beneath a clawed swing, and plunging her knife into the gap between armor plates. The second Kedgelony began a chant—dark syllables twisted into mimicry of Twili cadence. Lysa screamed. The sound cut off suddenly.

Kael turned, only to be struck from behind by an arc of corrupted shadow. He collapsed, limbs twitching. One of the guards turned to finish him, but Mira was faster. Her frost-burst carved the air in a wide arc, freezing the last soldier’s helm to his skull. His scream gurgled, then died. Silence returned.

Mira stood alone, trembling. Her cloak was soaked in blood not her own. Around her, three of her team lay motionless. One—maybe two—might still be breathing. But the fight was over, and now came the part she feared most.

She approached the crate. It was sealed not with padlocks or geomantic tags, but with woven dreams—corrupted mirror-runes etched in broken script. She traced them carefully, dissecting the lattice of false reality, and after a few slow breaths, broke the seal.

The lid cracked open. Inside lay the body of a Kobold—or what had once been one. Its skin was gray and stretched thin, cracked with shadow-rot. The gemlike eyes were clouded, lifeless. A black sigil had been burned into its sternum, its lines wrong, not just corrupt, but unstable. As if drawn by a hand imitating something never meant to be understood.

Mira stumbled back. Not dead. Not alive. A *Husk*.

She opened the second crate. Then the third. Each one held a body—each different, but unified by the same truth. These were not Twili, but corruptions.

She reached for the sigil on the first body’s chest and tapped it with the hilt of her knife. A soft hum. A flicker. A memory fragment spilled into her mind. A lab. Deep. Cold. Chanting. A Kedgelony mask watching behind thick glass. And a voice: “Another body corrupted. We cannot keep losing them like this. What is up with this magic?” The fragment broke.

Mira dropped to her knees. “They’re making them,” she whispered. “Husks... and they don’t even know it.” She looked at the crates. “They’re producing them in mass.”

Her mind raced. This was bigger than the shadows. Bigger than war. This was an abomination. The Empire wasn’t reaching out to Erosia, it was becoming something worse than her. “No,” Mira said, rising, eyes hard as flint. “We need to burn this place.” She turned to the crates. Then to the skiff, and one by one, set them alight.

The Federation warhold at Crescent Hall stood half-carved into the side of a mountain, its outer walls shaped from folded steel and etched stone—built not for beauty, but for resilience. Outside, banners rippled in the coastal wind; inside, tension hung thicker than the salt in the air. It had been three weeks since Chione’s descent into the vault beneath Vael. The construct twisted, spinning on its axis and striking with all six limbs—one toward Thedden, two toward Grenna, one sweeping at Finn, and two more curling around to flank Mira and Chione. Its coordination was flawless—inhuman.

Thedden ducked and unleashed a glyph-blast under its feet. The explosion staggered it for only a heartbeat—but that was enough.

Chione conjured a wall of frost and slammed it into the construct’s flank. It cracked—but again, the mirrored surface simply rebuilt itself from inside.

“Mirrors are regenerating!” Thedden called.

“They’re pulling magic from the ambient leylines!” Mira shouted. “We need to cut it off.”

Then Vithra’s voice returned—sharp and close: “You severed my threads. Let’s see if I can return the favor.”

A pulse of anti-magic rippled through the room. The team staggered—glyphs fizzled, frost dulled. Mira stumbled, and her twilight blade flickered out.

“She's targeting our spellcasting directly!” Finn barked. “This is a kill-box.”

Chione grit her teeth. “Then we destroy the source.”

She looked at Mira, “You get to her. We’ll hold this thing.” Mira nodded.

Without hesitation, she turned and sprinted toward the back of the chamber—where Vithra now stood, her platform descending from the air on trails of shadowlight and circuitry. Her eyes blazed.

“You could have led them,” she spat.

Mira replied, “You don’t lead what you steal.”

She drew from the Twilight Realm again—but not the blade. Not this time. She opened a tether—not into the world behind the mirrors, but through it, slicing reality in half like silk. Shadow poured into her hands, forming chains of liquid darkness.

Vithra summoned blades of mirror-glass and launched them. Mira’s chains caught them in the air—wrapped, snapped, shattered. The platform collapsed. Vithra fell. Mira met her midair. They clashed in a spray of broken light and echoing pain—Twilight versus mimicry, truth against constructed obsession.

Across the chamber, the others attacked the construct. Thedden hurled his last disruptor rune into the exposed seam. Grenna roared and drove her shattered axe into the chest—wedged it in. Chione surged forward and froze the air inside the construct’s core cavity.

Finn loosed a final arrow. It hit the shard that wasn’t a mirror—but the core. The construct screamed, collapsing inward.

Mira pinned Vithra to the ground, one shadow-chain around her throat, another pinning her arms. “I could kill you,” she said coldly.

Vithra coughed, smiling through blood. “Then you’ll become me.”

“No.” Mira leaned closer. “I’m still someone my family can trust.”

And then—she severed the glyph at the base of Vithra’s skull. Clean. The light fled her body like dust drawn to a void. Vithra went still. The construct collapsed behind them—its limbs twitching, then inert. Silence settled. Chione stepped forward and reached out a hand. Mira took it.

The lab collapsed behind them in silence. No dramatic explosion. No screams. Just silence—final and complete. Whatever anchored the complex to the leylines had failed the moment Vithra's last glyph was severed. The mirrored walls had crumpled in on themselves like melting glass, and the construct's remains had turned to dust.

The team surfaced through a ventilation shaft just past sunrise, climbing onto a quiet ridge above the ash plains. The sky was bruised orange and violet, the air cold, the volcano behind them sleeping once more. They didn’t speak at first. Not out of grief—but exhaustion.

It wasn’t until they reached the coast, where a Federation submersible skiff waited under cloaking veil, that words returned. Mira stood apart from the others, arms folded, watching the tide.

Chione joined her, “You did it,” she said gently.

“We did,” Mira corrected. “But it doesn’t feel like a victory.”

Chione nodded. “They’ll ask us what we found.”

Mira looked down. “What do we tell them?”

Grenna approached from behind, still limping slightly but grinning. “That we cut the head off a monster before it cracked the world open.”

Thedden added, “That Twili magic is still too dangerous for fools to play with.”

Finn shrugged. “That Vithra Cirell got what was coming.”

Chione was quiet for a moment. “No.” The others turned. “We don’t just tell them what happened,” she continued, “We tell them what it means. That obsession, even born from love, can become rot. That fusion isn’t unity unless it honors what came before. That monsters can sound like dreamers when they lie well enough.”

Mira met her eyes. And nodded. They boarded the skiff in silence. When they arrived at Crescent Hall days later, battle-worn and gaunt, the war council was waiting. They asked what had happened in the depths of Korland. Chione answered. Mira added only one thing, at the very end. “There’s still shadow in the world,” she said. “But I’m not afraid of it anymore.” Then she turned to her family—her real one—and walked out of the council chamber, into the morning light

# Epilogue: For the Good of the Republic

The rendezvous took place just after midnight, beneath the archives rotunda of Crescent Hall. The interior was dark, save for the low glow of containment lanterns and the ever-present flicker of geothermal flame from the deep-pipes far below.

Finn stood alone near the archive stairwell, a plain satchel in his hands, worn and stained with volcanic soot. The quiet tapping of metal feet on tile announced the arrival of the one he’d summoned.

Director Mavveen Tasselcog, Newly appointed First Operative of the Gnomish Republic's Internal Research Directive, entered with her usual grace—compact, hooded, lenses retracted, posture perfect. Her coat bore no insignia, but every movement whispered power.

She stopped before him, adjusting her gloves. “You’re early,” she said.

“You raised me that way,” Finn replied coolly.

Mavveen allowed a rare smile to pass across her lips—brief, efficient. “And?” she asked.

Finn handed her the satchel. Inside: three mirrored core fragments, a coded sigil matrix torn from Vithra’s console, and a rune-mapped replication formula partially scrawled in the late arcanist’s own hand. Notes. Blueprints. Glyph progressions. The future of hybrid magic.

Her eyes gleamed as she examined the contents. “Excellent,” she murmured. “Absolutely excellent. I was worried when communications went dark, but you delivered.”

Finn didn’t reply.

Mavveen looked up. “And your companions?”

“They think it was all destroyed,” he said. “And it was—everything they could see.”

She tilted her head. “And Mira?”

“Traumatized. Tired. Relieved.” He paused. “Not suspicious.”

Mavveen closed the satchel, then stepped forward, resting a hand lightly on his shoulder. “You’ve done a great service to the Republic, son. Knowledge like this doesn’t belong in the hands of dreamers or broken empires.”

Finn’s jaw tightened. “It doesn’t belong to anyone.”

She nodded. “That’s why we’ll keep it safe.” There was a pause between them—longer than either preferred.

Then Finn said quietly, “It wasn’t betrayal.”

Mavveen blinked. “Of course not.”

He met her gaze. “It’s loyalty. To our people. To *you*.”

She smiled again, warmer this time, and turned to go, but before she vanished into the stairwell shadows, she said: “One day, they’ll thank you for what you did.”

Finn stood alone a moment longer. Then turned his back to the dark, and walked up into the light.
