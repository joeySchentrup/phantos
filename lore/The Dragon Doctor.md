I do not understand why the others insist creation requires a plan. Rokesh planned, of course he did. He considered the shape of the mountains before raising them. He thought about bones and muscles, how creatures would walk, how they would burrow, how they would eat. He could spend centuries contemplating the proper curve of a horn. Ouro’ras was hardly better. Everything had purpose. Everything had order. Everything belonged somewhere. Even Yvander, for all her silence, watched and considered.

I never understood it. When Kalistos gave us Phanatos, I looked upon that empty world and saw possibility. Endless, glorious possibility, and so I opened myself. I poured mana into the world, not carefully, not delicately. I simply let it flow. Arcane power spilled from me in great rivers of violet and blue, twisting through the air, striking mountains, lakes, clouds, forests, wherever it wished.

And so I watched. Oh, how I watched. Sometimes something beautiful emerged. Sometimes something strange. Sometimes something exploded, those were usually the most interesting. Creatures stepped from pools of raw mana with wings where wings ought not be, eyes that reflected colors which did not yet have names, bodies that changed when no one was watching. Fairies fluttered from sparks. Changelings crawled laughing from mirrored pools. Creatures of living magic formed, dissolved, and formed again.

Some of them looked at me. I looked back. “Well,” I said, “what are you?” They rarely knew. Neither did I. Wonderful. That meant we could discover it together.

My brothers and sisters sometimes accused me of being careless, perhaps I was, but creation should contain surprises. If you know exactly what something will become before you make it, have you really discovered anything? And discovery became my great pleasure.

When the world began filling with the children of the Six, I wandered among them constantly. I wanted to know what they were doing. What they were thinking. What they would build. More importantly, I wanted to know what they *could* build, so I began leaving gifts. Experiments, really. Although “gift” sounds nicer.

For the children of Golestandt, I made a doorway. Darkness fascinated me. Not mere absence of light, true darkness. Darkness with substance. Darkness untouched by flame or sun or star. So I gathered night together, folded it upon itself, pierced the veil between places, and created a gateway. Beyond it lay a realm of perfect night. No dawn.No day. Only an endless sky of shadow. I thought Golestandt’s children would enjoy it. They did. Perhaps too much.

Later, when they followed Erosia in rebellion, that refuge became their prison. The gateway I had created became the road by which they were sealed away. The Realm of Night became the Twilight Realm. I have wondered many times whether I should regret making it. I do not. Regret is a strange response to knowledge, but I learned to be more careful about doors.

For Ouro’ras’s children, I created the Artifacts. That was an especially enjoyable experiment. Could an object hold intention? Could metal remember? Could stone amplify virtue or vice? Could a tool become something greater than the one who forged it? The answer, I discovered, was yes. Very much yes. I taught the people of Hurley how to make them.

For a time, they became extraordinarily good at it. The greatest among them created wonders whose powers echoed far beyond the lives of their makers. Then came Ha'des. And afterward, the knowledge vanished. Not completely, perhaps. Knowledge rarely disappears completely. It hides.

Fragments survive in ruins. Techniques become rituals whose practitioners no longer understand their origins. Symbols are copied long after their meanings have been forgotten. Someday someone will discover it again. I hope I am watching when they do.

For Vlaurunga’s children, I made companions. She had filled the deep places of the earth with heat and fire, creating life where few others could endure, but her children worked constantly. They mined. Forged. Built. Burned. So I wondered: What if something else could work beside them? Thus came the automata. Metal given movement. Gears given purpose. Machines capable of labor without exhaustion. Vlaurunga adored them. At least, I believe she did. She laughed for three days when the first one accidentally walked into a lavafall. It survived. Mostly.

There was one difficulty. The automata required tremendous amounts of fire mana to function. Outside the deep places where Vlaurunga’s power saturated the earth, they slowed, weakened, and eventually became still. I tried solving that problem. Several times. One attempt exploded. Another achieved consciousness and refused to participate further. I respected its decision.

For Yvander’s children, the problem was obvious. She had created marvelous peoples of the seas, but many could never travel beyond them. The ocean is enormous, certainly, but curiosity should not be bounded by shorelines. So I created a potion. A rather elegant one, I think. Drink it, and the body alters itself according to its surroundings. Those born beneath the waves could walk upon land. Those shaped for land could descend beneath the sea. Transformation without destruction. Adaptation without losing identity. I was very proud of it.

Naturally, people found a terrible use for it. They began using it as punishment.Apparently if you give mortals the ability to transform bodies, someone will eventually decide that forcing transformation upon criminals is a suitable legal penalty. I complained about this extensively. Yvander listened. She always listens. I am uncertain whether she agreed.

Then there were my own children. Surely I owed them something. So I created the focuses. Crystals. Rods. Staffs. Orbs. Rings. Talismans. Devices capable of gathering the mana already present within a person and concentrating it. A weak spell became stronger. A difficult spell became easier. An impossible spell occasionally became merely extremely dangerous.

They spread everywhere. Humans used them. Elves used them. Gnomes used them. Eventually nearly every magical tradition on Phanatos incorporated some variety of focus. After several centuries, most people forgot they had originally been gifts to my own children. I found that rather amusing. A gift that becomes so common no one remembers it was a gift may be the most successful gift of all, but none of these was my greatest creation.

My greatest gift came because of Rokesh. Rokesh was different from me, very different. I scattered possibilities. He built foundations. I asked questions. He sought answers. I changed direction whenever something interesting appeared. He could stare at the same mountain for a thousand years and somehow find new reasons to admire it. For a long time, I simply watched him. I told myself I was studying him. That was technically true. I studied the way he shaped stone. I studied the patience with which he watched his children. I studied the strange tenderness beneath his immense strength. I studied how Kobolds gathered around him in prayer. I studied how he pretended not to notice me studying him. Eventually I reached a conclusion, I loved him.

This was inconvenient. So naturally, I decided to conduct an experiment. I wanted to create something for his children. Not something practical. Not something powerful. Something beautiful. Something alive. Something that would express what I could not quite explain. I took stones born from Rokesh’s earth and filled them with my mana. I shaped them carefully. Very carefully. Perhaps for the first time in my existence, I did not simply pour power into something to see what happened. I knew what I wanted. I wanted affection given form. The first stone trembled in my hand. Then it moved.

A tiny creature of crystal and mana looked up at me. I laughed. It chirped. I made another. And another. Soon they were crawling everywhere. Little living stones with personalities, tempers, habits, loyalties. They bonded with people. They played. They sulked. They loved. The Kobolds called them *pierres d’amour*. Stones of love. I gave them to Rokesh’s children, not to Rokesh. That part mattered. I wanted him to understand. If I loved him, then I would love what he loved, and Rokesh loved his children.

He understood. Of course he understood. He always understands eventually. He came to me holding one of the little stones in his great claws. It had apparently decided his shoulder was the proper place to sleep. “You made these?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“For my children?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

I had prepared several explanations. Most were brilliant. All disappeared. So I told him the truth, “Because I love you.”

Rokesh said nothing. He simply looked at me. Then at the stone. Then back at me. For perhaps the first time since creation began, I became uncomfortable with silence. Finally he stepped closer. And the Earth loved the Arcane. From that love came Agape. Not forged. Not constructed. Not poured accidentally from an excess of mana. Born. Our daughter. Love itself given wings. And the little stones changed with her birth. What I had made as symbols of affection became something deeper. Their small souls became stronger. Their bonds became truer.

Thousands of years later, mortals still carry them. Kobold children still play with them. Lovers still exchange them. Families still cherish them. Empires rise. Empires fall. Knowledge disappears. Portals become prisons. Artifacts become legends. Potions become punishments. Machines rust. Even magic changes. But the pierres remain. Of all the strange things I have made, and I have made many strange things, they are still my favorite. Perhaps because they taught me something I had never expected to discover. I always believed creation was beautiful because you never knew what would emerge. Rokesh taught me that sometimes creation becomes even more beautiful when you know exactly why you are making it.
