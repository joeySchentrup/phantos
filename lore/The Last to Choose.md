# The Last to Choose

A Lament of Golestandt

I remember the night before there was sorrow. Mortals speak of darkness as though it is emptiness. They fear the places where light does not reach and imagine that nothing dwells there but teeth, cold, and death. They do not understand. Darkness was never empty to me; it was quiet. It was the stillness beneath the roots of the mountains. It was the silence between one heartbeat and the next. It was the cool earth where seeds waited unseen. It was the sky before the first star dared to shine. And from that quiet, I made my children.

The Twili were the first and the foremost. I remember them rising from the moonless pools, pale hands breaking the black water without a sound. I remember their eyes opening. I remember the first one who looked upon me and did not recoil from my great form. She reached toward me, such a small hand; I lowered my claw, and she placed her palm against it. That was the first time I understood what it meant to create something that could love you in return. They were mine; not my possessions, but my children.

I taught them the language of silence. I showed them how the shadows could shelter rather than frighten. I taught them to hear what others ignored: the whispered prayer, the hidden grief, the footsteps behind the wall, the dreams that came only when the brilliant noise of the day had passed away, and they were beautiful.

The others never understood that as I did. Ouro’ras loved the brilliance of his children: they built cities beneath his light and raised towers that glittered beneath the sun. Rokesh delighted when his creatures shook the earth beneath their feet. Vlaurunga rejoiced in trials by fire. Yvander heard songs in the deep. Lotus smiled whenever some new impossibility sprang forth from the Arcane. And I? I watched my children disappear whenever the sun rose. I told myself I did not mind, for they were creatures of night as was I.

Then came Erosia, our final work as dragons of creation. Our daughter of night to mirror her brother of day. When she first opened her eyes, I loved her; perhaps that was my mistake. The others saw in her the culmination of our power. I saw my children. She understood the Twili in a way no other being could. She walked among them without demanding that they speak louder. She sat beside them beneath the stars. She listened and they loved her; how could they not?

The world praised her brother: Quint laughed, and nations laughed with him. Quint danced, and cities opened their gates. Quint raised his hand, and the dawn came. Erosia raised hers, and frightened mothers pulled their children indoors.

I saw it. I said nothing. That is the first sin for which I have never forgiven myself.

Perhaps, had I spoken then, none of what followed would have happened. Perhaps if I had stood before Ouro’ras and demanded that his children cease calling mine monsters… Perhaps if I had rebuked Quint when his jokes cut more deeply than he knew… Perhaps if I had gone to Erosia when sorrow first became bitterness… Perhaps… Ancient beings have more years than mortals can imagine, yet we are haunted by the same word: Perhaps.

When the first night refused to end, I knew why. The others came to me in fury. Ouro’ras demanded that I command my children to surrender. Rokesh spoke of balance. Vlaurunga spoke of cities burning. Yvander spoke of seas gone cold beneath an unmoving moon. Lotus said little, for she knew better than the others what they were asking of me.

I refused them. Again. And again. And again.

While they prepared their champions, I remained alone.

“Choose,” Ouro’ras told me.

“No.”

“Golestandt, the world is dying.”

“They are my children.”

“They have chosen Erosia.”

“They chose the only one who loved them.”

The words wounded him. I intended them to. Perhaps that was another failure…

Rokesh came next. He was gentler, “The others have chosen.”

“I know.”

“Quint cannot defeat her without all six.”

“Then perhaps Quint should learn what it is to lose.”

Rokesh lowered his great head, “This is not about Quint anymore.”

I hated him for saying it. Because I knew he was right. Still, I did nothing.

The war continued. I watched from places where even dragons could not find me. I watched the children of Light flee through fields swallowed in twilight. I watched the Twili tear down temples in which they had been cursed for generations. I watched them dance openly in streets where they had once hidden their faces, and, for one terrible moment, I was glad. That memory disgusts me now. Not because my children were free; no, they deserved freedom, but, rather because I mistook vengeance for freedom.

The war changed them, or perhaps it revealed what grief had already made. The shadows became weapons. The quiet arts I had taught them became instruments of terror. Dreams became places of torment. Those who had been called monsters learned to answer the insult with teeth; still I could not raise my claw against them. How could I?

I remembered their first night. I remembered that tiny hand against my talon. I remembered Erosia sitting beneath my wing, long before bitterness touched her, asking me why the stars were visible only in darkness. I had answered her then: “Because some beautiful things can only be seen when the light grows quiet.” She had smiled, my daughter, my child. How could they ask me to destroy her?

At last, all five came. No messengers. No champions. The dragons themselves. They found me at the edge of the world, where the black sea met a sky without stars. For a long while, none spoke.

Then Ouro’ras stepped forward, “It must end.”

I did not look at him, “Then end it.”

“We cannot.”

“You mean you will not.”

“We cannot,” Lotus said softly. “Not without you.”

I closed my eyes. They had chosen their champions. Five mortals now carried five fragments of the power that shaped the world. Only Darkness remained. Only me, “You ask a father to arm the hand that will strike his children.”

No one answered. That silence was their confession.

Rokesh finally said, “Yes.”

I turned upon him, and for one instant the darkness around us became so deep that even the dragons could not see one another. I wanted them afraid. I wanted them to feel even the smallest portion of what they were demanding. “You made your creatures,” I told them. “You watched them grow. You heard them call your names. Tell me, Rokesh, if it were your children standing beside Erosia, would you do this?” He did not answer. “Would you?” Still nothing.

I looked to Yvander, “Would you drown yours?” To Vlaurunga, “Would you burn yours?” To Lotus, “Would you erase yours from the very fabric of magic?” Finally I looked at Ouro’ras. “Would you condemn your children to a realm beyond the world and call it victory?”

His light dimmed, “No,” he said. At least he gave me that. “No,” he repeated. “But I would do it if the world depended upon it.”

I hated him then: for his answer, for its certainty, for forcing me to understand it.

And so I chose. There was no thunder, no proclamation, no great sign written across the heavens. I simply looked into the world and found one soul among the creatures of night, A mortal who knew darkness without surrendering to hatred, one who understood that shadow could conceal a blade, but could also shelter a frightened child.

I touched that soul. The mortal awoke beneath a blackened sky and heard my voice. The other champions received blessings, mine received an apology: “I have chosen you,” I whispered.

The mortal fell to her knees, “My lord…”

“Do not call me that…” Silence. “I have no right to lordship tonight.” I gave the champion a portion of my power. Darkness flowed into mortal hands, and with it went something I have never recovered. “Fight beside Quint,” I commanded, the words nearly failed me. “Stand with the other five. When the hour comes… help them seal Erosia away.”

The champion hesitated, “And the Twili, my kin?”

I could not answer. Perhaps the mortal understood from my silence… Perhaps that was crueler than words… “At the end,” I finally said, “do what must be done.”

And so Darkness joined the Light against its own children. The war ended soon after. Mortals would later sing of the Six Champions. They would speak of Quint restored. They would tell how Erosia was struck down and how the great Seal divided the world from the Twilight Realm. They would call it salvation. They would call it victory. They would say that the dragons had acted as one. They lie.

There were only five dragons standing together when the Seal was made. I watched from afar. I heard Erosia scream: not in fear, but in betrayal. She knew. Of all the wounds she suffered that day, mine was the deepest.

“Father!” Across all creation, through stone and flame and sea and magic, I heard her. “Father!”

I did not answer. The Seal closed. The Twili vanished. The world saw its first dawn in many years, and all creation rejoiced, all except me. When the others came to find me, I had already gone. I descended beneath the deepest places of the world, beneath roots and caverns and buried fires, into a darkness no mortal had yet touched. There I folded my wings. I withdrew my voice from prophets. I withdrew my hand from kings. I withdrew my face from temples. Let the others govern the world they had saved.

I wanted no part in its celebration, for whenever I looked upon Phantos, I saw only the places where my children had once walked, and whenever the night came, I remembered that once the darkness had been filled with their voices. Now it was only quiet…, true quiet…, empty quiet. The kind mortals mistakenly think darkness has always been. Perhaps someday the Twili will forgive me. Perhaps Erosia will understand… Perhaps I will again walk beneath the stars and hear one of my children call me Father without hatred… I do not expect it.

I chose the world over them. Whatever the reason, whatever the necessity, whatever the other dragons demanded of me; I chose. And so I remain here. Alone beneath creation. Waiting in the dark I once loved, for the first time since the beginning of all things, wishing there were light enough that I did not have to see the shadows of my children everywhere.
