I remember when the earth was quiet, not empty, never empty. Kalistos had made it, and therefore even the barren stone possessed purpose. Mountains waited beneath the crust. Veins of metal slept in darkness. Crystal grew where no eye yet existed to see it. The soil had not yet been walked upon, but already it bore the promise of every footprint that would someday cross it. That promise was mine to uncover, for I am Rokesh, dragon of the earth.

Kalistos was the one who gave me the earth, not that it should belong to me, for nothing truly belongs to any of us except by His will, but that I should tend it, shape it, draw from it what He had placed within.

At first, I was content simply to shape. I raised mountains because the horizon seemed lonely without them. I carved valleys because mountains ought to have somewhere for their waters to go. I pressed my claws into the earth and opened caverns so deep that even Golestandt admired their darkness. I scattered gemstones beneath the mountains, not because they were needed, but because I liked the thought of beauty hidden where one must labor to find it: quartz, ruby, sapphire, emerald, diamond, and thousands more for which mortals would someday invent thousands of names. I placed them beneath plain stone and common dirt, and I laughed to myself. Let them dig. Let them wonder. Let them discover that the earth does not reveal all its treasures merely because one looks upon its surface.

Then I began making things that moved, that was more difficult. A mountain is patient. You place it somewhere, and, barring Vlaurunga getting overly enthusiastic beneath it, it generally remains where you put it, beasts do not. My first creatures were simple things. Crawlers. Burrowers. Little creatures that scratched tunnels through the soil and turned dead earth into living ground. I liked them, so I made larger ones: creatures with hooves to cross the plains, creatures with claws to climb the mountains, creatures with great horns, thick hides, sharp teeth, long tails, small tails, no tails at all. Some were magnificent, others were mistakes; I will admit that.

There is a creature somewhere in the southern marshes which I maintain was caused by Quintara Lotus touching something she should not have touched; she maintains that I made it. We have agreed not to discuss the matter, but I loved the beasts. I loved watching them discover the world without knowing that they were discovering anything at all. A wolf did not need to understand the philosophy of the mountain to run across it. A great stag did not need to comprehend the miracle of life to lower its head toward a stream. They simply were, and there was something beautiful in that.

I thought perhaps that would be enough for me, then I made the Kobolds. Even now, I am not entirely certain why they were different. I had already made beings who walked upright; creatures who spoke; creatures who reasoned and built and remembered. The Centaurs were strong; the Goliaths possessed the strength of the mountains themselves; the Earth Genasi bore my element proudly. There were Minotaurs and Tabaxi and Lizardfolk and creatures who would eventually scatter across lands I had not yet finished shaping. I loved them all; I want that understood. I loved them, but then...there were the Kobolds.

I shaped their bodies from the earth itself: stone for their flesh, crystal for their eyes, the endurance of mountains in their bones, but I did not wish them to be statues. I gave them faces like the beasts I loved: something of the wolf, something of the hound. Strong jaws, sharp ears, and eyes that could communicate more in a glance than some creatures managed in a speech, and then I breathed my power into them. The first Kobold opened his eyes, he looked at me, and I forgot everything else. I know that sounds terrible.

Ouro'ras certainly thought so. “Rokesh,” he would say, “there are others who need your attention.”

“Yes.”

“You have responsibilities.”

“Yes.”

“The western mountains are collapsing.”

“They will stop eventually.”

“ROKESH.”

“Yes?”

“The Kobolds are fine.”

I never understood how he could say that so casually. Of course they were fine, I was watching them constantly. I watched the first Kobolds discover fire, though Vlaurunga insists she deserves some credit for that. I watched them learn to shape stone. I watched them carve homes into my mountains. I watched them gather into families, then clans, then tribes. They argued, constantly. They fought over caves. They fought over food. They fought over whose stone carving was better. Once, two tribes fought for three days over ownership of a particularly attractive boulder. I loved them, every ridiculous, stubborn, ambitious one of them. Perhaps I saw too much of myself in them. They were creatures of earth, and earth does not like to move.

Once a Kobold decided something, convincing him otherwise could require forces normally associated with continental drift. They were proud. They were loyal. They could be possessive. They could be foolish. They held grudges as though grudges were precious minerals to be passed from generation to generation, and yet they loved with the same stubbornness.

A Kobold who called you family would cross a mountain for you. A Kobold who considered a place home would rebuild it from rubble a hundred times. A Kobold who gave his word would sooner crack than bend it. How could I not adore them?

So I watched, and watched, and watched. The forests grew without me. The beasts multiplied without me. Rivers changed their courses. Some of my other children wandered into distant lands and began cultures and kingdoms of their own. I knew these things, usually because one of the other dragons told me, occasionally because Ouro'ras shouted them at me, but the Kobolds were building a new settlement, and I wanted to see where they placed the walls. I told myself this was devotion, perhaps it was, but devotion can become obsession when love forgets how to look beyond the beloved. I did not understand that then; Quintara did.

Lotus had always been difficult for me to understand. I create slowly, she creates because she wonders what will happen. I build foundations; she opens doors. I once spent a century arranging a mountain range until every ridge followed exactly the pattern I desired; Lotus once poured raw magic into a lake because she wanted to see what color the fish would become: they became invisible, she considered this a success. And yet she understood me in a way the others did not; She never told me to stop loving the Kobolds. That would have failed, instead, she loved them too.

I did not notice what she was doing at first. I noticed only that the Kobolds had discovered something new: small stones, living stones. They moved, not much, mind you. They rolled. They hopped. Some followed their owners. Some refused to move unless properly encouraged. One developed the habit of hiding underneath a Kobold's sleeping mat and vibrating until he woke. I watched for three days, then four, then seven.

“What are those?”

Lotus was beside me. I had not noticed her arrive. That happened frequently. “Do you like them?” she asked.

“What are they?”

“A gift.”

“For whom?”

She smiled, “For them.”

That caught my attention. She had taken what was mine–not stolen, but borrowed: stone, crystal, mineral, the fruits of earth, and through her arcane power, she had placed within them something new, something warm, something alive. Not life as Jinshi would someday govern it, something smaller, simpler: affection.

The Kobolds loved them immediately. Of course they did. They carried the little stones everywhere. They decorated them, named them, argued over which was cutest. I saw one warrior, a Kobold who had killed three beasts with his bare hands, spend an entire afternoon attempting to convince a stubborn little blue stone to come out from beneath a table. He finally offered it a polished piece of quartz; it emerged for that. I laughed until the mountain shook. Lotus laughed too, then I looked at her, really looked at her for perhaps the first time.

“You made these for them?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Her expression changed. Quintara Lotus, who could speak for hours about the theoretical behavior of magic under conditions that existed nowhere in creation, suddenly seemed unsure of herself, “Because you love them.” I said nothing. “And,” she continued, “I thought perhaps... if I made something for that which you love, you might understand something.”

“What?”

“That I love what you love because I love you.”

I have seen mountains split. I have felt continents shift. I have listened to the roar of stone grinding against stone beneath the deepest oceans. None struck me as violently as those words. I had spent ages watching the Kobolds. Their first steps. Their first settlements. Their first friendships. Their first wars. I thought I understood love because I understood attachment, but Lotus had shown me something different.

Love was not merely staring endlessly at something precious. Love could expand. Love could make room. Love could draw one outward without diminishing what had first been loved. I looked again upon the Kobolds: one held a tiny pierre against his chest, another gave one to his companion, a third was trying unsuccessfully to stop his pierre from eating pebbles. I looked back to Lotus, and suddenly the world seemed larger than it had the moment before.

I had forgotten the beasts. I had forgotten the forests. I had forgotten the mountains I once spent centuries shaping. I had forgotten, at times, even my siblings. The Kobolds had filled my vision until I mistook a part of my vocation for the whole of it. Lotus did not take them away from me. She simply stood beside them, and because I loved them, I began to love her, and because I loved her, I began again to see everything else. Not equally, I should be clear about that. The Kobolds are still my favorites.

Ouro'ras tells me I should not say this. He has favorites too. He simply pretends otherwise, but I began once more to walk the earth. I visited the Centaurs.I watched the Goliaths climb mountains I had made before their grandparents' grandparents existed. I returned to the beasts and discovered that several had evolved in ways I had absolutely not authorized. I repaired the western mountains. Ouro'ras was insufferably pleased about that.

And I spent time with Lotus, real time, not merely allowing her to sit nearby while I watched Kobolds. We explored. We created. We argued. She ruined several perfectly good geological formations. I organized portions of her laboratory when she was not looking. She reorganized them afterward according to a system she called “wherever I remember putting things.”

It was horrifying, but I was happy. From our love came Agape. Not shaped by duty as the others had been, not merely formed because some aspect of creation required governance, our daughter came from love itself. When I first looked upon her, I finally understood what Lotus had been trying to teach me. Stone is strong because it endures, but a world cannot be made from stone alone. Stone supports roots. Stone redirects rivers. Stone shelters creatures. Stone holds gems. Stone becomes the foundation upon which others build. That is its strength, not isolation, but relationship.

I still watch the Kobolds. I always will. When they build beneath the mountains, I hear their hammers. When they carve my image into their temples, I see it, though I wish they remembered more often that I too am only a creature beneath Kalistos. When they argue, I groan. When they suffer, the earth feels heavier. When they prosper, there are few things in creation that bring me greater joy, but now, sometimes, when I find myself watching them too closely, I feel something bump against my claw.: a little stone, a pierre d'amour. Usually Lotus has sent it. Sometimes there is a message attached, *There is a whole world behind you,* and I turn around. Usually she is there, and usually she is smiling, so I follow. The Kobolds will still be there when I return. They are stone, after all, and stone endures.
