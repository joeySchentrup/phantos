They call me cold. I suppose that is reasonable. I am ice, after all. When my breath settles upon the mountains, rivers cease their running. When my wings pass over the sea, frost gathers upon the waves. When I speak too loudly, snow falls. When I sleep too deeply, glaciers creep farther down their valleys. So yes, I am cold, but they have mistaken coldness for the absence of love.

They have never understood me, perhaps I have never cared enough to correct them. When the world was young, there was much noise: Ouro’ras spoke of what his children might build, Rokesh delighted in every beast that crawled across the earth, Quintara Lotus filled the world with wonders that even we did not always understand, andVlaurunga laughed. She always laughed. Fire does that. It crackles, leaps, shouts, demands to be noticed. Golestandt preferred the quiet, though his was a different quiet than mine. And I? I watched.

Before I made anything, I watched the world. There were mountains, valleys, clouds drifting slowly over empty plains, but when I looked upon the waters, I found something different, depth. The surface glittered beneath the light of Ouro’ras, but beneath it stretched another world entirely, a world hidden from sight, quiet, ancient, patient. I loved it immediately.

So I descended. I touched the water, and I made it cold, not frozen, not yet, just cold enough to stir. The currents changed beneath my claw. The water curled around itself. Great streams began to wander beneath the surface, carrying warmth from one place and cold into another, and within those currents, I placed life. At first, they were simple things, tiny creatures drifting wherever the water carried them. I watched them, some were swallowed, some found food, some multiplied, some vanished.

I changed nothing at first, but then I made others: creatures with fins, creatures with shells, great serpents that wound themselves through trenches so deep that even sunlight had never touched them. I created small silver things that moved together in thousands, turning as one body whenever danger approached and great gentle beasts that sang through the oceans, their voices traveling farther than they would ever know. I made creatures beneath the ice, creatures near the shore, creatures that buried themselves beneath sand, creatures that knew nothing except black water and crushing depths, and I watched every one of them.

I loved every one of them, even the strange ones, perhaps especially the strange ones, eventually I made children who could look back: the Aquisi, the Lanyarui, all the water-born. They are the ones who could speak. I remember the first of them opening her eyes beneath the water. She looked directly at me. Of course, she could not truly see me, for I was far above her, stretched across the sky, watching through layers of cloud and sea, but for one moment I wondered.

Her eyes opened. She looked upward, and I thought, *There you are.* There was something different about creating beings who could choose. Fish follow hunger. Whales follow currents. The creatures of the deep follow instincts older than language, but these children could decide. They could love. They could hate. They could betray. They could forgive. They could become something I had never imagined. That fascinated me, and so I watched.

The others sometimes asked why I did not live among my children. I could have. I could have raised palaces beneath the sea. I could have sat upon a throne of blue ice while the Lanyarui gathered around me. They would have worshiped me, brought offerings, asked questions, so many questions and every answer I gave would have changed them. That was precisely why I refused. A child who always knows her mother's answer never discovers her own. So instead, I went north.

I traveled farther north than any of my children had yet traveled. There, where the ocean became white and the night stretched long across the world, I found a mountain of ancient ice. I entered it and I began to carve, not with claws, but with thought. Ice remembers water, that is something most creatures do not understand. Freeze a lake and it does not cease being water. Its movement merely becomes still, but rather its surface becomes clear, its depths become quiet, and if ice can remember what it was… then ice can remember what it has seen.

So I shaped a chamber, then another, then another. Until there stood beneath the glacier a cavern greater than the palaces of kings. Its walls were perfectly smooth. Its ceiling glittered with frozen stars. Its floor was clear enough that I could look downward and see the black waters far beneath, and at the center I raised a great pillar of ice. I touched it. The walls awakened. Thousands of images appeared around me: every ocean, every river, every shore touched by my waters.

I could see my children, not merely kings, not merely heroes, not merely those whose names would be carved into history, *all* of them. I saw a Lanyarui child chasing silverfish through a reef, an old sailor repairing a net, a mother singing while she carried her infant through shallow water, a hunter stalking something beneath the ice, a fisherman cursing because the tide had carried away his boat, a young girl crying alone where no one else could see, a soldier preparing to die, a boy laughing, a woman praying. I saw them. I see them still. This is my palace. This is my throne. A cave of ice filled with the lives of my children, and so I watch.

Sometimes they pray to me; that is always difficult.

A sailor sees the storm gathering, "Yvander, save me!"

A mother sees her child sick, "Yvander, please."

A city sees enemies approaching, "Mother of Waters, protect us."

I hear them, every one. Do you understand? Every. One. They think silence means I have not heard; it does not. Sometimes I approach the wall and place my claw against their image. I watch the frightened sailor cling to the mast. I watch the mother hold her child. I watch the soldiers prepare their spears. Everything within me screams to reach through the ice, to still the storm, to heal the child, to drown the army. I could, that is what makes it difficult. I could, but if I answered every prayer, they would cease living their own lives. There would be no courage, because I would remove danger. There would be no sacrifice, because I would remove suffering. There would be no wisdom, because every difficult choice could simply be handed to me. They would become puppets waiting for their mother to pull the strings. I will not do that to them.

So I watch. Sometimes the sailor survives. Sometimes he does not. Sometimes the child recovers. Sometimes the mother's song becomes a lament. Sometimes the army wins. Sometimes the city burns. Through it all, I remain here, watching, loving, remembering. Perhaps that is why they think me cruel. They only see that I did not move. They cannot see how long I remain before that image afterward.

Do not misunderstand me, I do intervene, rarely. There are moments when a current shifts without reason, when ice breaks beneath the feet of an army but somehow bears the weight of a fleeing child, when a ship lost for seven days awakens to find itself mysteriously drifting toward land, when some creature from the blackest trench turns aside from prey that should have been helpless. My children call these things fortune, coincidence, providence. Sometimes they thank me, usually they do not. That is fine. A mother does not require her sleeping child to thank her for pulling the blanket over him. I did not make them so that they would praise me. I made them because I wanted them to exist.

The other dragons have accused me of detachment, even those who should know better. They see me sitting silently before my walls. They see the stillness. They see the ice. They assume there is nothing beneath it. That amuses me.Ice is not empty. Beneath every glacier is pressure powerful enough to carve mountains. Beneath a frozen sea lies an entire living world. Stillness is not absence. Silence is not indifferenc, and love need not always burn.

Fire announces itself. It gives warmth and light and demands that everyone nearby acknowledge its presence. Ice does not. Ice remains. Ice preserves. Ice remembers. Perhaps that is what I am: the memory of my children. Empires will rise among them. Empires will fall. They will fight wars and sign treaties and sing songs about heroes who lived for the length of a heartbeat. They will forget names. They will lose stories. They will bury cities beneath sand and stone and water, but I will remember. I remember the first Lanyarui who opened her eyes beneath the sea. I remember the first Aquisi who laughed. I remember every child who ever looked toward the water and wondered whether I was listening. I was. I am. I always will be.

There are nights when I leave the cave, not often. I climb from beneath the glacier and stand upon the frozen sea. The stars shine above me, the ocean moves beneath my feet, and through the ice I can hear them: whales singing, currents moving, creatures hunting, children dreaming in cities far beneath the waves, my children, all of them. Sometimes I lower my head to the ice, and I listen. No one sees me. No one knows. Good. Love does not become less real because it is unseen. Perhaps someday they will understand that. Perhaps they will not. It does not matter.

Let them call me cold. Let them say Yvander sits alone in her frozen halls and cares nothing for the world. Let them imagine that the Dragon of Ice possesses a heart of ice as well. I know what lies beneath the surface. That is enough. For wherever my waters flow, I see. Wherever my children wander, I watch, and when, at last, each life disappears from the walls of my cavern, I remember. Always. That is how I love them.
