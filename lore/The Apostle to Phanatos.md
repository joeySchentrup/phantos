# Prologue: The Dream

It began with a dream.

Roger Barron, Auxiliary Bishop of the Diocese of Dulcia, had retired to his chamber in the bishop’s residence after a long day of confirmations and clergy meetings. As always, he had ended the day with prayer before the Blessed Sacrament, whispering to Christ in the tabernacle, "Lord, I am your servant. Send me where you will."

That night, he dreamed—not of saints or sermons—but of a garden not of this Earth. It was vast, moonlit, and filled with creatures of impossible beauty: birds that sang in chords of light, trees whose leaves whispered prayers in languages he did not know. At the center of the garden was a Man, luminous with peace and sorrow, His wounds still fresh but His eyes overflowing with glory.

“Roger,” the Man said, His voice the voice of the Eucharist, of Calvary and the empty tomb. “My Gospel must be preached to all nations… even those not of this world.”

The bishop fell to his knees. “What world, Lord?”

“A world called Phanatos,” said the Lord. “They do not know Me, at least not in the depths that you do, but they hunger for truth. I will give you the tongue to speak all tongues, and bread that will never run out.”

The Man extended His hand. “Will you go?”

Bishop Barron, recognizing the man, wept in the dream. “Here I am, Lord. Send me.”

# Chapter One: The Awakening

He awoke not in his bed, but in a warm, unfamiliar room. The scent of wild herbs and woodsmoke lingered in the air. The walls were of hewn stone, yet bore none of the marks of any known architecture from Earth. The air was cool and clean, and the room was lit by a gentle glow—not from a lamp or fire, but from a blue crystal suspended in a wrought iron sconce.

Bishop Roger Barron sat up slowly. His episcopal ring remained on his finger. His pectoral cross still rested on his chest. Everything familiar… and nothing familiar.

Beside the bed sat a satchel—worn leather, beautifully tooled, and embossed with a Chi-Rho in gold. Opening it, he found treasures both spiritual and strange:

- A Mass kit, small but ornate. The chalice and paten gleamed with sacred polish, and the pyx refilled itself when he prayed in silence. The wine never soured. The hosts were always fresh.

- A complete Bible, bound in leather, yet heavier than it looked. When he opened it, the pages shone faintly and were footnoted not just with human theologians, but—he sensed—with wisdom meant for this world.

- A collection of prayer books, spiritual reading, and liturgical guides, written in a multitude of languages—none of which he had ever studied, yet he read them as clearly as the Roman Missal.

- And finally, a small vial case containing the Sacred Oils—Oil of the Catechumens, Oil of the Sick, and Holy Chrism—each warm to the touch, their scent unmistakable: holy, familiar, alive.

He was still kneeling beside the satchel when the door creaked open with gentle purpose.

An elderly gnome stood in the threshold. He had a round face lined by age and joy, a moss-colored beard tucked into his leather apron, and bright, amber-colored eyes that sparkled like a hearth on a cold night.

“You're awake,” the gnome said, his voice full of the patient wonder of one who had waited a long time. “That’s good.”

Roger blinked, steadying himself. “You speak English?”

The gnome chuckled. “No, sir. But you hear me in your tongue, and I, in mine.”

Roger instinctively made the sign of the cross. “The gift of tongues,” he murmured. Then, looking up, he rose to his feet. “You know who I am?”

“I know you’re a man of faith,” the gnome replied. “And that the stars told me you’d come. Or... at least, something did.”

Roger stepped closer, eyes narrowing. “Who are you?”

The gnome bowed slightly. “Name’s Tobin Hillfoot. Keeper of this inn, steward of six goats, father of four daughters, and humble resident of the Highroad province of Avon—though folk round here just call it the ‘upper coast.’”

“Avon,” Roger repeated slowly. “That’s where I am?”

“Aye. Avon, one of the southern realms of the continent called Hurly, on the coast of the River of Fate. You’re about a day’s walk from the city of Iverness, if the winds are kind.” Tobin scratched his beard thoughtfully. “You wouldn’t be from around here, I take it?”

Roger laughed despite himself. “No. I’m from Earth.”

Tobin’s expression did not change. “Never heard of it, but I suppose that explains the clothing and the air around you.”

Roger sat on the edge of the bed. “How did you know I would come?”

The gnome stepped inside and closed the door gently behind him. “I had a dream. Just last week.”

Roger leaned forward, listening.

“I saw a Man,” Tobin said, his voice quiet now. “Bright as starlight. Wounded hands, but smiling eyes. He stood in my orchard, and he said, ‘Tobin, ready the prophet’s bed. A shepherd from beyond the stars will come to water the dry roots. Let him sleep near the fire. And prepare him a breakfast that restores the soul.’” He looked sheepish. “So I made up the room. Told my wife we might be having strange company soon.”

Roger’s heart beat hard in his chest. “Did He... say His name?”

Tobin nodded. “Said He was called the Lamb and the Morning Star. I figured that meant He was someone important.”

Roger swallowed, tears pricking the edge of his vision. “It was Christ.”

Tobin smiled, not fully understanding, but not doubting either. “He smelled like sunlight and old wine. I’d trust a Man like that.”

The bishop reached for the satchel again, pulling free the Bible and clutching it to his chest. “Then I’m here by His command. This world has not known Him, but He has not forgotten it.”

Tobin inclined his head. “Then I’ll get you something to eat, Holy One. Prophets don’t save the world on empty stomachs.”

As the door closed behind the innkeeper, Roger Barron bowed his head in prayer.

“Lord Jesus Christ,” he whispered, “You have sent me to Hurly. To Avon. I know nothing here, but I know You, and that is enough. Be with me on this journey. Amen.”

# Chapter Two: The Meal and the Making

The scent of sizzling root vegetables and fire-smoked bread reached the bishop’s room long before he entered the kitchen. When Roger stepped through the low archway, he was greeted by a crackling hearth, a heavy iron pan spitting cheerfully, and the sound of a knife tapping rhythmically on a thick oak cutting board.

Tobin stood at the stove in a checkered tunic, humming to himself and expertly flipping something golden and steaming in the skillet.

“Sit, sit,” he said without looking back. “Breakfast is nearly done. Spiced yam hash, fried morningroot, and fresh flatbread. I’d have made eggs, but the duck that lays them is still dreaming she’s a goat.”

Roger smiled as he took the offered seat at a round wooden table, its center etched with swirling patterns—older than the gnome, perhaps older than Avon itself. He set his hands on the table and took in the kitchen. It was cozy, filled with little shelves and jars labeled in strange alphabets, but everything radiated care.

Tobin slid a hot plate in front of him with a proud nod. “Now eat. You’ll need strength. You look like you wrestled with a storm on your way down from the stars.”

Roger bowed his head and quietly whispered a blessing: “Benedic, Domine, nos et haec tua dona…”

Tobin tilted his head. “That sounded like Elven, but with a bit more thunder behind it.”

“It’s Latin,” Roger said. “A holy tongue, from where I’m from.”

“Well,” Tobin said, sitting down with his own plate, “a fine blessing it was. May it sit kindly on your stomach.”

They ate in silence for a time—Roger savoring the strange but hearty flavors, Tobin enjoying his own cooking with the dignity of a man who never rushes a meal. Eventually, the bishop set his fork down and asked, “You mentioned this morning that this land is called Hurly?”

“Aye. Hurly is the continent,” Tobin replied, chewing slowly. “Avon’s just one of the countries. Humble folk here, mostly. Gnomes, halflings, and quite a few humans near the eastern plains. A wandering goblin or dwarf will travel though as well. We’re not warlike, not like those goblins or the dwarves. Though everyone gets dragged in eventually, one way or another.”

Roger leaned in. “Can you tell me… how it all began? This world. Phanatos, you said?”

Tobin nodded and took a long sip from his tea. “That’s the old name. Phanatos, the living earth, the spinning gift. We’ve stories about how it came to be. You want the true version or the prettier one?”

“I want the truth.”

“Then I’ll tell it as it was told to me by my gran,” Tobin said, folding his hands and gazing into the hearth. “A bit rough at the edges, but it still burns clean.”

He cleared his throat. “In the beginning, there was Kalistos—the Creator of all. Not a god like some tall warrior with a sword and a crown. No, He was the Source. The flame that breathes, the light that thinks. Kalistos didn’t make things because He was lonely. He made them because creation is what love does when it has no reason to stop.”

Roger’s breath caught slightly. *The phrasing… it sounds like St. Bonaventure’s mysticism or the writings of the Eastern Fathers.*

Tobin continued, “So Kalistos sang, or maybe He dreamed, or maybe He just was—and from that came the Six Primal Dragons. Each one a force, not of chaos, but of truth. Pillars, we call them.”

He began to count on his fingers. “There was Ouro’ras, the Dragon of Light. He gave us sentience, humans and their kin—elves, dwarves, gnomes, all the thinking ones. Then came Golestandt, Dragon of Darkness, who gave form to night, mystery, and all that hides. Vlaurunga brought fire and invention—she’s the reason for steel, for forges and fury. Yvander shaped the sea and the cold, cradled the fish and the snow. Quintara Lotus, the Arcane One, gave magic—wild and strange. And last, Rokesh, the Earth-Dragon, father of beasts and builders alike.”

Roger listened closely, already forming parallels with the angelic choirs, or perhaps a distorted echo of creation narratives in Genesis—cosmic forces acting under the divine will.

“Kalistos didn’t set them loose like dogs,” Tobin said firmly. “He gave them a task—to shape a world not just of matter, but of meaning. And so they came together and formed Phanatos.”

Tobin gestured wide with his hands. “This place. Our world. They didn’t just pile up land and throw trees on it. They harmonized their elements. Light braided with shadow to form life. Ice danced with fire to birth storms. Earth and arcane embraced and gave us love. Their children—what we call the Greater Dragons—govern these things still. They don’t rule us, not exactly, but their essence is in the fabric of the world.”

Roger murmured, “Almost like virtues personified.”

“Maybe,” Tobin said. “Or maybe they’re songs in the body of the world. Either way, they made the place. And then, Kalistos… stepped back.”

Roger frowned slightly. “He abandoned you?”

Tobin shook his head. “No. Not abandoned. He’s still here. In everything. But He wanted His children—and their children—to choose freely. That’s what gran always said. ‘Kalistos makes the stage. But we choose the play.’”

The gnome sipped his tea again. “Course, not all went well. One of the twins, Erosia, she tried to take it all for herself. But that’s a longer story, the twins as a whole are, and it’s not my tale to tell.”

Roger sat back in his chair, heart pounding. It wasn’t Catholic theology, not in form. And yet... it was not pagan either. It was primordial, intuitive, even theologically resonant in ways he couldn’t explain.

“You believe this?” Roger asked.

Tobin looked surprised. “Course I do. Don’t you?”

Roger smiled slowly. “Yes. Or... I believe I’m here to discover why I was sent to a world where such stories are waiting for Christ.”

Tobin grinned. “Then it’s a good thing you landed in Avon, not somewhere full of fire and politics. You’ll have time to listen here. Learn.”

Roger looked down at the Bible beside his plate. “And preach?”

Tobin nodded. “Sure.”

Later that morning, after breakfast was cleared and the kitchen swept, Bishop Roger Barron stood in the entry hall of the inn, his hand resting gently on the strap of his satchel.

“Tobin,” he said carefully, “I’d like to ask a favor.”

The gnome turned from where he was hanging herbs to dry. “Course, Bishop. What do you need?”

Roger hesitated a moment, searching for words Tobin might understand. “There’s a prayer I need to say. A very important one. It’s... more than a prayer, really. It’s a sacred rite. I would need a quiet place, and a table—something simple, but clean.”

Tobin scratched his beard. “We’ve a back room for sorting deliveries. Bit dusty, but the table’s flat and there’s a window. Would that work?”

Roger smiled. “That would be perfect. And... if you’re willing, I’d be honored if you would stay and watch.”

Tobin looked at him sideways. “Watch?”

“It won’t take long. And you don’t need to do anything—just be present.”

The gnome nodded slowly. “Alright then. I may not understand what I’m watching, but I’m good at sitting quiet. This way.”

The sorting room was plain, lit by a single high window and filled with the earthy smell of burlap and dry wood. The table in the center was worn smooth from years of use—scuffed, practical, and just wide enough for a makeshift altar.

Roger set down his satchel and began to unpack: a small cloth, white as snow; a paten and chalice of polished gold; a folded stole embroidered with green thread. His movements were slow, precise, prayerful.

Tobin stood just inside the door, uncertain. “You said this was a prayer?”

Roger smiled gently. “Yes. But not just any prayer. This is what we call the Mass. It’s the heart of my faith. The moment where Christ becomes present—not in memory, but truly.”

Tobin blinked. “Christ... that’s your Lord? The one you said sent you?”

Roger nodded. “This is how He gives Himself to us. Through bread and wine.”

“Bread and wine?” Tobin asked, peering toward the chalice and the small pyx.

Roger didn’t explain further. “Just watch,” he said softly, “and listen.”

He kissed the altar cloth, placed the Scriptures beside him, and began: “In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit…”

Tobin heard none of those names before, but somehow they resonated—like a melody half-remembered from childhood. He folded his hands, respectful but unsure, and watched as Roger prayed.

The liturgy moved slowly, reverently. Roger prayed aloud in English, but Tobin heard it in his own tongue—each word meeting his mind with gentle clarity. Roger offered readings from memory, and when he spoke of Christ’s last supper, his voice deepened with something Tobin couldn’t name.

Then came the moment of institution.

Roger lifted a small host in both hands and spoke, voice firm but full of wonder: “This is My Body, which will be given up for you.”

Tobin blinked. The room shivered. Not from wind—but something deeper. A hush. A stillness.

Roger lifted the chalice next. “This is the chalice of My Blood, the Blood of the new and eternal covenant…”

And then the light came.

It was not a flame, nor a lightning flash, nor magic. It was light—clear and warm, pouring from the altar like sunlight through diamond. It filled the corners of the room, passed through the window, and spilled into the hallway. Every nail and knot in the wood gleamed. Even the table seemed to breathe.

Tobin stepped back, shielding his eyes. The hairs on his arms stood on end. The air itself thrummed like a silent chord.

And then, as swiftly as it came, the light faded.

Roger stood in silence, holding the consecrated host, eyes closed. For a long moment, neither man moved.

Then, Tobin spoke—quietly, reverently. “Bishop… what just happened?”

Roger opened his eyes, his face radiant with peace. “That was Him.”

“Him,” Tobin echoed. “Christ.”

“Yes.” Roger gently lowered the host to the paten. “The Son of God. Not a symbol. Not a memory. The living God, truly present. Body, Blood, Soul, and Divinity.”

Tobin swallowed hard. “You brought Him here?”

Roger shook his head. “He came on His own. I only opened the door.”

The gnome stared at the table, then at Roger, eyes wide. “This world’s seen plenty of magic... but I’ve never seen that.”

Roger smiled faintly. “I will admit that it was a bit, uh, flashier than normal..”

And in the silence that followed, as Roger knelt to pray and Tobin simply stood in awe, it became clear that something ancient had stirred in Avon. Not magic. Not legend. Grace.

And Phanatos would never be the same.

The silence that followed the miracle was deeper than before—not the quiet of still air, but of reverence. The sorting room felt... changed. Not brighter, but clearer. As though the dust had fled and even the walls were listening.

Bishop Roger Barron lowered the host with care, consumed it in silence, and drank from the chalice with measured reverence. He folded the corporal gently, placed the sacred vessels back into the satchel, and then knelt for the prayer of thanksgiving. Even as he did, the corners of his eyes shimmered.

It had never happened like that before. Not in Rome, not in Dulcia, not even on Easter Sunday with the choir rising behind him. Never like this.

He whispered: “Not to us, Lord, but to Your name give the glory.”

Behind him, Tobin remained near the wall, stock still. He hadn’t moved in minutes. Only when Roger stood and turned did the gnome speak—quietly, in a voice more prayer than question.

“That… was Him. Wasn’t it?”

Roger nodded. “Yes. Christ. God, made flesh. Here, now.”

Tobin’s mouth opened, then closed again. “And you do this often?”

“Every day.”

“Every day!?” His voice squeaked.

Roger smiled. “Back home, yes. And I intend to do so here.”

Tobin stepped closer, eyes wide and brimming with questions. “But how? What is He, exactly? Is He a dragon? A spirit? A sorcerer? What kind of god gives Himself like food?”

Roger laughed softly, though his eyes stayed serious. “He’s not a dragon. He’s the Son of God. The Second Person of the Trinity. He became man to save us.”

Tobin blinked. “Save us from what?”

“From sin. From death.”

“Wait—what is sin? Is it like corruption magic? Is this tied to the Greater Dragons somehow—maybe Agape or Jinshi? Wait, are you one of their priests?”

Roger took a breath to answer, but was interrupted by the sound of voices. Dozens. Muffled at first, then louder, rising just beyond the pantry door.

“…You saw it too, didn’t you?” “It came from here!” “Some sort of lightspell?” “I told you something sacred happened!” “Open the door, Tobin! What are you hiding in there?”

Tobin’s eyes widened. “That’s half the village!”

Roger’s face shifted from surprise to purpose in a heartbeat. He stepped past Tobin and unlatched the door. “Then let’s not hide,” he said. “Let’s proclaim.”

The inn’s common room was full—shoulder-to-shoulder with villagers. Gnomes and humans, a few goblins, even a tall Lanyaru woman in traveling leathers. Their eyes were wide, whispering feverishly as the door opened and Roger stepped through, stole still hanging over his neck.

The room fell silent. All eyes turned to the bishop. Tobin followed behind, still pale, and cleared his throat. “Uh, this is Roger. He’s… um. A bishop. A holy man. He, he’s not from around here.”

Roger raised a hand, calm and open. “Peace be with you.”

They didn’t understand the words exactly, but they felt them.

He stepped onto the low stone ledge near the hearth, where a musician might normally sit, and looked out at the crowd—young, old, weathered, wary. “I am Bishop Roger Barron,” he said, voice steady and full. “I come from a land beyond your stars. But I was sent here not by magic or ambition, but by Christ. A God of love. A God who knows you. Who made this world. And who has not forgotten it.”

There was a murmur.

“You saw the light,” he continued, gesturing gently toward the back of the inn. “That was not my doing. It was His. Not a spell. Not a trick. It was a sign, a message.”

He raised the Bible from his satchel and held it aloft. “You have your creation stories. And I believe they hold the truth. But today, I tell you a greater truth still: the God who made the Primal Dragons, the One before all, has entered into His own creation. He took flesh. He walked. He healed. He wept. And He died, for all of us.”

Some gasped. One woman scoffed. “Gods don’t die.”

Roger looked directly at her. “Only One ever did—and in dying, He conquered death. Not with power, but with love. And I’m here to share that love. To teach. To serve. To suffer if needed. And to offer, every day, the mystery you witnessed this morning. Not magic. Not fire. But something deeper.”

He stepped down slowly, voice softening. “You don’t have to believe yet. Just come. See. Ask.”

A silence followed—deep, uncertain, and full of wonder.

Then an old man near the door spoke. “Would we be allowed to watch, then? This… what do you call it?”

Roger looked at him kindly. “The Mass. And yes. All are welcome.”

Tobin exhaled and murmured, “Well… guess I’d better clear the sorting room again.”

# Chapter Three: The Shepherd in a Foreign Land

Phanatos had never seen a cleric like Roger Barron. When he first arrived in the little gnomish village along the highroad of Avon, most had mistaken him for a wandering sage or a traveling healer. He wore strange robes. He spoke with authority but little pride. He knew nothing of the local plants or dragons or politics, but somehow, when he looked at someone, they felt seen.

After the light in the inn, the questions came like rain. At first, they trickled, curious farmers and herbalists peering through the doorway as he prayed the Mass in Tobin’s back room. A goat-herder named Merril asked to stay after and speak. A traveling trader from Iverness came two days later and asked him to bless her child. She didn’t know what “blessing” meant, only that when he touched the baby’s forehead and said the words, the child stopped coughing for the first time in weeks.

Word began to spread. The room behind the pantry could no longer hold them, so Tobin cleared the stableyard and built a wooden platform near the well. Roger said Mass there each morning as mist rolled in from the north, vesting beneath a makeshift canopy while Tobin arranged benches for those who wished to sit.

They came from nearby valleys, then from Iverness itself. Then from as far south as the River Delny and the Elven hamlets beyond the forest’s edge. He did not perform signs and wonders to attract them. He simply preached, and what he preached was strange, unsettling, beautiful.

He spoke of a God who created not out of power, but out of love. Of a world fallen not by war or corruption magic, but by pride. Of a Savior, born among peasants, crucified as a criminal, risen from the dead, who offered forgiveness freely, and of a Spirit who breathed new life into hearts once stone.

When people asked what this God wanted from them, Roger’s answer was always the same: “To be made new, and to love as He loves.”

He baptized the first souls of Phanatos in the village river—three gnomes, two humans, and a traveling dwarven metalwright who wept uncontrollably as Roger poured the water over his brow and spoke the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit.

In time, he baptized thousands. He translated the Mass into their languages, though he still kept the Latin prayers sacred in his heart. He wrote a catechism using stories familiar to them, blending the wisdom of the dragons with the Gospel, showing how Kalistos was not a distant myth, but the One who had fulfilled His promise in Christ, and slowly, a new community took shape.

It wasn’t a parish, not yet, but it was the Church. They met in old stone barns and cleared groves, gathering to hear the Scriptures, to confess their sins, to sing strange but heartfelt hymns. Some wore robes, some wore toolbelts. One little girl brought her pet spark-lizard to every Mass and swore it stopped hissing whenever Roger lifted the chalice, and then came the callings.

Ten years after his first Mass in Tobin’s sorting room, Roger stood beneath the boughs of a hilltop shrine and laid hands on a young man named Gaius—a farmer’s son, once full of mockery, now full of mercy. The bishop anointed him with chrism and ordained him as the first native priest of Hurly.

He would not be the last. Over the next few years, more followed: a Lanyaru healer, a gnome scribe, a widowed mother of three who asked to become a sister to tend the sick and bury the dead. Roger discerned each with care, formed them slowly, taught them about the Mass and the Creed and the weight of the cross.

And from them came the beginnings of a true presbyterate—a Church with roots, not just memory. Tobin lived to see it all. He remained at Roger’s side those first years as an innkeeper, then as a sacristan, then simply as a friend. He never asked for baptism himself, not right away. But one day, late in his life, as the village prepared for the feast of Pentecost, he came to Roger quietly and said, “I’ve seen enough to know. I want to belong to Him.” Roger wept as he poured the water.

In the lands of Hurly, where faith had always meant fear or tribute to dragons, something new had entered. Not a rebellion. Not a faction. But a kingdom, not of this world. And its first bishop sat beneath a tree one afternoon, barefoot, sipping tea brewed by a gnome, and writing homilies on scraps of parchment as children played by the well. And heaven watched it unfold.

# Chapter Four: The King’s Call

It began with a procession of six riders bearing the seal of Iverness—a white stag crowned with stars.

They arrived at midday, flanked by dust and formality, their cloaks pressed and their voices clipped. The leader, a knight-captain with pale eyes and weathered bronze armor, dismounted in front of the village well and asked, not unkindly, “Which of you is the priest who commands the light?”

Tobin, ever helpful, pointed to Bishop Barron as he emerged from the stable chapel, sleeves rolled, hands still dusted with ash from blessing a hearthstone.

“I am Bishop Roger Barron,” he said simply.

The knight bowed with formal grace. “His Majesty, King Halbrecht of Iverness, High Sovereign of the Southern Crest, requests your presence in his court. He asks that you preach your message to him directly.”

Roger felt the weight of those words. The king was no minor noble. Iverness was the heart of Avon’s southlands—wealthy, influential, and bound by ancient treaties to the priesthood of Ouro’ras, the Dragon of Light.

He bowed slightly. “And if I refuse?”

The knight’s mouth twitched with something like admiration. “Then His Majesty will simply wonder what kind of gospel fears a throne.”

Roger wrestled with the decision for days.

He prayed through the night, consulted Tobin, contemplated the Gospels, and even fasted for three days in solitude. Christ had told him to come to this world; not to overthrow kings or challenge temples, but to plant seeds. Was it prudent to walk willingly into a palace bound to another god? And yet… how could he not go, if the king himself had asked to hear the Word?

He packed his satchel, placed his Bible within, and mounted a borrowed horse. “I have to go,” he said. “Even if it ends in silence.”

The court of Iverness was made of silver stone and carved blueglass. It shone like a sunrise frozen in architecture. The throne room was tall and airy, filled with golden banners and fragrant with the smoke of sandalwood offerings. At its head sat King Halbrecht, a broad-shouldered man in a wool mantle, with a keen gaze and laugh lines at the corners of his mouth.

He stood when Barron entered. “You’re not what I expected,” the king said. “No staff. No gems. No choir behind you.”

Roger bowed. “I bring only the Gospel.”

“And a great deal of rumors,” the king added with a smirk. “They say the innkeeper's fire glowed like the sun for three days. That a river changed course when you washed your hands in it.”

“Exaggeration,” Roger said. “The fire glowed for less than a minute. The river curved on its own.”

The king laughed. “I like you already. Please, preach. Not to my court. To me.”

Roger spoke for only ten minutes. He told of a God who stepped into flesh. Of a kingdom that was not defended by blades, but by mercy. Of a love that forgave even enemies. He quoted no spells. He invoked no dragons. But when he spoke of Christ crucified, the hall fell quiet.

The king said nothing when he finished. He only nodded, thoughtful, and then, after a moment, he said, “I believe you are honest. But I also believe my priest is holy.” He waved a hand, and a figure stepped forward from behind a veiled screen: tall, robed in white and gold, eyes dark as iron. He bowed once and stood at the king’s side.

“This is High Priest Selenon of Ouro’ras,” the king said. “He is learned in all things divine. I trust him with the soul of my city. And he has asked—fairly, I think—that your message be tested, not by fire, not by blade, but by truth.”

Roger said nothing.

“A debate,” the king said. “In the amphitheater of the Temple of Light, three days from now. You will speak. He will respond. The people will listen, and so shall I.”

“And the outcome?” Roger asked quietly.

“If you win, I will ask to be baptized. As will many in my court, I imagine.”

“And if I lose?”

“Then you will leave Iverness. Not under chains. But you will not return.”

There was no malice in the king’s tone. Only gravity. He was not a tyrant—but he was a ruler. And he meant every word.

Roger nodded slowly. “Then may the Spirit speak through me. I accept.”

# Chapter Five: Three Days of Silence

Roger Barron was escorted from the throne room not to a prison, nor a chapel, but to a modest stone chamber within the city’s guest cloister—neither luxurious nor austere. There was a cot, a basin, a writing desk, and a single window overlooking the southern garden. The guards at the door were polite. Respectful. But always present. He was a guest. And a contestant. He had three days to prepare.

## Day One: The Weight

He spent the first day in silence. He laid out his Bible and his breviary on the desk. He prayed the Liturgy of the Hours as if they were anchors holding him fast in an unfamiliar tide. But his heart was not still. Not yet.

“This is not why I came,” he whispered aloud in the late afternoon light. “I came to plant. Not to fight.”

But even Christ had faced His challengers. Pharisees, scribes, Roman governors. He had debated not to win, but to reveal. To uncover the heart.

Roger remembered Christ's words: “Do not worry about how you are to speak... for it will not be you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.”

He knelt on the stone floor that night until the sun rose.

## Day Two: The Scrollmaker

The next morning, a visitor came. A young Elven woman in scholar’s robes, wearing a satchel bulging with scrolls and writing instruments. She introduced herself with a bow.

“I am Dessa, court scribe and translator. I was tasked with preparing transcripts of the debate. I asked the king for permission to speak with you.”

Roger rose. “Then I’m glad you came.”

She stepped forward, curious. “You’re not trained in our theology. Not in the rites of Ouro’ras. Would you allow me to explain what the High Priest will likely argue?”

Roger considered her. “You’re a court scribe. And yet you offer help?”

“I’m also a historian,” she said. “And I love the truth.”

She stayed for two hours; she explained that the followers of Ouro’ras taught that the world was created in tiers of light, each step a refining of the divine spark. That mortals were to rise upward through meditation, purity, and alignment with natural order. Sin was not rebellion, but dissonance, imbalance. Salvation was harmony.

Roger listened. He took notes. But more importantly, he asked questions.

“Is there mercy in this system?”

“Only if one achieves it.”

“And the poor?”

“They are often seen as broken mirrors—unfortunate, but spiritually inefficient.”

Roger closed his eyes. “Then it is a system without a shepherd.”

Dessa hesitated. “There are many here who feel the same.”

## Day Three: The Voice Within

The final day, Roger did not read. He did not prepare a speech.

Instead, he prayed in his improvised chapel at dawn, knelt before the portable crucifix he had carved during his early years in Avon, and simply listened. He thought of the people he had baptized, of Tobin, of the little girl with the spark-lizard, of King Halbrecht’s weary eyes, looking not for power, but for peace, and he knew the debate would not be about winning. It would be about witness.

That evening, as the bells rang out over Iverness, Roger walked the place halls where he was staying one last time. The guards watched him with quiet respect. The gardeners paused their work. One servant girl crossed herself, though she did not know what the gesture meant.

And in the gathering dusk, Dessa found him again, “Are you ready?”

Roger smiled. “I will speak the truth. That is enough.”

The debate would begin with sunrise, and already, the city was holding its breath.

# Chapter Six: The Debate at the Temple of Light

The Temple of Light was the most sacred site in Iverness. Carved from alabaster and infused with sunstone veins, its domes reflected the dawn like crowns of flame. Within its amphitheater, built to magnify even a whisper, the gathered citizens sat shoulder-to-shoulder—scribes, nobles, soldiers, beggars, and merchants alike. This was not a trial. It was a spectacle.

At the highest seat of the half-circle stood King Halbrecht, not in a crown, but a plain mantle of linen. Beside him sat Dessa, scrolls ready. Two pulpits had been raised below: one faced the rising sun, gilded and adorned with the sigil of Ouro’ras; the other, simple wood, faced the people.

High Priest Selenon stepped forward first. He was tall, pale, and robed in pure white stitched with constellations. His voice was smooth as polished glass.

The High Priest Speaks: “Brothers and sisters,” he began, “we do not gather to condemn, but to compare. The stranger before you claims he has come with revelation. We must test it, as light tests every shadow.” He bowed toward Barron: formal, precise.

“Our faith holds that Kalistos is the source of all. This, we share, but where we differ is this: we teach that Kalistos poured His perfection into six Primal Dragons. Among them, Ouro’ras—Light Unbounded—shines brightest. It was through Ouro’ras that order was shaped, harmony achieved. He is not a false god, but the purest reflection of Kalistos’ will in this world.”

A murmur of assent rippled through the crowd.

“Sin,” Selenon continued, “is imbalance. Misalignment. To be saved is to rise—through clarity, through discipline, through purity. Our temples purify. Our light cleanses. We do not condemn the broken, but we do not coddle them.”

He turned to Roger. “The stranger says his god died. That he offers grace to all, undeserved. That even the lowest may inherit eternity.” He paused, his voice rising. “But what kind of order is that? Where is justice? Where is refinement? Should the murderer feast beside the sage? Should the thief sing with the wise?”

He bowed once more and returned to his seat. Applause followed—polite but firm. Then came the silence.

Bishop Roger Barron stood slowly. His robes were plain. His stole green and gold, well-worn. His Bible, clasped in both hands. He stepped to the pulpit that faced the people.

He spoke softly at first. “Thank you, High Priest. You have spoken with clarity and conviction. And I honor your love for Kalistos.”

That turned a few heads. “I do not deny Kalistos,” Roger continued. “In truth, I preach Him. But I preach what Kalistos did, not only in the forging of the world through the Primal Dragons, but in the fulfillment of creation through His Son.”

He lifted the Bible. “In my world, and now in yours, Kalistos entered His creation. Not through a dragon or flame—but through a child, born of a virgin, in a village no larger than one of yours. He grew. He walked. He healed. And yes, He died.”

A hush settled over the crowd.

Roger's voice strengthened. “But death was not defeat. It was victory. Because He rose. Not as a symbol. Not as a ghost. But as God-in-flesh, the Lamb and the Light. He did not come to call the perfect—but the sinner. He did not come to reward the proud—but to lift the broken.”

He stepped away from the pulpit and walked forward, eyes meeting those seated in the crowd. “You ask, ‘Should the thief dine with the sage?’ I say: yes. Because the sage, too, is hungry. Because grace is not merit. Grace is gift. None of us are worthy. That is why we call Him Savior.”

A silence deeper than before.

He returned to the pulpit. “Ouro’ras is a reflection. But Christ is the source. Light reflected must bow before Light Incarnate. The dragons echo Kalistos. But Christ is Kalistos made visible.”

He closed his Bible gently. “You asked where justice lies? It lies in a God who dies for His enemies. You asked where refinement is? It is in the soul who says ‘yes’ to love, not because it earned it—but because it was given.”

Then, his voice dropped to a whisper:

“We are not saved by rising up, we are saved because He came down.”

He stepped back. No applause. No murmur. Only stillness. Bishop Barron returned to his seat, and for a long moment, the amphitheater held its breath.

Then Selenon rose once more. His steps were measured, his tone even. But his eyes held steel, “You speak of grace,” the high priest said, “but you offer chaos.” He turned to the crowd now. “This stranger offers love to the wicked, inheritance to the unworthy, and dares to name it justice. But what is justice without balance? If sin is rebellion, and all may be forgiven without cost, then law means nothing. Purity means nothing. All is equal. All is mud.”

A few in the crowd murmured approval. Selenon pressed on. “He says Ouro’ras reflects Kalistos, and I do not deny that the Primal Dragons are not gods in themselves. But we do not merely revere Ouro’ras—we walk his path because he brings clarity. He teaches us to ascend, not to be dragged downward by the fallen.”

He pointed to Roger now, “You say your god died for the guilty. Then what becomes of the innocent? What justice is there in the slaughter of the pure to redeem the impure?” And then, slowly, he turned back to the king. “Your Majesty, would you give your life for a thief who would steal from your widow? Would you spill your blood for the man who burns your home?”

Roger stood again. This time, he did not approach the pulpit. He stepped out into the open space between the two podiums, facing Selenon directly. “I would not,” Roger admitted. “I would not give my life for a thief. I am too proud. Too selfish. Too afraid.” He turned to the king, then to the people. “But Christ did. That is what makes Him worthy of worship.”

He held up his Bible, hand trembling with emotion now, not fury, but deep, aching hope. “The Cross is not logical. It is not efficient. It is love beyond measure. A love so relentless that it would rather suffer injustice than inflict it.”

“You ask what justice is?” Roger’s voice lifted. “True justice is not balance—it is mercy wrapped in truth. And the innocent man who died was no victim. He was the Author of life, offering His blood to rewrite our story.”

Now he walked toward the center of the amphitheater, his voice resonating: “You call my God’s love chaos. But it is not chaos. It is a fire, refining not by destruction, but by transformation. My God does not demand we climb to Him. He comes to us. He does not despise the sinner. He dines with him. He does not destroy the weak. He lifts them.”

He turned to Selenon one last time, “You call my message dangerous because it lowers the gates of heaven to the poor, the outcast, the unclean. But that is the scandal of grace. And it is the only hope this broken world has ever had.”

He bowed, not to the crowd, not to the king, but to the truth itself. “This is not a religion of worth. It is a relationship of love, and love, real love, never balances the scales. It empties itself for the beloved.”

The silence returned—but this time, it was different. Not tense. Not uncertain. Convicted.

Even those loyal to Selenon sat still, their faces troubled—not because Roger had humiliated their priest, but because he had pierced their hearts. Selenon stood motionless. He did not rebut. He did not bow.He only said, “May Kalistos judge between us.”And returned to his seat.

The king rose, slow and solemn. He looked to Selenon, the priest who had raised him. Then he looked to Barron, the man who had preached Christ crucified. “I have seen the light of Ouro’ras all my life,” he said. “But today, I have seen the Light that shaped him.”

He stepped down from the dais and removed the signet ring from his finger. He knelt at Roger’s feet. “Bishop Barron. Will you baptize me?” A gasp. A shout. Then, stillness. Roger knelt with him and replied, “Yes.”

# Chapter Seven: The Waters of the Kingdom

The baptism of King Halbrecht did not echo like a trumpet blast; It moved like a tide. When Roger Barron lifted the king from the sacred spring beneath the Temple of Light, the crowds stood not in applause, but in reverent silence. For a moment, even the air felt hushed—as if the world itself paused to witness what no oracle or high priest had foreseen: that a monarch would kneel not before dragon or throne, but before the crucified God of love.

And from that quiet moment, conversion swept Avon like sunrise chasing away fog.

The next day, hundreds gathered at the river gates to beg for baptism. By week’s end, the numbers had grown to thousands. Entire noble houses sought instruction. Gnomish guilds debated late into the night, then came forward in bands to be received into the Church. Dwarven veterans wept openly as they knelt in public, confessing sins they had carried for decades.

Those who had once viewed Roger as a foreign curiosity now referred to him as Bishop Barron of Avon, and his faith as the Universal Faith—the Catholic one, a name whispered in dozens of languages, yet somehow understood the same.

King Halbrecht declared that his conversion was not a private conviction, but a public commission. He ordered the construction of churches throughout the urban heartlands of Avon twelve in Iverness alone. Though the Temple of Light remained open, many of its attendants now crossed the city to kneel at stone altars bearing the crucifix, not the sigil of Ouro’ras. Barron did not demand the old temples be shuttered. He only preached, and the people came.

The churches rose swiftly, blending native Avonic stonework with the Romanesque style of Earth: arched vaults, bell towers, and sanctuaries carved with images of Christ, Mary, the apostles, and the dragons beneath Kalistos’ gaze.

The great Cathedral of the Incarnate Word was completed in just under five years. It stood not on a hill of triumph, but in the heart of the market quarter, so that the poor, the widowed, the wounded, and the working might walk through its doors without fear.

The baptisms continued in waves. From city artisans to traveling merchants, from scholars to former temple acolytes, each soul received instruction, confession, and rebirth. Even clergy of Ouro’ras came in secret to seek understanding, and they were not alone.

Those who felt called to the priesthood came as well, first by the dozens, then by the hundreds. Barron interviewed each himself. He spent months walking with them, praying with them, discerning carefully before ordaining anyone. Many he ordained as deacons or priests. Some as monastics, sent to the wilderness to found small houses of prayer. And then, after years of watching the fire spread, Roger knew he could no longer shepherd it alone.

After weeks of fasting, prayer, and counsel with his fellow clergy, Roger made a decision as heavy as it was joyful. He would ordain three new bishops—not as replacements, but as co-shepherds. One for each of the three peoples who had most responded to the Gospel in Avon.

The first was Brother Savael, an elf of the eastern woodlands, once a contemplative hermit devoted to Ouro’ras. He had memorized nearly the entire Gospel of John before he ever held a page in his hands. Roger appointed him Bishop of the Coastal Wilds.

Next was Father Corbin Timberbind, a gnome theologian with a mind like a clockwork vault and a heart like a hearth. He translated the Catechism into five Avonic dialects before his own ordination. Roger named him Bishop of the Hill Circles and Northern Roads.

Finally Father Brokk Earthmantle, a dwarf veteran turned evangelist, who lost his family in a mine collapse and found hope in the homilies of the Gospel. He was courageous, disciplined, and unfailingly loyal to the truth. Roger consecrated him as Bishop of the Deep Ridges and Border Holds.

And though not ordained, one other soul became a pillar of the growing Church: Delgrid Stonevoice, a dwarf widow and former artisan, who took private vows of chastity and service. She founded the Order of the Hearth of Mary, gathering women who wished to serve the sick, teach the faith, and live in prayer. Roger named her Abbess of the First Order of Sisters in Hurly, a spiritual mother to many and a counselor to all.

He consecrated the three bishops beneath the high altar of the Cathedral of the Incarnate Word, before a congregation of nearly thirty thousand. As the chrism touched their foreheads and the miters were placed upon them, a storm broke above the city—rain falling as if the heavens wept with joy.

In ten years, the Church in Avon had transformed from an inn’s storage room to a full ecclesial province, humming with life.

Parishes rose in towns once indifferent to faith. Monasteries appeared on mountaintops. Seminaries opened in old guildhalls. The calendar filled with feast days and fasts. The sacraments were preached, and practiced, and passed on.

And in the center of it all stood Bishop Roger Barron, not as a ruler, but as a servant.

# Chapter Eight: The Vine and the Crown

King Halbrecht of Avon, the first monarch to bend the knee to Christ in the world of Phanatos, died as he had lived after his baptism—quietly, devoutly, and without regret. He did not seek glory. He did not claim sainthood. But in his final days, surrounded by priests, monks, and grandchildren, he made one final proclamation that would change history.

From the altar in his private chapel, with Bishop Roger Barron at his side, he dictated a decree: “Let the crown of Avon pass not to my blood, but to the blood of Christ. Let this land be consecrated not to dynasties, but to the Church. Let it be governed not by power, but by truth.”

He handed over his royal signet to Barron and declared: “From this day forth, let Avon belong to the Gospel. Let it be the Throne of the Church in Hurly, and let it be led not by kings, but by the shepherd who first brought us light.”

Then he closed his eyes, received the Anointing of the Sick and Viatecum, and passed into eternal rest, the sign of the cross traced gently upon his brow.

In the weeks that followed, the Church gathered in Iverness to mourn, to celebrate, and to discern. Bishops and abbots, priests and theologians came from across the Avonic lands and beyond. The question was not one of power, it was one of calling, and the answer, for them, was already clear.

Bishop Barron had not sought authority. He had sought to serve. But now, the mantle was laid at his feet. On the vigil of Pentecost, in the Cathedral of the Incarnate Word, the three bishops he had consecrated laid hands on him and spoke with one voice:

“We name you Papal Vicar, Vicar of the Vicar of Christ, shepherd of this land, protector of the Gospel in Hurly. May you remain in union with the whole Church, and especially with our savior, Jesus Christ.”

And Bishop Roger Barron, now in his sixty-eigth year, bowed his head in obedience—not to ambition, but to grace and he took a new name: Papal Vicar Surculus, Latin for “the shoot” or “the branch,” to denote that he was not the leader of the Church, only the head for the Church here on Hurly. If Peter was the Rock, then he would be a branch.

The new vicar’s first act was to complete what he had long prayed over in silence. He ordained eight new bishops, drawing from every race and region where the faith had taken root. With these, the number of bishops in Hurly came to twelve, echoing the apostles of old.

Each bishop was given a region, a mission, and a fragment of the Church’s unity. Each was entrusted not with dominion, but with discipleship. The bishops set out to teach, to serve, and to suffer, bearing not swords, but sacraments.

And from the city of Iverness, now monikered the home of the Ecclesia Nova, the Church flourished as never before.

Pope Surculus never returned to Earth. He never sought to. He had been sent as an apostle, and he died as a father. By the time of his death, the Catholic Church had spread to nearly every corner of Avon and had begun taking root evenbeyond the boarders of the new Papal States, among goblins, gnomes, dwarves, elves, and peoples still hidden beyond the wildlands.

He left behind a Church not only of cathedrals, but of hearts renewed. Not of domination, but of invitation, and the people of Phanatos, those once ruled by stars, dragons, and ancestral fear, now are beginning to walk in the light of the Lamb. A branch had been planted, and it would grow.
